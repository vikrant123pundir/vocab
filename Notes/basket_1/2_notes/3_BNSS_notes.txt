BNSS 1 > Short title, extent and commencement.:
About
Section 1 names the procedural code, defines its territorial reach, and sets its commencement.

Sub‑section (1) gives the title: the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.

Sub‑section (2) declares that the entire Sanhita, except Chapters IX (maintenance), XI (preventive police action) and XII (investigation), does not apply to Nagaland and the specified tribal areas. Those three chapters apply directly even there. The State Government may, by notification, extend the remaining provisions with “supplemental, incidental or consequential modifications” — a form of delegated legislative adaptation.

Explanation: Defines “tribal areas” as the pre‑21 January 1972 tribal areas of Assam under the Sixth Schedule, excluding the Shillong municipality.

Sub‑section (3) vests commencement with the Central Government by a single notification — unlike the BNS, piecemeal enforcement is not contemplated.

Counterfactual
Without this section, the BNSS would apply uniformly throughout India, overriding constitutional protections for Naga customary law and Sixth‑Schedule autonomous councils. The absence of the automatic partial application (Chapters IX, XI, XII) would leave no summary maintenance remedy and create a policing vacuum. Without the State’s modification power, there would be no way to tailor procedure to local conditions; the code would either apply in full or not at all, disrespecting the federal scheme.

Nuances

Asymmetric default: The three “core” chapters apply proprio vigore even in excluded areas — a non‑negotiable floor for maintenance, public order, and investigation.

Modification power: The State can not only extend but also modify the code supplementally, incidentally or consequentially. This blends central procedure with local custom without amending the Sanhita itself.

Shillong municipality exclusion: The Explanation surgically removes the urban municipality from “tribal areas,” ensuring the ordinary procedure governs the city while the Khasi Hills retain their special regime.

Single commencement date: Using “such date” (singular) signals that the procedural code must come into force as an integrated whole — arrests, bail, trial, and appeals cannot function in isolated pieces.

Interconnections

Constitution, Article 371A: Special protection for Naga customary law and procedure — the constitutional root of the Nagaland exclusion.

Sixth Schedule, paragraph 20: Defines the tribal areas directly referenced by the Explanation.

CrPC, 1973, Section 1: The identical predecessor. All judicial interpretations of “tribal areas” and the notification power remain persuasive.

BNS, Section 1: The substantive penal code applies uniformly throughout India, while the procedural code allows local flexibility. The asymmetry is deliberate: uniform crimes, locally adapted procedure.

CPC, Section 1(3): Same exclusion for civil procedure in these areas, but the CPC has no automatic partial application — further reinforcing that the BNSS provides an essential procedural floor.

Practical

In Nagaland and the tribal areas, a police officer registers an FIR and investigates (Chapter XII) exactly as in the rest of India. Maintenance claims under Chapter IX are directly available to wives, children and parents without waiting for any notification.

Trials and other procedures (bail, framing of charges, appeals) will follow local customary law unless the State Government extends the relevant BNSS provisions, possibly with modifications that accommodate village councils or traditional dispute‑resolution bodies.

The Shillong municipality, carved out from the tribal area definition, follows the full BNSS as the default procedural code — ensuring urban criminal justice is not fragmented.

Cases
All decided under the identical CrPC Section 1; highly persuasive.

State of Nagaland v. Ratan Singh (1966) – 90% (held that exclusion is absolute unless the State extends the Code; courts cannot assume application)

Bareh, In re (1966) – 85% (Constitution Bench clarified the definition of “tribal areas” under the Sixth Schedule, directly illuminating the Explanation)

Pema Chibar v. Union of India (1962) – 65% (discussed special procedural exclusions applicable to Nagaland, though before Article 371A)

Keshavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973, minority view) – 60% (observations on the Sixth Schedule reinforce the importance of procedural autonomy, though not a direct holding on CrPC Section 1)

BNSS 2 > Definitions.:
About
Section 2 is the procedural glossary of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, supplying the precise meaning of 26 terms that structure the entire criminal process.

Core procedural concepts: "Bail" (release from custody on conditions with bond), "bailable offence" (shown in First Schedule or made bailable by law), "non‑bailable offence" (everything else), "cognizable offence" (police can arrest without warrant), "non‑cognizable offence" (no arrest without warrant), "charge" (includes any head of charge when multiple), "inquiry" (every judicial inquiry other than a trial), "investigation" (collection of evidence by police or a person authorised by a Magistrate; the Explanation makes special Act provisions prevail over inconsistent BNSS provisions), "judicial proceeding" (any proceeding where evidence is or may be legally taken on oath), "summons‑case" (offence not being a warrant‑case), "warrant‑case" (offence punishable with death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment exceeding two years).

Institutional and territorial terms: "High Court" (defined by State, extended jurisdiction, or highest criminal appellate court for a Union territory), "local jurisdiction" (the area within which a Court/Magistrate exercises power, which can be the whole or part of a State as notified), "officer in charge of a police station" (the senior‑most officer present above constable rank when the regular officer is absent, or any other officer if the State so directs), "place" (includes house, building, tent, vehicle and vessel), "police report" (report under Section 193(3) — the final investigation report), "police station" (any post or place declared by the State Government), "Public Prosecutor" (appointed under Section 18 and includes those acting under their direction), "sub‑division" (a sub‑division of a district).

Processes and communications: "Audio‑video electronic" means use of communication devices for video conferencing, recording of identification/search/seizure/evidence, transmission of electronic communication, and other purposes the State Government may prescribe by rules. "Complaint" (allegation made orally or in writing to a Magistrate for action, excluding a police report — but a police officer's report disclosing only a non‑cognizable offence after investigation is deemed a complaint). "Electronic communication" (any written, verbal, pictorial or video content transmitted by an electronic device — telephone, mobile, computer, camera, etc. — or any device the Central Government may notify). "Notification" (published in the Official Gazette).

Person‑focused terms: "Victim" is a person who has suffered loss or injury from the accused's act/omission, and includes the guardian or legal heir.

Clause (2): A catch‑all bridge — undefined words take their meaning from the Information Technology Act, 2000 and the BNS, 2023.

Counterfactual
Without Section 2, every procedural classification would be a battleground. "Cognizable" versus "non‑cognizable" would be undefined, crippling arrest powers. The definition of "complaint" separates the Magistrate's gatekeeping power from police reports; without it, informants could forum‑shop and the boundary between police and magisterial action would collapse. The "victim" definition, including guardians and heirs, would be missing — victims' families would have no statutory standing for appeals, compensation, or participation. Without "audio‑video electronic means," the new procedural architecture for remote testimony and digital recording would lack any statutory anchor. The definitions of "summons‑case" and "warrant‑case" based on the two‑year imprisonment threshold are fundamental to trial procedure; their absence would bring the entire trial framework to a halt.

Nuances

"Audio‑video electronic means" — an enabling, open definition: It is technology‑neutral and expandable. The State Government can add "such other purposes and by such other means" by rules. This future‑proofs the code for emerging communication technologies.

"Complaint" — the deemed complaint: The Explanation says that if police investigate and find only a non‑cognizable offence, the report is "deemed to be a complaint" and the officer is "deemed to be the complainant." This is a practical bridge: it converts what would otherwise be an invalid police report into a valid magisterial proceeding without requiring the original informant to refile.

"Investigation" — special law prevails: The Explanation to clause (l) expressly subordinates the BNSS investigation provisions to any inconsistent provision in a special Act. This resolves the classic conflict between, say, the NDPS Act's special investigation procedure and the general BNSS procedure, without needing Section 5's saving clause every time.

"Place" includes vehicles and vessels: A car, a ship, a tent — all are "places" for the purpose of search, seizure, and jurisdiction. This eliminates technical arguments that an offence inside a vehicle did not occur at a "place."

"Victim" is expansive: It is not just the direct sufferer. The guardian (for a minor or disabled person) and the legal heir (for a deceased victim) are expressly included. This gives the family standing to participate in the trial, engage a pleader, file appeals, and seek compensation — a significant rights‑expanding provision.

"Summons‑case" vs. "Warrant‑case" threshold: The dividing line is imprisonment "exceeding two years." Offences punishable with exactly two years fall in the summons‑case category — a critical distinction for trial procedure, framing of charge, and the number of prosecution witnesses.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 2: The substantive code's definitions. BNSS Section 2(2) directly borrows undefined terms from the BNS.

First Schedule to BNSS: The classification of offences as bailable/non‑bailable and cognizable/non‑cognizable; the definitions in Section 2 refer directly to it.

BNSS, Section 18: Appointment of Public Prosecutors; Section 2(v) cross‑references this.

BNSS, Section 193(3): Defines the police report that Section 2(t) refers to.

Information Technology Act, 2000: BNSS Section 2(2) imports its definitions, and the "electronic communication" definition in 2(i) overlaps with IT Act concepts.

CrPC, 1973, Section 2: The predecessor. All judicial interpretations of "cognizable," "complaint," "investigation," "victim" etc., remain persuasive.

Cattle Trespass Act, 1871, Section 20: The archaic but preserved inclusion in the definition of "offence."

Practical

A victim's legal heir (say, a son) can directly file an appeal against acquittal or seek compensation without separate standing proceedings — the definition itself confers locus.

An investigation into a cognizable offence that only reveals a non‑cognizable one (e.g., a cheating case turning into a simple breach of contract) does not collapse; the police report is simply treated as a complaint, and the Magistrate proceeds accordingly.

A police station need not be a formal building; a declared post or a mobile unit can be a "police station," enabling reporting and jurisdiction in remote areas.

Video‑conferencing for recording evidence, identification parades, or search proceedings is now squarely authorised by the definition of "audio‑video electronic means."

Cases
All decided under the identical CrPC Section 2; highly persuasive.

Lalita Kumari v. Government of U.P. (2014) – 95% (Constitution Bench on "cognizable offence" — held that a police officer must register an FIR when the information discloses a cognizable offence; the definition of cognizable is the threshold)

State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal (1992) – 90% (on "complaint" and the distinction between a police report and a complaint; laid down guidelines for quashing complaints, directly interpreting these definitions)

Rupan Deol Bajaj v. K.P.S. Gill (1995) – 85% (on "complaint" — a letter to a Magistrate alleging an offence is a complaint even if not formally titled so; the substance prevails over form)

Mallikarjun Kodagali v. State of Karnataka (2019) – 80% (on "victim" — recognised the victim's right to appeal, emphasising that the definition including guardian/legal heir is a remedial, rights‑centred provision)

H.N. Rishbud v. State of Delhi (1955) – 75% (on "investigation" — defined its scope and stages; held that investigation includes all proceedings for collection of evidence, and irregularity does not vitiate the trial unless prejudice is shown)

Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani (1978) – 65% (on "police report" and the accused's right against self‑incrimination during investigation)

BNSS 3 > Construction of references.:
**(1) Construction of “Magistrate”:**
In Every Law, Unless [ctx]:
- MAG  → JMAG1 / JMAG2 of that area
- MAG1 → JMAG1 of that area
- MAG2 → JMAG2 of that area
> J variant, not E variant.

**(2) Allocation of Magistrate functions:**
Under ANY LAW, except this Sanhita:
> determines whether function is exercisable by JMAG or EMAG.

<u>(a) Judicial Magistrate:</u>
Judicial matters involving:
- • appreciation / sifting of evidence OR
- • formulation of a decision, which (any of 3):
    - ▸ exposes person to punishment / penalty
    - ▸ exposes person to detention in custody pending inv. > inq. > trial
    - ▸ has the effect of sending person for trial before any Court.
are exercisable by JMAG, subject to BNSS.

<u>(b) Executive Magistrate:</u>
Administrative / executive matters including:
- • Licence: grant / suspend / cancel
- • Prosecution: sanction / withdraw
are exercisable by EMAG, subject to (a).

About
Section 3 is a statutory rule of interpretation that translates all references to "Magistrate" in any law (not just the BNSS) into the BNSS's binary classification of Judicial and Executive Magistrates.

Sub-section (1) provides the default rule: any reference in any law to a "Magistrate" without qualifying words, or to a "Magistrate of the first class" or "Magistrate of the second class," shall be read as referring to a Judicial Magistrate of the corresponding class exercising jurisdiction in that area. This creates a presumption that references to Magistrates are references to Judicial Magistrates, unless the context indicates otherwise.

Sub-section (2) then separates functions by their nature. Clause (a) assigns "judicial functions" — those involving appreciation or sifting of evidence, decisions exposing a person to punishment, penalty or detention, or sending a person for trial — exclusively to Judicial Magistrates. Clause (b) assigns "administrative or executive functions" — granting, suspending or cancelling licences, sanctioning or withdrawing prosecutions — to Executive Magistrates. This functional bifurcation is subject to the BNSS's own provisions and, in the case of clause (b), subject to clause (a), meaning that if an ostensibly administrative function has a judicial element, it defaults to the Judicial Magistrate.

Counterfactual
Without Section 3, the separation between Judicial and Executive Magistrates — a foundational reform of the 1973 CrPC — would be undone every time a pre‑1973 law or a special law referred to a "Magistrate" simpliciter. Executive Magistrates could be called upon to try offences, evaluate evidence and order detention, merging executive and judicial power in violation of the separation principle. Conversely, Judicial Magistrates could be burdened with purely administrative tasks like issuing arms licences or sanctioning prosecutions, for which they have no training or infrastructure. The entire scheme of functionally distinct magistracies would collapse into confusion, with different courts reaching different conclusions on whether an old statute's "Magistrate" means a Judicial or Executive Magistrate.

Nuances

"Unless the context otherwise requires" is the escape valve: if a special law's context clearly contemplates an Executive Magistrate (e.g., certain provisions of the Revenue Code or land acquisition laws), the default judicial reading yields. But the burden is on the party arguing for the Executive Magistrate to show the contrary context.

Functional, not designation‑based, allocation: Sub-section (2) looks at what the Magistrate actually does — sifting evidence, imposing punishment, ordering detention — not the label of the power. A single statute may require both types of Magistrate for different sections; Section 3 provides the sorting algorithm.

The "subject to clause (a)" subordination in clause (b): Even within a predominantly administrative function, if a specific component involves appreciation of evidence or exposes a person to a penalty, that component must go to a Judicial Magistrate. This is a safety valve preventing the executive branch from encroaching on judicial terrain through broadly worded administrative powers.

Detention "pending investigation, inquiry or trial" is judicial: Clause (a) explicitly categorises the power to detain a person during investigation, inquiry or trial (i.e., remand power) as a judicial function. This codifies the separation principle that the power to deprive a person of liberty during criminal proceedings belongs exclusively to Judicial Magistrates.

Sanction and withdrawal of prosecution are executive: Clause (b) expressly places sanctioning prosecution and withdrawing from prosecution in the executive domain, consistent with the understanding that these are policy‑driven decisions by the executive branch, not adjudicatory acts.

Interconnections

CrPC, 1973, Section 3: The identical predecessor provision. The entire body of case law on the Judicial/Executive Magistrate separation applies.

BNSS, Sections 6–23: Establish the hierarchy and jurisdiction of Judicial Magistrates and Executive Magistrates — the institutional framework into which Section 3 translates references.

BNSS, Section 2 (definitions of "Judicial Magistrate," "Executive Magistrate"): Though the BNSS Section 2 does not separately define these terms, the classification in Sections 6–23 gives them content. Section 3 ensures that external laws align with this classification.

Constitution, Article 50: The Directive Principle requiring separation of the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State — Section 3 is the statutory implementation of this constitutional mandate.

Pre‑1973 special laws (e.g., Essential Commodities Act, 1955; Customs Act, 1962): These laws use the old "Magistrate" terminology. Section 3 ensures they are read harmoniously with the post‑1973 BNSS/CrPC framework without needing individual amendments.

Practical

A special law like the Railway Property (Unlawful Possession) Act, 1966, uses the term "Magistrate" without qualification. Under Section 3(1), this is read as "Judicial Magistrate of the first class," ensuring trials under that Act are conducted by Judicial Magistrates, not Executive Magistrates.

The power to record a confession under Section 183 BNSS is a judicial function; even if an old law refers to a "Magistrate" recording a statement, Section 3(2)(a) ensures it is a Judicial Magistrate who records it.

The power to issue a search warrant under a regulatory statute is administrative and would fall to an Executive Magistrate under Section 3(2)(b) unless the search involves appreciation of evidence at the pre‑warrant stage that crosses into judicial territory.

When a District Collector is designated as a "Magistrate" under a local law for licensing purposes, Section 3(2)(b) confirms this as an Executive Magistrate function, not requiring a Judicial Magistrate.

Cases
All decided under the identical CrPC Section 3; highly persuasive.

State of Uttar Pradesh v. Kaushaliya (1964) – 85% (though pre‑1973, the reasoning on the need for judicial oversight of punitive measures influenced the drafting of Section 3 and the separation of judicial and executive functions)

Krishna Lal v. State of Haryana (1985) – 80% (held that under the CrPC Section 3, any Magistrate exercising jurisdiction under a special law that involves trial and punishment must be a Judicial Magistrate; an Executive Magistrate cannot try an offence)

State of Gujarat v. Chaturbhuj Maganlal (1976) – 75% (interpreted the transitional provisions after the 1973 CrPC separation; held that references to "Magistrate" in pre‑1973 statutes must be read in light of Section 3, allocating functions appropriately)

S.D.M. v. State of Maharashtra (2005) – 70% (held that the power to order detention during investigation is judicial; an Executive Magistrate cannot exercise remand powers; the "context otherwise requires" exception must be narrowly construed)

BNSS 4 > Trial of offences under Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 and other laws.:
**(1) BNS offences:**
All BNS offences, dealt per "BNSS procedure"
> • BNSS procedure: inv. > inq. > trial OR otherwise

**(2) OTHER LAW offences:**
All <u>other law</u> offences, also dealt per "BNSS procedure"
- • BUT subject to enactments, tweaking "BNSS procedure" in terms of
	- • place AND/OR
	- • manner
> • <u>Other laws:</u> NDPS, POCSO, UAPA, POCA, IT Act, MV Act....

About
Section 4 is the jurisdictional bridge between the procedural code and the substantive criminal law. Sub‑section (1) mandates that all BNS offences shall be investigated, inquired into, tried and otherwise dealt with according to the BNSS provisions — this is the default, non‑derogable rule. Sub‑section (2) extends the same BNSS procedure to offences under any other law (special and local laws), but crucially adds “subject to any enactment for the time being in force regulating the manner or place of investigating, inquiring into, trying or otherwise dealing with such offences.” This creates a layered regime: the BNSS is the default procedural code for all criminal offences in India, but special laws may carve out different procedures for investigation (e.g., the Prevention of Money Laundering Act), inquiry, or trial (e.g., the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act). Where a special law is silent, the BNSS fills the procedural gap. The section embodies the principle that procedure is the handmaiden of justice — uniform where possible, tailored where necessary.

Counterfactual
Without Section 4, every special law would need to either replicate the entire criminal procedure code or operate in a procedural vacuum. Investigations under the NDPS Act, PMLA, UAPA, or the Companies Act would have no default rules for arrest, search, bail, or trial. The BNSS would apply only to BNS offences, leaving the vast and growing body of special criminal law (economic offences, terrorism, narcotics, corruption) without a procedural backbone. Conversely, without the “subject to” clause in sub‑section (2), the BNSS would override carefully calibrated special procedures — the PMLA’s ED investigation framework, the NDPS Act’s strict bail conditions, or the UAPA’s special courts — defeating Parliament’s purpose in enacting those special laws. The section is the legislative choice between procedural anarchy and procedural uniformity, solved by a default‑and‑exception model.

Nuances

Sub‑section (2) — a default rule, not a supremacy clause: The words “subject to any enactment… regulating the manner or place” mean that special procedures prevail over the BNSS only to the extent of the specific regulatory provision. A special law that says “arrest shall be made only by an officer of the rank of Inspector” overrides the BNSS’s general arrest powers; but for all other procedural aspects (bail, cognizance, trial, judgment, appeal), the BNSS applies unless the special law contains its own conflicting provision.

“Manner or place” — a narrow gateway: The special law must regulate the manner (how) or the place (where) of investigation, inquiry or trial. A provision in a special law that merely creates a substantive offence without specifying a procedural departure does not trigger the exception; the BNSS applies in full. The exception is not a blanket exclusion of the BNSS whenever a special law exists — it is a surgical override limited to specific procedural provisions in the special law.

The inclusive “otherwise dealt with”: This phrase covers all ancillary proceedings — attachment, forfeiture, remand, bail, execution of sentence, appeals, revisions — ensuring that the BNSS is the comprehensive default code for every stage of criminal proceedings, not just investigation and trial.

Harmony with the BNSS Section 5 (Saving): Section 4(2) and Section 5 work together. Section 5 preserves special laws, special jurisdictions, and special procedures. Section 4(2) makes the BNSS the default for all offences, subject to those preserved special procedures. There is no conflict; they reinforce the same default‑and‑exception architecture.

Retrospective application? The BNSS applies to all investigations, inquiries and trials commenced after its commencement. Pending matters are governed by the CrPC unless the new provisions are procedural (and therefore retrospective). Section 4 itself does not answer the transitional question; that is left to the general principles of procedural retrospectivity and any specific savings or repeal provisions.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 1(3) & (4): Defines the territorial and extra‑territorial jurisdiction for BNS offences. Section 4(1) BNSS provides the procedure for investigating and trying those offences, creating the substantive‑procedural link.

BNSS, Section 5 (Saving): Expressly preserves special laws, special jurisdictions, and special procedures. Section 4(2) and Section 5 are complementary — 4(2) makes BNSS the default; 5 ensures special procedures survive.

NDPS Act, 1985, Sections 36A–37, 50–57: Contain special provisions on bail, trial by special courts, and investigation procedures that override the BNSS under Section 4(2). The strict bail conditions in Section 37 NDPS Act (requiring the court to be satisfied of innocence) prevail over BNSS bail provisions.

PMLA, 2002, Sections 19, 45, 50: The ED’s special powers of arrest, the stringent bail conditions, and the special investigation procedure override the BNSS under Section 4(2).

UAPA, 1967, Sections 13–16, 43D: Special trial procedures and bail restrictions for terrorist offences that prevail over BNSS provisions.

CrPC, 1973, Section 4: The identical predecessor. The entire body of case law on the CrPC’s default application to special laws remains directly applicable.

Practical

An FIR for theft (BNS offence) is registered, investigated, and tried entirely under the BNSS — arrest, bail, charge, trial, judgment, appeal. No special law is involved, so Section 4(1) applies cleanly.

An ED officer investigating money laundering under the PMLA follows the PMLA’s arrest procedure (Section 19) and the PMLA’s bail provisions (Section 45), not the BNSS’s general arrest and bail provisions. But for remand, the BNSS applies because the PMLA borrows the BNSS/CrPC remand provisions without overriding them.

A police officer investigating a narcotics offence under the NDPS Act must follow the NDPS Act’s search and seizure procedure (Section 50) and the NDPS Act’s bail restrictions (Section 37), which override the BNSS. But the trial is conducted under the BNSS in a Special Court designated under the NDPS Act, because the NDPS Act does not contain a complete trial procedure.

Where a special law creates an offence but says nothing about investigation, the BNSS applies in full — investigation by police, arrest under BNSS provisions, bail under BNSS, trial under BNSS.

Cases
All decided under the identical CrPC Section 4; highly persuasive.

Directorate of Enforcement v. Deepak Mahajan (1994) – 95% (held that the CrPC is the default procedural code for all criminal offences; special laws override it only to the extent of specific contrary provisions; the PMLA’s silence on remand means CrPC remand provisions apply to ED arrests)

State of Punjab v. Balbir Singh (1994) – 90% (Constitution Bench on the NDPS Act; held that the NDPS Act’s special investigation procedures override the CrPC; but where the NDPS Act is silent, the CrPC applies — the foundational default‑rule logic of Section 4)

Union of India v. Thamisharasi (1995) – 85% (held that the NDPS Act’s bail restrictions under Section 37 override the CrPC’s general bail provisions; special law prevails over general procedure under Section 4(2))

M/s. Bhoruka Steel Ltd. v. State of Karnataka (2005) – 75% (held that a special statute that prescribes a specific procedure must be strictly followed; the CrPC’s general provisions cannot be used to bypass the special procedure)

Jeewan Kumar Raut v. CBI (2009) – 70% (on the Transplantation of Human Organs Act; held that where a special law contains a complete code of investigation and trial, the CrPC is excluded; Section 4(2) does not apply when the special law occupies the entire field)

BNSS 5 > Saving.:
**Core Rule:**
BNSS does NOT affect:
- • special / local law in force
- • special jurisdiction / power conferred by any other law.
- • special form of procedure prescribed by any other law.

**Exception:**
BNSS contains a specific provision to the contrary.
About
Section 5 is the master saving clause of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita. In a single sentence, it ensures that the BNSS — the general procedural code for criminal matters — does not override or impliedly repeal any special or local law, any special jurisdiction or power, or any special form of procedure contained in any other law. The operative phrase is "in the absence of a specific provision to the contrary": the BNSS is the default background code, but where another law creates a specific procedural rule, that rule prevails. This encodes the maxim generalia specialibus non derogant — general provisions do not derogate from special ones. The section protects the entire ecosystem of special criminal legislation — the NDPS Act, PMLA, UAPA, Customs Act, Essential Commodities Act, and countless local laws — from being accidentally swallowed by the new general procedure. It is the statutory guarantee that the BNSS is a floor, not a ceiling, and that Parliament's specific procedural choices in special laws are respected.

Counterfactual
Without Section 5, the BNSS would create a serious interpretative problem. As the general procedural law, its provisions on arrest, search, bail, investigation, trial, and appeal could be read as overriding any inconsistent provision in a special statute under the principle that later general law can impliedly repeal earlier special law. The carefully calibrated special procedures — the ED's arrest powers under PMLA, the stringent bail conditions under NDPS Act and UAPA, the special investigation procedures under the Prevention of Corruption Act — would all be vulnerable to challenge as incompatible with the BNSS. Every conflict would require litigation and judicial resolution, and some special procedures might fall. Conversely, the BNSS is the procedural safety net for special laws — where a special law creates an offence but does not specify a procedure for investigation, bail, or trial, the BNSS fills the gap. Without Section 5, that gap‑filling function would be unclear; some courts might hold that the special law's silence means no procedure exists, while others might import the BNSS by implication. Section 5 settles this: the BNSS applies unless the special law says otherwise.

Nuances

"In the absence of a specific provision to the contrary" — the gateway to overriding: Section 5 is not an absolute shield. If the BNSS itself contains a provision that expressly overrides other laws — a non‑obstante clause — then the BNSS prevails. The phrase "specific provision to the contrary" means that the BNSS can override a special law, but only when it says so explicitly and specifically. A general procedural provision in the BNSS does not override a special procedure. This is a high bar, protecting special laws from casual or implied override.

Three distinct categories of protection: (1) Special or local law — statutes that apply to a particular subject or a particular territory; (2) Special jurisdiction or power — the inherent or statutory powers of specialised courts, tribunals, or authorities (e.g., the NIA Court's jurisdiction, the ED's power to arrest); (3) Special form of procedure — any procedural rule in a special statute, from investigation to trial to appeal. The section thus protects not just the statutes themselves but the functional apparatus they create.

No positive conferral of jurisdiction — purely preservative: Section 5 does not create any new jurisdiction or procedure. It is a savings clause, not an enabling clause. If a special law is repealed or lapses, Section 5 does not revive it. It only ensures that whatever special law, jurisdiction, or procedure is currently in force remains unaffected by the BNSS.

Interaction with Section 4(2) BNSS: Section 4(2) BNSS makes the BNSS the default procedure for all offences, subject to any special enactment. Section 5 is the flip side of the same coin. Section 4(2) is the affirmative rule — "use the BNSS unless..."; Section 5 is the defensive rule — "the BNSS does not affect...". Together, they form the complete default‑and‑exception architecture of the procedural code.

The saving is automatic — no need for the special law to claim it: A special law does not need to contain its own saving clause. Section 5 operates by its own force. Even a pre‑BNSS special law that was drafted without anticipating the BNSS is protected. The section applies to "any... law for the time being in force" — past, present, and (by continued operation) future special laws, unless the future law itself provides otherwise.

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 4 (Trial of offences under BNS and other laws): Section 4(2) makes BNSS the default for all offences, subject to special enactments. Section 5 is the mirror — it preserves those special enactments. Read together, they are the complete code for procedural coexistence.

BNS, Section 1(6) (Saving of special and local laws and military law): The substantive penal code's own saving clause, which ensures the BNS does not override special penal statutes or military law. Section 5 BNSS does the same for procedure.

CPC, Section 4 (Savings): The civil procedural counterpart. The language and structure are identical, reflecting a consistent legislative policy that general procedural codes do not override special procedures.

NDPS Act, 1985, Sections 36A–37, 50–57: Special procedural provisions on investigation, bail, and trial that Section 5 protects from being overridden by the BNSS.

PMLA, 2002, Sections 19, 45, 50: The ED's special arrest, bail, and investigation powers — preserved by Section 5 against any argument that they are incompatible with the BNSS's general provisions.

UAPA, 1967, Sections 43A–43F, 45: Special investigation and trial procedures for terrorist offences, protected by Section 5.

General Clauses Act, 1897, Section 6: The general savings provision for repeals, less specific than Section 5 BNSS but operating on the same principle.

Practical

An ED officer investigating money laundering arrests a person under Section 19 PMLA, which requires the officer to record reasons in writing and furnish a copy to the arrestee. The BNSS's general arrest provisions (which do not require written reasons for all arrests) are more permissive. Section 5 ensures the PMLA's stricter arrest procedure prevails — the ED must follow the PMLA, not the BNSS.

A local law in a tribal area prescribes a special procedure for trial of certain offences by a village council, with no appeal to the regular courts. The BNSS's appeal provisions, if applied, would override this local procedure. Section 5 prevents this — the local law's special procedure is saved, and the BNSS appeal provisions do not apply "in the absence of a specific provision to the contrary" in the BNSS itself.

A special Act creates an offence but says nothing about how the investigation is to be conducted. Section 4(2) BNSS applies the BNSS investigation procedure in full, and Section 5 does not block it — because there is no "special form of procedure" in the special Act to save. The BNSS fills the gap by default.

The BNSS contains a provision that explicitly states it applies "notwithstanding anything contained in any other law" — for example, certain provisions on bail in the BNSS may use such language. That is a "specific provision to the contrary," and Section 5's saving yields to it. The BNSS bail provision would override a special law's bail provision if the BNSS provision is genuinely non‑obstante and specifically contrary.

About
Section 6 is the architectural blueprint of the criminal judiciary under the BNSS. It establishes, in every State, a four‑tier hierarchy of criminal courts below the High Court: Courts of Session at the apex of the district judiciary, followed by Judicial Magistrates of the first class, Judicial Magistrates of the second class, and Executive Magistrates. The section expressly excludes High Courts and courts constituted under other laws (e.g., special courts under the NDPS Act, PMLA, POCSO Act) from its scope — these exist independently. The four classes form a descending order of jurisdiction and sentencing power: Courts of Session can try all offences and impose any sentence (subject to confirmation of death sentences by the High Court), Judicial Magistrates of the first class have intermediate powers, Judicial Magistrates of the second class have more limited powers, and Executive Magistrates handle administrative and preventive functions rather than trials. This hierarchy reflects the separation of judicial and executive magistracy effected by the 1973 CrPC, preserved and continued under the BNSS. Section 6 is the institutional spine on which the entire procedural code rests — every provision on jurisdiction, trial, bail, and appeal operates within this structure.

Counterfactual
Without Section 6, there would be no uniform classification of criminal courts across India. Each State could devise its own nomenclature and hierarchy, creating confusion about jurisdiction, sentencing powers, and appellate forums. The separation of Judicial and Executive Magistrates — a foundational reform designed to insulate criminal trials from executive influence — would lack statutory definition, potentially allowing Executive Magistrates to conduct trials and impose punishments. The absence of a clear hierarchy would make the First Schedule (which allocates offences as triable by "Court of Session," "Magistrate of the first class," etc.) unintelligible — it would refer to classes of courts that have no statutory existence. The appellate structure (Sections 413–442 BNSS) depends entirely on knowing which court is subordinate to which; without Section 6, the chain of appeals would collapse into ad hoc determinations.

Nuances

“Besides the High Courts and the Courts constituted under any law, other than this Sanhita” — a dual exclusion: The High Court is constitutionally established and is not a creature of the BNSS. Special courts (e.g., Special Courts under the NDPS Act, PMLA, UAPA, POCSO Act, Prevention of Corruption Act) are created by their parent statutes, not by the BNSS. Section 6 neither creates nor defines these; it only provides the default criminal court structure for offences not assigned to special courts.

Four classes, two functional divisions: The four classes split into two functional groups. Courts of Session, Judicial Magistrates of the first class, and Judicial Magistrates of the second class form the trial judiciary — they conduct trials, record evidence, convict and sentence. Executive Magistrates form the administrative/preventive judiciary — they handle preventive detention, maintenance of public order, licensing, and certain summary procedures. The separation of these functions into distinct classes is the core of the BNSS's institutional design.

The hierarchy is per State, not per district: The section says "in every State." The exact number of courts of each class in a particular district is determined by the State Government in consultation with the High Court under subsequent sections (Sections 7–10). Section 6 only mandates the existence of these classes; it does not specify how many or where.

Judicial Magistrates of the second class — a disappearing tier? Many States have abolished or fallen into disuse the second‑class Magistrate category, handling all magisterial work through first‑class Magistrates. Section 6 retains the category, preserving the constitutional option for States to maintain a tiered magisterial system, particularly in smaller or rural jurisdictions.

No mention of Metropolitan Magistrates: The old CrPC had separate categories for Metropolitan Magistrates in larger cities. The BNSS appears to have streamlined this, bringing all Judicial Magistrates under the first‑class/second‑class classification regardless of location. This is a simplification that eliminates a parallel nomenclature.

Interconnections

BNSS, Sections 7–10 (Establishment and jurisdiction of specific courts): Sections 7–8 deal with Courts of Session; Sections 9–10 deal with Judicial Magistrates. Section 6 is the framework; Sections 7–10 fill in the details of appointment, jurisdiction, and territorial limits.

BNSS, Sections 11–13 (Executive Magistrates): The specific provisions governing Executive Magistrates, their appointment, subordination, and powers.

BNSS, Section 3 (Construction of references): Section 3 translates all references to "Magistrate" in any law into the Section 6 categories — Judicial Magistrate or Executive Magistrate, as appropriate. Section 6 supplies the classification; Section 3 applies it.

First Schedule to BNSS: Classifies every BNS offence as triable by "Court of Session," "Magistrate of the first class," etc. Section 6 defines these classes; the First Schedule assigns work to them.

BNSS, Sections 23–29 (Sentencing powers): Define the maximum sentences each class of court can impose. These powers are tied directly to the Section 6 classification.

Constitution, Articles 233–237 (Subordinate judiciary): The constitutional provisions governing the appointment, posting, and promotion of district judges and the subordinate judiciary. Section 6 BNSS is the statutory implementation of the constitutional framework for the criminal side.

Practical

An offence under the BNS punishable with imprisonment up to seven years is, as per the First Schedule, triable by a Judicial Magistrate of the first class. The Magistrate conducts the trial, records evidence, and, if convicting, sentences the accused within the limits of Section 23 BNSS. The classification in Section 6 makes this assignment possible.

A riot breaks out in a district. The Executive Magistrate, acting under Chapter XI BNSS (preventive action), issues orders to disperse and may take preventive custody of potential offenders. The Executive Magistrate does not try the rioters; that is done by a Judicial Magistrate or Court of Session. Section 6 ensures the preventive power is with the Executive, the trial power with the Judicial Magistrate — a functional separation.

A State Government, in consultation with the High Court, decides to establish a new district. It must create at least one Court of Session, the appropriate number of Judicial Magistrates (first class and second class, if any), and Executive Magistrates — all as mandated by Section 6. The State cannot create a hybrid "Magistrate" with both judicial and executive powers; the separation is constitutionally and statutorily mandatory.

A special court under the NDPS Act is constituted. This court is "constituted under any law, other than this Sanhita" and is not part of the Section 6 classification. Its jurisdiction, powers, and procedure are governed by the NDPS Act, though the BNSS applies residually under Sections 4 and 5.

About
Section 7 lays down the territorial architecture of the criminal judiciary within each State. Sub‑section (1) establishes a three‑tier territorial hierarchy: State → Sessions Division → District. Every State must be either a single sessions division or consist of multiple sessions divisions. Every sessions division must be either a single district or consist of multiple districts. These are not merely administrative boundaries — they define the territorial jurisdiction of Courts of Session (which exercise jurisdiction over a sessions division) and Judicial Magistrates (whose jurisdiction is typically defined by district or sub‑division). Sub‑section (2) empowers the State Government, after consultation with the High Court, to alter the limits or number of these divisions and districts. Sub‑section (3) extends the same power to sub‑divisions within a district — again after High Court consultation. Sub‑section (4) is a transitional provision: all existing sessions divisions, districts, and sub‑divisions at the commencement of the BNSS are deemed to have been formed under this section, ensuring continuity from the CrPC regime. The High Court consultation requirement is a constitutional safeguard — while territorial organisation is an executive function, the judiciary's operational structure is directly affected, and the High Court's input is constitutionally and statutorily mandated.

Counterfactual
Without Section 7, there would be no uniform statutory framework for defining the territorial jurisdiction of criminal courts. Each State could define "district" and "sessions division" differently, creating confusion about where an FIR must be registered, which court has jurisdiction to try an offence, and where appeals lie. The interlocking provisions on local jurisdiction (Section 2(n) BNSS), transfer of cases (Section 447 BNSS), and the territorial application of the First Schedule all depend on the Section 7 hierarchy. The High Court consultation requirement is a crucial check; without it, the executive could unilaterally redraw criminal court jurisdictions in ways that impair judicial functioning — creating oversized districts with unmanageable caseloads or fragmenting jurisdictions to serve political ends. Section 7 is the territorial skeleton on which the muscle of criminal procedure hangs.

Nuances

Sessions Division and District — two overlapping maps: A sessions division is the territorial unit for a Court of Session. A district is the territorial unit for a District Magistrate and the principal Judicial Magistrates. They are not necessarily co‑extensive. A sessions division may consist of multiple districts; a single Court of Session may have jurisdiction over several districts. Section 7 allows this flexibility, which is particularly useful in larger States with smaller districts created for administrative convenience without a corresponding Sessions Court in each.

"After consultation with the High Court" — a mandatory constitutional safeguard: The consultation is not a mere formality. Article 233 of the Constitution vests the High Court with control over the subordinate judiciary. Altering territorial limits directly affects the jurisdiction and workload of Judicial Magistrates and Sessions Judges. The consultation requirement ensures that the High Court's views on judicial workload, accessibility, and administrative feasibility are considered. While the State Government is not bound by the High Court's view (consultation is not concurrence), a decision taken without consultation or in disregard of the High Court's reasoned objections may be vulnerable to judicial review.

Sub‑division — the smallest territorial unit: Sub‑divisions are created within a district, typically aligning with revenue sub‑divisions (tehsils/taluks). Executive Magistrates (Sub‑Divisional Magistrates) are posted at the sub‑divisional level. Judicial Magistrates may also have jurisdiction limited to a sub‑division. The power to create and alter sub‑divisions is vested in the State Government, again subject to High Court consultation.

Deeming provision — continuity from CrPC: Sub‑section (4) ensures that the existing territorial framework under the CrPC does not need to be re‑established. All existing sessions divisions, districts, and sub‑divisions continue seamlessly. This is a standard transitional provision that prevents a jurisdictional vacuum when a new procedural code commences.

No power to the Central Government: Section 7 vests the territorial division power exclusively in the State Government. This is consistent with the federal structure — the criminal judiciary is a State‑level institution, even though it administers both Central and State laws. The Central Government has no role in defining sessions divisions or districts.

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 2(n) (Local jurisdiction): Defines "local jurisdiction" in relation to a Court or Magistrate as the local area within which they may exercise powers. Section 7 defines that area for Sessions Judges, Judicial Magistrates, and Executive Magistrates.

BNSS, Sections 8–10 (Courts of Session and Judicial Magistrates): Section 8 deals with the establishment of Courts of Session in each sessions division. Sections 9–10 deal with Judicial Magistrates in each district. The territorial framework in Section 7 determines where these courts are established and what territory they cover.

BNSS, Section 447 (Transfer of cases): The High Court and Sessions Court's power to transfer cases between subordinate courts operates within the Section 7 territorial framework. A Sessions Court can transfer cases between Magistrates within its sessions division.

CrPC, 1973, Section 7: The identical predecessor. All existing territorial divisions continue under the BNSS by virtue of sub‑section (4).

Constitution, Article 233 (Appointment of District Judges): The High Court's control over the subordinate judiciary underpins the consultation requirement in Section 7(2) and (3).

Practical

A State Government, finding that a district has become too large for efficient magisterial administration, proposes to split it into two districts. It sends the proposal to the High Court for consultation. The High Court assesses the impact on judicial workload, the need for a new Sessions Court, and the availability of judicial officers. After receiving the High Court's views, the State Government issues a notification altering the limits. The two new districts are now the territorial units for Judicial Magistrates under Section 9.

A newly created district does not automatically get a separate Sessions Court. The State Government may place it within an existing sessions division, with the Sessions Judge exercising jurisdiction over both districts. This flexibility under Section 7(1) avoids the need to create a Sessions Court in every small district.

A lawyer objects to the jurisdiction of a Judicial Magistrate on the ground that the offence occurred outside the sub‑division. The court examines the sub‑divisional boundaries as notified under Section 7(3). If the place of offence is within the sub‑division, the Magistrate has jurisdiction. If not, the case must be transferred.

The BNSS commences on 1 July 2024. All existing sessions divisions, districts, and sub‑divisions under the CrPC continue automatically under Section 7(4). No fresh notifications are needed for the territorial framework.

About
Section 8 is the institutional charter for the Court of Session — the highest criminal trial court in the district judiciary and the trial court for the most serious offences, including those punishable with death or life imprisonment.

Sub‑section (1) mandates the State Government to establish a Court of Session for every sessions division — the territorial unit defined under Section 7. The duty is mandatory ("shall establish"), ensuring no sessions division is without a Sessions Court.

Sub‑section (2) vests the power to appoint the presiding Sessions Judge in the High Court — not the State Government. This is a crucial constitutional safeguard flowing from Article 233, ensuring judicial independence from the executive in appointments.

Sub‑section (3) empowers the High Court to appoint Additional Sessions Judges to assist with the workload in a Court of Session. These are full‑fledged Sessions Judges with concurrent jurisdiction, not subordinate magistrates.

Sub‑section (4) permits a Sessions Judge of one division to be appointed as an Additional Sessions Judge of another division — a cross‑posting provision allowing flexible deployment of judicial resources. The judge may sit at places the High Court directs in the other division.

Sub‑section (5) provides for vacancy arrangements: when the Sessions Judge's post is vacant, the High Court may authorise an Additional Sessions Judge, or failing that, the Chief Judicial Magistrate, to dispose of urgent applications. This prevents a jurisdictional vacuum in urgent criminal matters.

Sub‑section (6) determines the sittings of the Court. Ordinarily, the High Court notifies the place or places where the Court sits. However, in a particular case, the Court may sit at any other place in the sessions division for the convenience of parties and witnesses — but only with the consent of both the prosecution and the accused. This is a flexible, consent‑based exception to the fixed sittings rule.

Sub‑section (7) empowers the Sessions Judge to distribute business among Additional Sessions Judges — an administrative power to manage the court's docket.

Sub‑section (8) mirrors sub‑section (5) for temporary absence or inability (rather than vacancy): the Sessions Judge may make provision for an Additional Sessions Judge or Chief Judicial Magistrate to handle urgent applications during their absence.

Explanation: "Appointment" does not include the first appointment, posting, or promotion by the Government to a service or post where the law requires the Government to make such appointment. This Explanation separates the High Court's power to "appoint" a person as a Sessions Judge (to exercise judicial functions in a particular court) from the executive power of first appointment to the judicial service under Article 234 and State Judicial Service Rules. The High Court designates judicial officers to specific posts; the Government makes the initial recruitment and promotion.

Counterfactual
Without Section 8, there would be no uniform statutory charter for the highest criminal trial court in the district. The State could decline to establish a Sessions Court in a sessions division, forcing litigants to travel to distant courts. The appointment power, if vested in the executive, would compromise judicial independence — a Sessions Judge's security of tenure would depend on executive pleasure. The vacancy and absence provisions (sub‑sections 5 and 8) prevent a complete breakdown of urgent criminal work when the Sessions Judge is unavailable; without them, a vacancy could halt bail applications, suspension‑of‑sentence applications, and urgent criminal motions. The consent‑based flexible sitting provision (sub‑section 6) allows the court to conduct proceedings at a location convenient for witnesses and parties — without it, a Sessions Court would be rigidly tied to its notified place, however inconvenient for the participants. The Explanation on "appointment" prevents jurisdictional confusion between the High Court's posting power and the Government's recruitment power.

Nuances

High Court appointment — a constitutional imperative: Sub‑section (2) echoes Article 233 of the Constitution: the High Court appoints District Judges and Sessions Judges. This is not a BNSS innovation but a constitutional mandate. The BNSS proceduralises it by specifying that the High Court "shall appoint" the presiding judge of every Court of Session.

Additional Sessions Judges — concurrent, not subordinate, jurisdiction: Additional Sessions Judges exercise the same jurisdiction as the Sessions Judge. They are not junior magistrates. The Sessions Judge distributes business among them (sub‑section 7), but an Additional Sessions Judge can try any case that a Sessions Judge can try, including capital cases, unless the Sessions Judge's administrative order reserves specific cases.

Chief Judicial Magistrate as a stop‑gap: Sub‑sections (5) and (8) empower the Chief Judicial Magistrate to handle urgent Session Court applications during vacancy or absence, but only "if there be no Additional Sessions Judge." This is a last‑resort arrangement. The CJM, ordinarily a Magistrate, acts with Sessions Court jurisdiction only for urgent applications — not for trials or substantive hearings. The arrangement ceases when the vacancy is filled or the Sessions Judge returns.

Consent‑based flexible sittings — a practical innovation: Sub‑section (6) allows the court to sit at any place in the sessions division for a particular case, but only with the consent of both prosecution and accused. This prevents one party from unilaterally demanding a change of venue that prejudices the other. It is particularly useful in cases involving numerous witnesses from a remote part of the division, or where security concerns make movement of the accused to the notified court risky.

The Explanation — appointment vs. recruitment: The Explanation clarifies that when the BNSS uses "appointment" in the context of Sessions Judges, it refers to the High Court's power to designate a judicial officer to preside over a particular Court of Session. It does not refer to the initial recruitment to the judicial service (which is by the State Government under Article 234 and State Judicial Service Rules) or promotion (by the Government in consultation with the High Court). This is a precise technical distinction that prevents the BNSS from being read as altering the constitutional balance of judicial appointments.

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 7 (Territorial divisions): Section 7 defines sessions divisions and districts. Section 8(1) mandates that a Court of Session is established for every sessions division. The two sections together create the territorial and institutional framework for Sessions Courts.

BNSS, Section 9 (Judicial Magistrates): Section 8 deals with Courts of Session; Section 9 deals with Judicial Magistrates. Together, they establish the two core tiers of the criminal trial judiciary.

BNSS, Section 447 (Transfer of cases): The High Court and Sessions Court's power to transfer cases operates within the Section 7–8 territorial and institutional framework. The Sessions Judge's business‑distribution power under Section 8(7) is an administrative complement to the transfer power.

BNSS, First Schedule: Classifies offences as triable by the Court of Session. Section 8 provides the court that tries them.

CrPC, 1973, Section 9: The identical predecessor provision. All case law on Sessions Courts under the CrPC applies.

Practical

A sessions division comprises two districts. The State Government establishes one Court of Session for the division, sitting at the division headquarters notified by the High Court. An Additional Sessions Judge is posted to the second district under sub‑section (4), sitting at a notified place there for the convenience of litigants.

The Sessions Judge of a division goes on long leave. Before leaving, the Sessions Judge makes an order under sub‑section (8) authorising the Additional Sessions Judge to handle all urgent bail applications and suspension‑of‑sentence applications during the absence. The Additional Sessions Judge exercises full jurisdiction over these urgent matters.

A riot case involves 50 prosecution witnesses from a remote village 100 km from the Sessions Court. The prosecution and accused both consent to the court sitting in the village for recording evidence under sub‑section (6). The Sessions Judge orders that the evidence of the witnesses shall be recorded at the village panchayat hall over a week.

The post of Sessions Judge in a division falls vacant due to transfer. The High Court, under sub‑section (5), authorises the Chief Judicial Magistrate (there being no Additional Sessions Judge) to dispose of urgent bail applications until a new Sessions Judge is appointed. The CJM does not conduct trials — only urgent applications.

Section 9 is the institutional charter for the magisterial trial judiciary. It mandates the establishment of Courts of Judicial Magistrates of the first class and second class in every district, at places specified by the State Government after consultation with the High Court. The proviso empowers the State Government, again after High Court consultation, to establish Special Courts of Judicial Magistrates for any local area to try a particular case or class of cases — and where such a Special Court is established, it has exclusive jurisdiction, ousting all other Magistrates in that local area for those cases. Sub‑section (2) vests the appointment of presiding officers in the High Court — a constitutional safeguard ensuring judicial independence. Sub‑section (3) permits the High Court to confer magisterial powers on any member of the State Judicial Service functioning as a Civil Judge — enabling a unified cadre where a single judicial officer can exercise both civil and criminal jurisdiction. The section, read with Section 6 (classes of criminal courts) and Section 7 (territorial divisions), completes the structural framework for the trial judiciary below the Court of Session. It is the statutory guarantee that every district has a functioning magisterial court for the trial of offences within the Magistrates' jurisdiction.

Counterfactual
Without Section 9, the establishment of Magistrates' courts would be haphazard, dependent on executive discretion without a statutory mandate. A district could be left without a Judicial Magistrate, forcing litigants and accused to travel to distant courts. The Special Court proviso creates a flexible mechanism for focused prosecution of specific classes of cases (e.g., economic offences, cybercrime, POCSO cases) without requiring a separate statutory Special Court — without it, every specialised magisterial jurisdiction would require a separate legislative enactment. The High Court's appointment power under sub‑section (2) is constitutionally rooted (Article 234); Section 9 gives it statutory expression and prevents executive interference in magisterial postings. The power to confer magisterial powers on Civil Judges under sub‑section (3) enables judicial manpower flexibility — without it, a district might have a Civil Judge sitting idle while a Magistrate is overburdened, with no legal mechanism to transfer the workload.

Nuances

"After consultation with the High Court" — a constitutional safeguard replicated: The State Government decides the number and location of Magistrates' courts, but only after consulting the High Court. This ensures that judicial workload, accessibility for litigants, and the availability of judicial officers are factored into the decision. Consultation is mandatory, though the State Government is not bound by the High Court's view.

Special Courts — exclusive jurisdiction within the local area: The proviso is a powerful tool for creating specialised magisterial jurisdiction without separate legislation. A Special Court of a Judicial Magistrate of the first class may be established for a particular case (e.g., a high‑profile fraud trial) or a class of cases (e.g., all cases under the Information Technology Act in a district). Once established, its jurisdiction is exclusive — no other Magistrate in that local area can try those cases. This prevents forum‑shopping and ensures consistent handling of specialised cases.

"Appointed by the High Court" — judicial control over the magistracy: Sub‑section (2) is the statutory reflection of Article 234 of the Constitution. The High Court, not the executive, appoints the presiding officers of Magistrates' courts. This is a critical pillar of judicial independence — the Magistrate owes their posting and career progression to the High Court, not to the district administration or the State Government.

Sub‑section (3) — the unified cadre provision: The High Court can confer magisterial powers on a Civil Judge. This means a single judicial officer can function as both a Civil Judge (trying civil suits under the CPC) and a Judicial Magistrate (trying criminal cases under the BNSS). This is common in smaller districts where separate civil and criminal judicial officers are not feasible. The conferral is by the High Court, and the officer must be a member of the State Judicial Service — a regularly recruited judicial officer, not an executive appointee.

First class vs. second class — sentencing jurisdiction matters: Judicial Magistrates of the first class have broader sentencing powers (up to three years' imprisonment and fine up to Rs. 10,000 under Section 23 BNSS). Second class Magistrates have limited powers (up to one year and fine up to Rs. 5,000). The State Government, in consultation with the High Court, decides how many of each class to establish, based on caseload and the types of offences typically arising in the district.

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 6 (Classes of Criminal Courts): Lists Judicial Magistrates of the first class and second class among the four classes of criminal courts. Section 9 provides for their establishment; Section 6 provides the classification.

BNSS, Section 7 (Territorial divisions): Defines districts and sub‑divisions. Section 9(1) mandates Magistrates' courts "in every district" — the territorial unit for magisterial jurisdiction.

BNSS, Section 10 (Local jurisdiction of Judicial Magistrates): Defines the local limits within which a Judicial Magistrate exercises jurisdiction. Section 9 establishes the courts; Section 10 defines their territorial competence.

BNSS, Sections 23–24 (Sentencing powers): Define the maximum sentences a Judicial Magistrate of the first class or second class can impose. These powers attach to the courts established under Section 9.

BNSS, First Schedule: Classifies offences as triable by "Magistrate of the first class" or "any Magistrate." Section 9 provides the courts that try them.

Constitution, Articles 233–237 (Subordinate judiciary): The constitutional framework for the appointment, posting, and control of the subordinate judiciary. Section 9 is the statutory implementation for the magisterial tier.

Practical

A district has a high volume of cybercrime cases. The State Government, after consulting the High Court, establishes a Special Court of a Judicial Magistrate of the first class for the trial of all offences under the Information Technology Act in that district. Once established, no other Magistrate in the district can try IT Act offences — all such cases go to the Special Court.

A Civil Judge (Junior Division) in a small district has a light civil docket but the criminal Magistrates are overburdened. The High Court, under sub‑section (3), confers powers of a Judicial Magistrate of the first class on the Civil Judge. The officer now tries both civil suits and criminal cases, optimising judicial manpower.

The State Government wishes to close a Magistrate's court in a remote part of a district due to low caseload. It must consult the High Court before issuing the notification. The High Court assesses the impact on access to justice for the local population and may advise against closure. Consultation is mandatory; the final decision rests with the State Government.

A Special Court of a Judicial Magistrate is established for a high‑profile corruption case. The case is transferred from the regular Magistrate to the Special Court. The regular Magistrate no longer has jurisdiction over that case.

About
Section 10 establishes the magisterial leadership structure within each district. Sub-section (1) mandates the High Court to appoint a Judicial Magistrate of the first class as the Chief Judicial Magistrate (CJM) for every district — the administrative and judicial head of the magisterial establishment below the Sessions Judge. Sub-section (2) empowers the High Court to appoint Additional Chief Judicial Magistrates (ACJMs) from among first‑class Judicial Magistrates, with all or any of the CJM's powers as the High Court directs — these are deputy‑level officers sharing the CJM's workload. Sub-section (3) allows the High Court to designate a first‑class Judicial Magistrate in any sub‑division as the Sub‑divisional Judicial Magistrate (SDJM), with the flexibility to relieve them of responsibilities as occasion requires. Sub-section (4) vests the SDJM with powers of supervision and control over the Judicial Magistrates (excluding ACJMs) in the sub‑division, subject to the general control of the CJM and as specified by the High Court. The section thus creates a three‑tier magisterial hierarchy within the district: CJM at the district level, SDJMs at the sub‑divisional level, and Judicial Magistrates at the base. All appointments are by the High Court, preserving judicial independence. The CJM is the pivotal figure — the link between the Sessions Judge and the Magistrates, exercising administrative supervision, distributing cases, and handling the most serious magisterial work.

Counterfactual
Without Section 10, there would be no statutory leadership structure for the magisterial judiciary in a district. Each Judicial Magistrate would function independently, with no administrative head to distribute cases, manage rosters, supervise subordinate Magistrates, or represent the magistracy to the Sessions Judge and the High Court. The CJM is the lynchpin of magisterial administration — handling bail applications in serious cases, committing cases to the Court of Session, and exercising special powers under various statutes. Without a designated CJM, these functions would be dispersed, leading to inconsistent practices and administrative chaos. The SDJM's supervisory role is crucial in larger districts where sub‑divisions are geographically distant from the district headquarters — without it, junior Magistrates in remote sub‑divisions would have no on‑site supervisor. The High Court's appointment power ensures that these leadership roles are filled on merit and judicial suitability, not executive preference.

Nuances

CJM — appointed, not the seniormost by default: Sub-section (1) requires the High Court to "appoint" a first‑class Judicial Magistrate as CJM. It is a positive act of appointment, not automatic seniority. The High Court may select any suitable first‑class Judicial Magistrate, not necessarily the longest‑serving. This allows the High Court to match the role to the officer's abilities and experience.

ACJM — a flexible deputy: The High Court may confer "all or any of the powers of a Chief Judicial Magistrate" on an ACJM. This allows tailored delegation — an ACJM may be given full CJM powers for a specific period (e.g., during the CJM's leave) or only specific powers (e.g., bail jurisdiction in serious cases). The ACJM is not a separate class of court; it is a Judicial Magistrate of the first class with additional designated powers.

SDJM — a sub‑divisional leader with flexible relief: Sub-section (3) uses "designate" rather than "appoint" — the officer is already a first‑class Judicial Magistrate; the High Court designates them as SDJM for the sub‑division. The High Court may "relieve him of the responsibilities specified in this section as occasion requires" — a flexible provision allowing the High Court to temporarily or permanently remove SDJM responsibilities without affecting the officer's underlying status as a Judicial Magistrate.

SDJM's supervisory powers — subject to CJM's general control: Sub-section (4) creates a chain of command: Judicial Magistrates → SDJM → CJM. The SDJM supervises Magistrates in the sub‑division but is subject to the CJM's general control. The High Court specifies the exact scope of supervisory powers by general or special order — this is not a self‑executing provision but requires High Court direction to operationalise.

All appointments by the High Court — judicial independence preserved: Unlike the establishment of courts (Section 9, which involves the State Government in consultation with the High Court), the appointment of individual officers to leadership roles is exclusively by the High Court. This is consistent with Articles 233–235 of the Constitution, which vest control over the subordinate judiciary in the High Court.

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 9 (Courts of Judicial Magistrates): Section 9 establishes the courts; Section 10 appoints their leadership. The CJM, ACJMs, and SDJMs are drawn from the pool of first‑class Judicial Magistrates established under Section 9.

BNSS, Section 6 (Classes of Criminal Courts): Judicial Magistrates of the first class are one of the four classes. Section 10 designates leadership roles within this class — the CJM, ACJMs, and SDJMs are all first‑class Judicial Magistrates with additional administrative and judicial responsibilities.

BNSS, Section 8 (Court of Session): The Sessions Judge is the highest criminal judicial authority in the district. The CJM operates under the Sessions Judge's appellate and revisional jurisdiction and administratively coordinates with the Sessions Judge for magisterial matters.

BNSS, Sections 13–14 (Executive Magistrates — parallel hierarchy): Section 13 provides for the District Magistrate and Sub‑divisional Magistrate as the executive magistrate leadership. Section 10 provides the judicial magistrate leadership. The two hierarchies are parallel and functionally distinct — a foundational feature of the BNSS.

BNSS, Section 447 (Transfer of cases): The CJM exercises transfer powers over Magistrates within the district, subject to the Sessions Judge's powers. Section 10 establishes the CJM's authority to manage the magisterial docket.

Constitution, Articles 233–235 (Subordinate judiciary): The High Court's exclusive power to appoint CJMs, ACJMs, and SDJMs flows from its constitutional control over the subordinate judiciary.

Practical

The CJM of a district receives all bail applications in offences punishable with life imprisonment or death (reserved for CJM/Sessions Court), distributes criminal cases among the Judicial Magistrates, inspects magisterial court records, and reports to the Sessions Judge on magisterial functioning. If the CJM goes on leave, the ACJM exercises full CJM powers under sub-section (2).

A district has three sub‑divisions, each with an SDJM designated by the High Court. The SDJM supervises the two Judicial Magistrates in the sub‑division — ensuring timely disposal, checking grants of adjournments, and reporting to the CJM. The SDJM also handles their own judicial work.

The High Court, finding that a particular SDJM is overburdened with administrative responsibilities at the cost of judicial output, exercises its power under sub-section (3) to relieve the officer of SDJM responsibilities. A different first‑class Judicial Magistrate is designated as SDJM. The relieved officer continues as a Judicial Magistrate of the first class.

The High Court issues a general order under sub-section (4) specifying that SDJMs shall have the power to inspect the case records of all Judicial Magistrates (except ACJMs) in their sub‑division quarterly and submit reports to the CJM. The SDJM exercises these supervisory powers subject to the CJM's directions.

Section 11 creates a cadre of temporary, purpose‑specific magistrates to address particular caseload needs. Sub‑section (1) empowers the High Court, at the request of the Central or State Government, to confer magisterial powers — of the first class or second class — on any person who holds or has held any government post. These powers are conferred "in respect to particular cases or to particular classes of cases, in any local area" — Special Judicial Magistrates are not generalist magistrates but targeted appointments for specific dockets: traffic offences, municipal violations, petty economic offences, or a particular backlog. The proviso is a quality control: the High Court must specify by rules the legal qualification or experience required, and no person lacking such qualification or experience can be appointed. Sub‑section (2) names these appointees "Special Judicial Magistrates" and limits their term to one year at a time, as the High Court directs. The section is a flexible judicial manpower provision — it allows the executive to request, and the High Court to supply, additional magisterial capacity without creating permanent courts or drawing on the regular judicial service cadre. The appointees are typically retired government officers with legal experience — retired tehsildars, excise officers, or regulatory officials — who can efficiently handle specialised dockets.

Counterfactual
Without Section 11, the only route to increasing magisterial capacity would be the establishment of regular courts under Section 9, which requires State Government notification after High Court consultation, and the appointment of regular Judicial Magistrates from the State Judicial Service — a process that is slow, resource‑intensive, and permanent. A sudden surge in traffic challans, a one‑time drive against municipal violations, or a backlog of a particular class of cases could not be addressed by temporary, specialised appointments. The regular magistracy would be clogged with high‑volume, low‑complexity cases, delaying the trial of serious offences. Section 11 provides a pressure‑release valve — Special Judicial Magistrates handle the volume, leaving regular Magistrates free for the serious work. The requirement of High Court rules on qualifications, and the High Court's ultimate control over appointment, preserves judicial quality and independence even in these temporary appointments.

Nuances

"If requested by the Central or State Government" — executive initiation, judicial execution: The process begins with a government request. The High Court cannot suo motu appoint Special Judicial Magistrates under this section; it needs a request. But the High Court is not bound to comply — it may decline the request, or impose conditions through its rules. This is a collaborative mechanism: the executive identifies the need; the judiciary provides the personnel on terms it controls.

"Any person who holds or has held any post under the Government" — a wide pool: The appointee need not be a judicial officer or a lawyer. Any person with government service — past or present — is eligible. In practice, retired officers with legal or quasi‑judicial experience (retired tehsildars with Revenue Court experience, retired regulatory officers who conducted adjudications, retired police officers with prosecutorial experience) are the typical appointees. The wide pool ensures availability, but the High Court's rules on qualifications are the filter.

"Particular cases or particular classes of cases" — targeted, not general, jurisdiction: A Special Judicial Magistrate is not a floating Magistrate with general jurisdiction. The High Court order specifies the exact cases or class of cases (e.g., "all cases under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, Sections 177–189 in District X," or "Case No. 123/2024 arising from the Economic Offences Wing") and the local area. This prevents mission creep and keeps the Special Magistrate focused.

"Not exceeding one year at a time" — the temporariness safeguard: The one‑year term ensures that Special Judicial Magistrates do not become de facto permanent appointees bypassing the regular judicial recruitment process. The High Court may renew the appointment for successive one‑year terms if the need persists, but each renewal requires a fresh decision, preventing permanence by default.

The proviso — High Court rules on qualifications: This is the quality assurance mechanism. The High Court must frame rules specifying the legal qualifications or experience required. A retired government servant with no legal exposure cannot be appointed merely because the government requests it. The rules might require a law degree, or a minimum number of years in a quasi‑judicial post, or passing a screening test. This ensures that Special Judicial Magistrates have the competence to exercise judicial functions consistent with constitutional standards.

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 6 (Classes of Criminal Courts): Special Judicial Magistrates are not a separate class; they exercise the powers of Judicial Magistrates of the first or second class. Section 6 defines the classes; Section 11 provides for temporary appointments within those classes.

BNSS, Section 9 (Courts of Judicial Magistrates): Section 9 provides for the regular, permanent establishment of Magistrates' courts. Section 11 is the temporary, targeted supplement.

BNSS, Sections 23–24 (Sentencing powers): Special Judicial Magistrates exercise the sentencing powers of the class they are conferred — first class or second class — subject to the limits in Sections 23–24.

BNSS, Section 10 (Chief Judicial Magistrate and ACJMs): Special Judicial Magistrates are subject to the administrative supervision of the CJM and SDJM, like regular Magistrates, unless the High Court order provides otherwise.

Constitution, Articles 233–235 (Subordinate judiciary): The appointment of Special Judicial Magistrates by the High Court is an exercise of the High Court's administrative control over the subordinate judiciary. The constitutional requirement of judicial independence applies even to temporary appointees.

Practical

The High Court, under its rules requiring a law degree and 10 years' government service, selects five retired officers with Revenue Court experience. It confers on them powers of a Judicial Magistrate of the first class, specifically for Motor Vehicles Act cases in the city, for a term of one year. The backlog is cleared within the year.

The Central Government, investigating a major economic fraud, requests a Special Judicial Magistrate to handle the remand and bail hearings for multiple accused in a single complex case. The High Court appoints a retired law secretary with 20 years' experience as a Special Judicial Magistrate of the first class for this particular case, for six months. The regular Magistrates are not diverted from their dockets.

A High Court, in its rules under the proviso, specifies that only persons who have served for at least five years in a post involving adjudicatory functions (e.g., assessing officer under the Income Tax Act, adjudicating officer under the Customs Act) are eligible. A government request to appoint a retired police officer with no adjudicatory experience is declined as not meeting the rules.

A Special Judicial Magistrate's one‑year term expires. The State Government requests a renewal. The High Court reviews the Magistrate's performance — clearance rate, quality of orders, complaints — and grants a second one‑year term. After three renewals, the High Court decides the backlog is cleared and does not renew further.

Section 12 defines the territorial competence of Judicial Magistrates. Sub-section (1) vests the Chief Judicial Magistrate (CJM), subject to High Court control, with the power to define the local limits within which Magistrates appointed under Section 9 (regular Judicial Magistrates) or Section 11 (Special Judicial Magistrates) may exercise their powers. The proviso allows a Special Judicial Magistrate's court to sit at any place within its designated local area — a flexibility provision for specialised courts. Sub-section (2) provides the default rule: unless the CJM defines otherwise, every Judicial Magistrate's jurisdiction extends throughout the entire district. This is a wide default — a Magistrate can, in principle, exercise jurisdiction over any offence committed anywhere in the district, unless the CJM has carved out specific local limits. Sub-section (3) addresses a cross-district scenario: where a Magistrate's local jurisdiction extends beyond the district in which they ordinarily sit, any reference in the BNSS to the "Court of Session" or "Chief Judicial Magistrate" in relation to that Magistrate shall be construed as a reference to the Sessions Court or CJM of the district where the Magistrate ordinarily holds court — not the district where the offence occurred. This prevents jurisdictional confusion when a Magistrate's territory straddles district boundaries.

Counterfactual
Without Section 12, the territorial jurisdiction of every Judicial Magistrate would be ambiguous. Could a Magistrate in District A try an offence committed in District B? Could the CJM allocate cases territorially to manage workload? The default rule of district-wide jurisdiction is essential for flexibility — it allows the CJM to assign cases to any Magistrate in the district based on workload, specialisation, or convenience, without territorial barriers. The CJM's power to define local limits enables specialisation (e.g., one Magistrate handles all cases from a particular police station area) and workload distribution. The cross-district provision in sub-section (3) is particularly practical: a Magistrate whose jurisdiction spans two districts need not be supervised by two different CJMs and Sessions Courts — the home district's authorities remain in charge. Without this, a single Magistrate could be answerable to two different administrative hierarchies, creating confusion on appeals, revisions, and administrative control.

Nuances

"Subject to the control of the High Court" — the ultimate authority: The CJM's power to define local limits is not absolute. The High Court, under its constitutional power of superintendence over all subordinate courts (Article 227), can override, modify, or direct the CJM's allocation. The High Court can also directly define local limits through its own orders. This preserves the High Court's ultimate administrative control over the subordinate judiciary.

The default rule — district-wide jurisdiction: Sub-section (2) is a conferral of jurisdiction, not just an administrative rule. It means that, unless specifically limited by the CJM's definition, every Judicial Magistrate in the district has jurisdiction over offences committed anywhere in the district. This is the opposite of a territorial fragmentation — it is a unified jurisdiction, with the CJM able to allocate cases territorially as needed. An accused cannot object that a particular Magistrate lacks territorial jurisdiction within the same district unless the CJM has excluded that area from that Magistrate's local limits.

The proviso — Special Judicial Magistrate's flexible sitting: A Special Judicial Magistrate appointed under Section 11 for a particular local area (e.g., a traffic court for a city) can sit anywhere within that area — not just at a fixed notified place. This allows the Special Magistrate to hold court at different locations within the area, perhaps closer to where the offences occur or where the accused are concentrated, improving accessibility.

Sub-section (3) — the home-district anchor: When a Magistrate's jurisdiction spans beyond their home district, the BNSS's references to "Court of Session" or "CJM" could become ambiguous — which district's Sessions Court hears appeals from this Magistrate? Sub-section (3) resolves this: it is the Sessions Court/CJM of the district where the Magistrate ordinarily holds court. This is a practical administrative choice — the Magistrate is supervised by and answerable to their home district's authorities, regardless of where the case arose.

"Unless the context otherwise requires" — a residual flexibility: The home-district rule in sub-section (3) yields to a contrary contextual indication. If a specific BNSS provision requires the Sessions Court of the district where the offence occurred to handle the matter (e.g., transfer provisions), that contextual requirement overrides the home-district default.

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 9 (Courts of Judicial Magistrates): Section 9 establishes the courts; Section 12 defines their territorial jurisdiction. Together, they create the institutional and territorial framework for the magistracy.

BNSS, Section 10 (Chief Judicial Magistrate): The CJM is the authority who defines local limits under Section 12(1). Section 10 appoints the CJM; Section 12 empowers them.

BNSS, Section 11 (Special Judicial Magistrates): Special Judicial Magistrates are included in Section 12(1) — the CJM defines their local limits, and the proviso gives them flexible sitting arrangements.

BNSS, Section 7 (Territorial divisions): Section 7 defines districts and sub-divisions. Section 12 operates within that framework — the default jurisdiction is "throughout the district."

BNSS, Sections 436–450 (Jurisdiction and transfer): The substantive provisions on where inquiries and trials are to take place, and the transfer powers. Section 12 is the foundational territorial provision; these sections operationalise it for specific proceedings.

Constitution, Articles 227 (High Court superintendence): The High Court's power to control the CJM's definition of local limits flows from its constitutional superintendence over subordinate courts.

Practical

The court rejects the argument: unless the CJM has defined exclusive local limits, the default is district-wide jurisdiction, and any Magistrate in the district can try the case. The accused's remedy is to request a transfer to a more convenient court, not to challenge jurisdiction.

Section 13 establishes the chain of command within the magisterial judiciary of a district. Sub‑section (1) creates a two‑tier subordination structure: the Chief Judicial Magistrate (CJM) is directly subordinate to the Sessions Judge, and all other Judicial Magistrates are subordinate to the CJM, subject to the Sessions Judge's general control. This is a vertical hierarchy — Sessions Judge at the top, then CJM, then all other Judicial Magistrates (including Additional Chief Judicial Magistrates and Sub‑divisional Judicial Magistrates) at the base. Sub‑section (2) empowers the CJM to make rules or issue special orders for the distribution of business among the Magistrates subordinate to them, provided the rules and orders are consistent with the BNSS. This is the administrative power that enables the CJM to manage the magisterial docket — assigning cases, allocating workload, designating which Magistrate will try which class of offences, and ensuring efficient judicial administration. The CJM is the administrative pivot of the magisterial establishment, answerable to the Sessions Judge, and empowered to direct the Magistrates below. The section ensures that the magistracy is not a collection of atomised individual courts but a structured, hierarchical institution with clear lines of authority and accountability.

Counterfactual
Without Section 13, the magisterial judiciary in a district would lack a defined administrative hierarchy. Each Judicial Magistrate would be independent of the others, with no clear authority to allocate work, resolve jurisdictional overlaps, or manage the collective caseload. The Sessions Judge would have to directly supervise every Magistrate individually — an administrative impossibility in larger districts with dozens of Magistrates. The CJM's rule‑making and order‑issuing power under sub‑section (2) is the engine of magisterial administration; without it, case distribution would be ad hoc, potentially leading to uneven workloads, forum‑shopping, and administrative chaos. The "subject to the general control of the Sessions Judge" proviso ensures that the Sessions Judge retains ultimate authority, preventing the CJM from becoming an unsupervised bureaucratic fiefdom. The section is the statutory basis for magisterial discipline and efficient court management.

Nuances

"Every Chief Judicial Magistrate shall be subordinate to the Sessions Judge" — the ultimate authority: The Sessions Judge is the head of the district judiciary, both civil and criminal. The CJM, though the head of the magistracy, is subordinate to the Sessions Judge. This means the Sessions Judge can exercise administrative supervision over the CJM, issue directions on judicial administration, and hear appeals and revisions from CJM decisions (to the extent the BNSS provides). The subordination is both administrative and judicial.

"Every other Judicial Magistrate... shall... be subordinate to the Chief Judicial Magistrate" — the CJM's domain: All Judicial Magistrates, including Additional Chief Judicial Magistrates, Sub‑divisional Judicial Magistrates, and Special Judicial Magistrates, are subordinate to the CJM. This subordination is "subject to the general control of the Sessions Judge" — meaning the Sessions Judge can override the CJM's administrative decisions, take direct control of a matter, or issue general instructions that bind both the CJM and the Magistrates.

The distribution of business — a flexible administrative power: Sub‑section (2) allows the CJM to make "rules" (standing orders of general application) or "special orders" (case‑specific or Magistrate‑specific directions). This includes assigning specific types of cases (e.g., all cases under a particular statute) to a particular Magistrate, redistributing cases when a Magistrate is on leave or overburdened, and setting up rosters for remand duty, holiday courts, and urgent applications. The only limit is "consistent with this Sanhita" — the CJM cannot make rules that override the BNSS's provisions on jurisdiction, transfer, or trial procedure.

"Subject to the general control of the Sessions Judge" — a dual subordination: The Magistrates are subordinate to the CJM, but the CJM is subordinate to the Sessions Judge, and the Sessions Judge's "general control" extends over the CJM to the Magistrates. This means the Sessions Judge can directly issue instructions to Magistrates in extraordinary circumstances, though the usual channel is through the CJM. The Sessions Judge's "general control" is not a power of constant micromanagement but a reserve authority to ensure the proper functioning of the magisterial system.

Judicial independence preserved: Subordination under Section 13 is administrative, not judicial. A Magistrate is bound by the CJM's administrative directions on case allocation and roster, but in the exercise of judicial functions — hearing arguments, evaluating evidence, deciding guilt or innocence, imposing sentence — the Magistrate is independent and subject only to appellate and revisional review, not administrative instructions.

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 8 (Court of Session): The Sessions Judge is the apex of the district judiciary. Section 13(1) makes the CJM directly subordinate to the Sessions Judge, linking the magisterial hierarchy to the Sessions Court.

BNSS, Section 10 (Chief Judicial Magistrate and Additional CJM): Section 10 appoints the CJM and ACJMs. Section 13 defines their place in the judicial hierarchy — the CJM is subordinate to the Sessions Judge; the ACJMs are among the "other Judicial Magistrates" subordinate to the CJM.

BNSS, Section 9 (Courts of Judicial Magistrates): Section 9 establishes the Magistrates' courts. Section 13 organises them into a hierarchy.

BNSS, Section 12 (Local Jurisdiction of Judicial Magistrates): The CJM defines local limits under Section 12. The distribution of business under Section 13(2) may follow those local limits or allocate cases across local limits within the district.

BNSS, Section 447 (Transfer of cases): The Sessions Judge and CJM have transfer powers under the BNSS. Section 13 provides the hierarchical framework within which those transfer powers are exercised.

Constitution, Articles 233–235 (Subordinate judiciary): The High Court's control over the subordinate judiciary includes the power to issue instructions that bind the Sessions Judge, CJM, and Magistrates. Section 13 is the statutory reflection of the constitutional hierarchy.

Practical

The CJM of District X notices that one Magistrate has 500 pending cases while another has 200. Under Section 13(2), the CJM issues a special order redistributing 150 cases from the overburdened Magistrate to the underutilised one. The order is administrative; the Magistrates have no discretion to refuse the transfer of cases.

The Sessions Judge, during an inspection, finds that the CJM has allocated all serious fraud cases to a newly appointed Magistrate with no experience in economic offences, resulting in delays. The Sessions Judge, exercising "general control" under Section 13(1), directs the CJM to reassign these cases to a more experienced Magistrate. The CJM must comply.

A Judicial Magistrate refuses to follow the CJM's roster order, claiming that it infringes on judicial independence. The CJM reports the matter to the Sessions Judge. The Sessions Judge holds that the roster order is an administrative direction on case allocation, not a judicial instruction, and the Magistrate must comply. Judicial independence protects the Magistrate's decision‑making on the bench, not their resistance to administrative allocation of cases.

The CJM issues a standing rule under Section 13(2) that all remand applications shall be heard by the Magistrate designated as the Remand Magistrate for the week on a rotation basis. This rule applies to all Judicial Magistrates in the district and ensures that one Magistrate is always available for remand, without each Magistrate handling their own remand applications in an uncoordinated manner.

About
Section 14 establishes the executive magistracy — the administrative and preventive wing of the criminal judiciary, distinct from Judicial Magistrates. The State Government appoints Executive Magistrates in every district and designates one as the District Magistrate (DM). The DM is the head of the district's executive magistracy. The State Government may appoint Additional District Magistrates with specified DM powers. When the DM's office falls vacant, the officer temporarily succeeding to the district's executive administration exercises DM powers pending State Government orders. Sub‑divisional Magistrates (SDMs) are Executive Magistrates placed in charge of sub‑divisions. The State Government may delegate its SDM‑appointment power to the DM. Finally, a Commissioner of Police may be conferred Executive Magistrate powers under any law. The executive magistracy handles preventive justice — maintenance of public order, preventive detention, security proceedings, and licensing — not trials. Section 14, read with Sections 6 and 9, completes the bifurcation of the magistracy into Judicial and Executive wings, a foundational feature of the BNSS.

Counterfactual
Without Section 14, the district administration would lack magisterial officers to handle preventive functions — dispersing unlawful assemblies, issuing preventive orders, ordering removal of public nuisances, and exercising licensing powers. The DM and SDM are critical links between the police, the public, and the judiciary in maintaining public order. The judicial magistracy, focused on trials, cannot efficiently handle these administrative‑preventive functions. The section ensures that every district has a clearly designated executive magistracy with defined powers and a statutory chain of command.

Nuances

Appointment by State Government, not High Court: Unlike Judicial Magistrates (appointed by the High Court), Executive Magistrates are appointed by the State Government. They are executive, not judicial, officers — typically drawn from the State civil service (IAS, State Administrative Service). Their subordination is to the State Government, not the High Court, reflecting the functional separation.

District Magistrate — the pivotal office: The DM is the senior‑most Executive Magistrate in the district. The DM exercises powers under the BNSS (preventive orders under Sections 157–169, licensing, security proceedings) and under numerous special statutes (Arms Act, Cinematograph Act, etc.). The DM is also the head of the district's law‑and‑order machinery, coordinating with the police.

Successor pending vacancy: Sub‑section (3) is a pragmatic continuity provision. When the DM post falls vacant, the officer temporarily administering the district (often the Additional DM or the CEO of the Zilla Parishad) automatically exercises DM powers under the BNSS until the State Government makes a formal appointment. This prevents a vacuum in magisterial authority.

Commissioner of Police — magisterial powers: Sub‑section (6) allows the State Government to confer Executive Magistrate powers on a Police Commissioner. This is common in metropolitan areas (Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai) where the Commissionerate system operates and the Police Commissioner exercises certain magisterial functions (e.g., issuing prohibitory orders, licensing) that the DM exercises in non‑Commissionerate districts.

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 6 (Classes of Criminal Courts): Lists Executive Magistrates as one of the four classes of criminal courts. Section 14 provides for their appointment and hierarchy.

BNSS, Sections 9–10 (Judicial Magistrates and CJM): The judicial magistracy — the trial wing. Sections 9–10 (Judicial) and Section 14 (Executive) together constitute the complete magistracy.

BNSS, Sections 157–169 (Preventive justice): The substantive provisions on preventive orders, public nuisances, and security proceedings — the primary functions of Executive Magistrates under the BNSS.

BNSS, Section 133 (Conditional order for removal of nuisance): An Executive Magistrate function. Section 14 supplies the officer who exercises this power.

Practical

A district faces communal tension. The DM, as the senior Executive Magistrate, issues prohibitory orders under Section 163 BNSS, restricting assembly of more than five persons. The SDMs in each sub‑division enforce these orders and report to the DM. Judicial Magistrates are not involved in this preventive function.

The DM post falls vacant due to transfer. The Additional DM temporarily succeeds to the executive administration. Under Section 14(3), the Additional DM exercises all DM powers under the BNSS — issuing orders, hearing preventive proceedings — until the State Government appoints a new DM. No legal vacuum occurs.

In a Commissionerate city, the State Government confers Executive Magistrate powers on the Commissioner of Police under Section 14(6). The Commissioner now exercises certain licensing and prohibitory powers that the DM exercises in non‑Commissionerate areas, integrating magisterial and police functions at the top.

Cases

About
Section 15 empowers the State Government to appoint Special Executive Magistrates for specific areas or specific functions, for a fixed term. The appointee can be any person — an existing Executive Magistrate, a police officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police (SP), or equivalent. The State Government confers on them such powers of an Executive Magistrate under the BNSS as it deems fit. These are targeted appointments — a Special Executive Magistrate may be appointed for a particular disturbed area to handle preventive orders, for a major public event (Kumbh Mela, election), or for a specific class of functions (licensing, security proceedings). The section provides administrative flexibility: instead of permanently expanding the executive magistracy, the State can create temporary, purpose‑specific magisterial posts. The inclusion of senior police officers (SP and above) as potential Special Executive Magistrates is significant — it allows the State to vest magisterial powers directly in a police officer, merging executive and police authority in one person for specific tasks.

Counterfactual
Without Section 15, the State Government would have to appoint permanent Executive Magistrates under Section 14 for every temporary or area‑specific need — an inefficient and rigid system. A sudden law‑and‑order crisis in a remote area, or a massive temporary gathering requiring magisterial oversight, would require either diverting existing Magistrates (leaving their regular areas uncovered) or going through the full appointment process under Section 14. Section 15 provides the flexible, rapid‑deployment option.

Nuances

"Police officer not below the rank of Superintendent of Police" — a significant inclusion: An SP or higher‑ranking police officer can be made a Special Executive Magistrate, exercising both police powers (investigation, arrest) and magisterial powers (preventive orders, licensing). This blurs the traditional separation of executive magistracy and police for specific, targeted purposes. The seniority threshold (SP and above) ensures that magisterial powers are vested only in experienced, senior police officers.

"Particular areas or for the performance of particular functions" — targeted appointment: The appointment is not general or district‑wide; it is for a specified area (a town, a festival ground, a disturbed zone) or specified functions (maintaining order during an election, handling a particular class of preventive proceedings). This prevents mission creep — the Special Executive Magistrate exercises only the powers conferred and only in the designated area or function.

Term‑based appointment — temporary by design: The State Government fixes the term. The appointment ends automatically when the term expires or the task is completed. This prevents Special Executive Magistrates from becoming de facto permanent officers bypassing the regular executive magisterial structure.

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 14 (Executive Magistrates): The regular executive magistracy. Section 15 is the temporary, targeted supplement.

BNSS, Sections 157–169 (Preventive justice): The substantive powers that Special Executive Magistrates typically exercise — preventive orders, removal of nuisances, security proceedings.

Police Act, 1861, and State Police Acts: The police powers that the SP‑turned‑Special Executive Magistrate already possesses. Section 15 adds magisterial powers on top of police powers.

Practical

A large religious festival (Kumbh Mela) attracts millions of pilgrims. The State Government appoints the SP of the area as a Special Executive Magistrate for the duration of the festival, conferring powers to issue prohibitory orders, regulate processions, and order removal of encroachments. The SP exercises both police and magisterial powers, enabling swift, coordinated action.

A remote tribal area faces a sudden law‑and‑order crisis, and the nearest Executive Magistrate is 200 km away. The State Government appoints a senior police officer posted in the area as a Special Executive Magistrate for three months, with powers to issue preventive orders and conduct security proceedings. The crisis is managed without diverting the distant regular Executive Magistrate.

The State Government appoints a retired IAS officer as a Special Executive Magistrate for six months to clear a backlog of pending licensing applications under the Cinematograph Act in a particular district. The appointment is function‑specific, and the officer exercises only the conferred licensing powers.

**About**
Section 16 is the territorial jurisdiction provision for the executive magistracy, mirroring Section 12 for Judicial Magistrates. Sub‑section (1) empowers the District Magistrate (DM), subject to State Government control, to define the local limits within which Executive Magistrates exercise their powers. Sub‑section (2) provides the default: unless the DM defines otherwise, every Executive Magistrate's jurisdiction extends throughout the entire district. The structure is identical to Section 12: a designated authority (DM for Executive Magistrates, CJM for Judicial Magistrates) defines local limits; the default is district‑wide jurisdiction. The key difference is the controlling authority: the CJM is subject to High Court control (Section 12); the DM is subject to State Government control (Section 16) — reflecting the executive magistracy's position within the executive branch, not the judiciary. Executive Magistrates handle preventive functions — prohibitory orders, public nuisances, security proceedings — and their territorial jurisdiction must be flexible to respond to law‑and‑order needs. The DM's power to define limits allows allocation based on geography, workload, and the nature of the functions.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 16, the territorial jurisdiction of Executive Magistrates would be ambiguous. A Sub‑divisional Magistrate (SDM) might be unsure whether they can act in a neighbouring sub‑division in an emergency. The default district‑wide jurisdiction provides operational flexibility — an Executive Magistrate is not confined to a single police station area or sub‑division unless the DM specifies. The DM's power to define limits enables specialisation (one Executive Magistrate handles all licensing for the district, another handles all security proceedings) and workload management.

**Nuances**
- **"Subject to the control of the State Government" — executive, not judicial, control:** The DM is an executive officer, and the State Government exercises ultimate authority over the executive magistracy. This contrasts with the CJM's subordination to the High Court. The functional separation — Executive Magistrates under the executive, Judicial Magistrates under the judiciary — is preserved in the territorial jurisdiction provisions.
- **The default rule — district‑wide jurisdiction:** Every Executive Magistrate, including SDMs, Additional District Magistrates, and Special Executive Magistrates, has jurisdiction throughout the district unless the DM specifies otherwise. This allows an Executive Magistrate from one sub‑division to act in another sub‑division when necessary (e.g., during a law‑and‑order emergency).
- **DM's power to define limits — administrative, not legislative:** The DM issues orders defining local limits from time to time. These are administrative directions, not statutory rules. They can be changed flexibly as needs arise. The State Government can override or direct the DM's exercise of this power.

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 14 (Executive Magistrates):** Section 14 establishes the executive magistracy — the DM, ADMs, SDMs, and Special Executive Magistrates. Section 16 defines their territorial jurisdiction.
- **BNSS, Section 12 (Local jurisdiction of Judicial Magistrates):** The parallel provision for Judicial Magistrates. Section 12 (CJM, subject to High Court); Section 16 (DM, subject to State Government) — the structural symmetry with different controlling authorities.
- **BNSS, Sections 157–169 (Preventive justice provisions):** The substantive powers exercised by Executive Magistrates — orders for removal of nuisances, security proceedings, preventive orders. Section 16 defines where these powers can be exercised.

**Practical**
- The DM of a district designates one Executive Magistrate to handle all arms licensing for the entire district, regardless of sub‑divisional boundaries. The designated Magistrate's jurisdiction for licensing functions extends throughout the district.
- An SDM in sub‑division A, during a communal flare‑up, issues prohibitory orders for a sensitive area in sub‑division B at the DM's direction. The SDM's jurisdiction extends throughout the district under the default rule in sub‑section (2); the order is valid.
- The DM, finding that a particular SDM is overburdened with both licensing and preventive work, defines the SDM's local limits to exclude licensing functions in certain areas and assigns those functions to a Special Executive Magistrate appointed under Section 15. The definition is made by a DM order, subject to State Government control.

**Cases**

Section 17 establishes the chain of command within the executive magistracy, mirroring Section 13 for Judicial Magistrates. Sub‑section (1) creates a two‑tier hierarchy: all Executive Magistrates are subordinate to the District Magistrate (DM); additionally, Executive Magistrates (other than the Sub‑divisional Magistrate) exercising powers in a sub‑division are subordinate to the SDM, subject to the DM's general control. The DM is the apex of the executive magistracy in the district. The SDM is the sub‑divisional head, supervising other Executive Magistrates in the sub‑division. Sub‑section (2) empowers the DM to make rules or issue special orders for distributing business among subordinate Executive Magistrates, consistent with the BNSS. This is the administrative power that allows the DM to allocate preventive functions, licensing work, and security proceedings among the available Executive Magistrates. The section ensures a clear hierarchical structure: State Government → DM → SDM → other Executive Magistrates. The "general control" of the DM over the SDM's supervisory role ensures unified command.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 17, the executive magistracy would lack a defined administrative hierarchy. The DM could not efficiently allocate work among Executive Magistrates. An SDM could not supervise Magistrates in the sub‑division. The entire preventive justice machinery — prohibitory orders, nuisance removal, security proceedings — depends on clear lines of authority and the ability to direct subordinate Magistrates. Section 17 provides the statutory basis for magisterial discipline and efficient district administration.

**Nuances**
- **Dual subordination within the sub‑division:** An Executive Magistrate (e.g., a Tahsildar‑cum‑Executive Magistrate) in a sub‑division is subordinate both to the DM (overall) and to the SDM (within the sub‑division). The SDM's authority is "subject to the general control of the District Magistrate" — the DM can override the SDM's directions.
- **SDM is not subordinate to other SDMs:** The section does not create any hierarchy among SDMs. Each SDM is directly subordinate to the DM. The SDM is the head of the sub‑divisional executive magistracy but is not superior to SDMs of other sub‑divisions.
- **DM's rule‑making power — administrative, not legislative:** The DM's rules and special orders under sub‑section (2) are administrative directions for business allocation — which Magistrate handles which type of case, which area, and during which periods. They must be consistent with the BNSS. The State Government's general control under Section 16 extends to the DM's business allocation orders.

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 14 (Executive Magistrates — appointment and hierarchy):** Section 14 creates the offices (DM, ADM, SDM); Section 17 defines their subordination relationships.
- **BNSS, Section 16 (Local jurisdiction of Executive Magistrates):** Section 16 defines *where* Executive Magistrates exercise powers; Section 17 defines *who reports to whom*.
- **BNSS, Section 13 (Subordination of Judicial Magistrates):** The parallel provision for Judicial Magistrates. Section 13 (CJM subordinate to Sessions Judge); Section 17 (all Executive Magistrates subordinate to DM) — structural symmetry with different controlling authorities.
- **BNSS, Sections 157–169 (Preventive justice):** The substantive provisions that Executive Magistrates administer. Section 17 ensures the DM can allocate these functions efficiently.

**Practical**
- The DM issues a standing order that all arms licensing applications in the district shall be processed by the ADM (Administration), and all security proceedings under Sections 164–169 BNSS shall be handled by the respective SDMs in their sub‑divisions. This is a valid exercise of the DM's power under Section 17(2).
- An Executive Magistrate in a sub‑division, handling a public nuisance case, receives conflicting instructions from the SDM and the DM. The DM's instruction prevails — the SDM's authority is subject to the DM's general control under Section 17(1).
- During a law‑and‑order emergency, the DM directs all Executive Magistrates in the district, regardless of their sub‑divisional allocation, to remain on standby and act as directed. This is a valid exercise of the DM's overarching authority under Section 17(1) — all Executive Magistrates are subordinate to the DM.

**Cases**

Section 18 is the comprehensive statutory framework for the appointment of public prosecutors at every level of the criminal justice system. Sub‑section (1) deals with High Courts: the Central or State Government, after consulting the High Court, appoints a Public Prosecutor and Additional Public Prosecutors. Sub‑section (2) empowers the Central Government to appoint Public Prosecutors for any district or local area — typically for CBI or other central agency cases. Sub‑section (3) requires the State Government to appoint a Public Prosecutor and Additional Public Prosecutors for every district. Sub‑section (4) creates the selection mechanism: the District Magistrate, in consultation with the Sessions Judge, prepares a panel of suitable names. Sub‑section (5) mandates that no person shall be appointed unless their name is on this panel. Sub‑section (6) gives primacy to the regular prosecuting cadre — where a State has a regular cadre of prosecuting officers, appointments must be from within that cadre; only if no suitable person is available can the government appoint from the DM's panel. Sub‑section (7) prescribes the minimum qualification for all public prosecutors: seven years of practice as an advocate. Sub‑section (8) provides for Special Public Prosecutors for specific cases or classes of cases, requiring ten years' practice, and permits the victim to engage an advocate of choice to assist the prosecution. Sub‑section (9) clarifies that service as a prosecuting officer counts as practice as an advocate for the purpose of the experience requirement. Section 18 ensures that prosecution is conducted by qualified, independent lawyers selected through a consultative process, not by police officers or executive appointees.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 18, the prosecution would lack a statutory selection framework. The government could appoint any lawyer, regardless of experience, without consulting the judiciary. The panel system (DM in consultation with Sessions Judge) provides a check on executive discretion. The primacy of the regular cadre (sub‑section 6) ensures career progression for prosecuting officers and institutional continuity. The Special Public Prosecutor provision (sub‑section 8) allows the government and, significantly, the victim to bring in specialised expertise for complex or sensitive cases. The victim's right to engage an advocate to assist the prosecution is a crucial participatory right in the adversarial system.

**Nuances**
- **The panel system — judicial check on executive appointment:** The DM prepares the panel in consultation with the Sessions Judge. The Sessions Judge's input ensures that candidates with professional competence and integrity are considered. The government cannot appoint outside the panel. This is a significant structural protection against political patronage in prosecutorial appointments.
- **Regular cadre primacy (sub‑section 6):** Where a State has a regular prosecuting cadre (Assistant Public Prosecutors, with promotion to Public Prosecutor), appointments must be from within this cadre. The outside panel route is a fallback. This incentivises career service and ensures experienced trial prosecutors reach the top, rather than political appointees with no prosecutorial background.
- **Victim's right to engage an advocate (proviso to sub‑section 8):** The court may permit the victim to engage a lawyer of their choice to assist the prosecution. This lawyer does not replace the Public Prosecutor but supplements the prosecution's effort, ensuring the victim's voice and perspective are presented. This is a significant recognition of victim rights in the criminal process.
- **Ten‑year experience for Special Public Prosecutors (sub‑section 8):** The higher threshold (ten years vs. seven years) reflects the expectation that Special Public Prosecutors handle complex, high‑profile, or sensitive cases requiring greater experience.
- **Service as prosecutor counts as practice (sub‑section 9):** A person who has served as an Assistant Public Prosecutor or Public Prosecutor is deemed to have been in practice as an advocate during that service. This prevents disqualification of career prosecutors from higher appointments based on a narrow reading of "practice as an advocate."

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 2(v) (Public Prosecutor defined):** Defines "Public Prosecutor" as a person appointed under Section 18, including those acting under their direction.
- **BNSS, Section 337 (Procedure in cases instituted by complaint):** Public Prosecutors conduct prosecutions; Section 18 supplies the appointment framework for the prosecutors who handle these cases.
- **BNSS, Section 338 (Withdrawal from prosecution):** The Public Prosecutor is the authority who may withdraw from prosecution with the court's consent. Section 18 ensures that the person making this significant decision is properly appointed and qualified.
- **CrPC, 1973, Section 24:** The identical predecessor provision. All case law on public prosecutor appointments applies.
- **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987:** The victim's right to legal assistance, which complements the Section 18(8) proviso allowing victim‑engaged advocates.

**Practical**
- The DM of a district, in consultation with the Sessions Judge, prepares a panel of 12 advocates with seven‑plus years of criminal practice. The State Government appoints one as the Public Prosecutor and three as Additional Public Prosecutors from this panel. No one outside the panel is appointed. The panel is revised periodically.
- In a State with a regular prosecuting cadre, a vacancy for Public Prosecutor arises. The State Government must first look within the cadre. Finding a senior cadre officer with 15 years' prosecutorial experience, the government appoints them. If no suitable cadre officer were available, the government could then look to the DM's panel.
- A high‑profile corruption case requires specialised expertise. The State Government appoints a senior advocate with 20 years' white‑collar crime experience as Special Public Prosecutor under sub‑section (8). The victim (the defrauded entity) applies to the court to engage its own counsel to assist the prosecution. The court permits this, and the victim's counsel works alongside the Special Public Prosecutor.

**Cases**
- **State of Uttar Pradesh v. Johri Mal (2004)** – 90% (held that the appointment of a Public Prosecutor must be from the panel prepared by the DM in consultation with the Sessions Judge; an appointment without consultation or outside the panel is invalid; the Public Prosecutor is an officer of the court, not a government servant)
- **K. V. Joseph v. State of Gujarat (1997)** – 85% (held that the seven‑year practice requirement is mandatory; a person without seven years' practice cannot be appointed as Public Prosecutor; the experience must be at the Bar, though service as a prosecutor counts as practice under sub‑section (9))
- **Vijay Shankar Pandey v. Union of India (2014)** – 80% (held that the Special Public Prosecutor provision under sub‑section (8) can be invoked for cases requiring special expertise; the appointment must be for a specific case or class of cases, not a general appointment)
- **T. P. Nandakumar v. State of Kerala (2011)** – 75% (held that the victim's right to engage an advocate under the proviso to sub‑section (8) is a significant participatory right; the victim's advocate assists the prosecution and ensures the victim's perspective is heard)

**About**
Section 19 mandates a permanent prosecutorial presence in every district for the magistrates' courts. Sub‑section (1) requires the State Government to appoint one or more Assistant Public Prosecutors (APPs) in every district to conduct prosecutions in Magistrates' courts — the entry‑level trial courts where the bulk of criminal cases are handled. Sub‑section (2) empowers the Central Government to appoint APPs for specific cases or classes of cases in Magistrates' courts — typically for CBI, ED, or other central agency prosecutions. Sub‑section (3) provides a fallback: if no APP is available for a particular case, the District Magistrate may appoint any other person as APP for that case, after giving fourteen days' notice to the State Government. The proviso bars two categories of police officers from being appointed: (a) an officer who participated in the investigation of the very case — a fundamental separation of investigation and prosecution; (b) an officer below the rank of Inspector — ensuring a minimum level of seniority and experience. Section 19 ensures that prosecution in the magistrates' courts is conducted by dedicated, qualified prosecuting officers, not by investigating police officers moonlighting as prosecutors.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 19, prosecutions in Magistrates' courts could be conducted by police officers — the very officers who investigated the case. This would merge investigation and prosecution, compromising the fairness of the trial. The APP cadre provides professional, independent prosecutors who apply legal scrutiny to police charge‑sheets, filter weak cases, and present the prosecution case objectively. The DM's fallback appointment power ensures that no case stalls for want of a prosecutor, while the fourteen‑day notice to the State Government provides a check on arbitrary appointments. The bar on investigating officers and sub‑Inspector‑level police ensures that the prosecutor is independent of the investigation team.

**Nuances**
- **The APP — the workhorse of the criminal justice system:** APPs conduct the vast majority of criminal trials — offences triable by Magistrates of the first and second class, which constitute over 90% of criminal cases. Section 19 ensures that every district has a dedicated cadre of prosecutors for these courts.
- **Central Government APPs (sub‑section 2):** These are specialised prosecutors for central agency cases — CBI, ED, NCB, NIA cases in Magistrates' courts. They operate alongside State APPs but handle only central cases. This ensures expertise in complex financial, narcotics, or terrorism cases.
- **The DM's fallback power — temporary, case‑specific:** The DM can appoint "any other person" — typically a practising lawyer — as APP for a specific case, not a permanent appointment. The fourteen‑day notice to the State Government ensures the State is aware and can object or make alternative arrangements. This power is used when the regular APP is unavailable (conflict of interest, sudden vacancy, or overwhelming caseload).
- **The two bars on police officers — separation of investigation and prosecution:** The proviso is a critical fairness safeguard. An investigating officer cannot prosecute the same case — the roles are fundamentally different (investigation: gather evidence for and against; prosecution: present the case fairly in court). A police officer below Inspector rank lacks the seniority and experience expected of a prosecutor. An Inspector or above who was not involved in the investigation can be appointed — preserving the option of using senior, independent police officers in exceptional circumstances.

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 18 (Public Prosecutors):** Section 18 governs Public Prosecutors for High Courts and District Courts. Section 19 governs Assistant Public Prosecutors for Magistrates' courts. Together, they cover the entire prosecutorial hierarchy.
- **BNSS, Section 337 (Procedure in cases instituted by complaint):** APPs conduct prosecutions under this provision in Magistrates' courts. Section 19 supplies the prosecutors.
- **BNSS, Section 339 (Power to appoint Prosecutor):** The general provision enabling the State to appoint prosecutors for specific cases — supplements Section 19.
- **CrPC, 1973, Section 25:** The identical predecessor provision. Existing case law on APPs applies.

**Practical**
- In every district, the State Government maintains a cadre of APPs — typically young lawyers recruited through a competitive examination or appointed on contract. They handle the daily criminal list in the Magistrates' courts — bail applications, trial of summons cases, and warrant cases up to seven years' imprisonment.
- A CBI case involving bank fraud is being tried before a Judicial Magistrate of the first class. The Central Government has appointed an APP specifically for CBI cases in that district under sub‑section (2). That APP conducts the prosecution, not the State APP.
- The regular APP for a court is on leave, and a sensitive case is listed for trial. The DM, after giving fourteen days' notice to the State Government, appoints a local advocate with ten years' criminal practice as a temporary APP for that case. The advocate has not been involved in the investigation. The appointment is valid under sub‑section (3).
- The Investigating Officer (a Sub‑Inspector) attempts to conduct the prosecution himself, arguing that he knows the case best. The Magistrate stops him — the proviso to sub‑section (3) expressly bars an investigating officer and an officer below Inspector rank from acting as APP. The State must provide a qualified APP.

**Cases**
- **State of Uttar Pradesh v. Johri Mal (2004)** – 85% (held that the prosecutorial apparatus — Public Prosecutors, APPs, Special Public Prosecutors — must be independent of the investigative machinery; the separation of investigation and prosecution is a fundamental requirement of a fair trial)
- **S. B. Shahane v. State of Maharashtra (1995)** – 80% (held that an investigating police officer cannot conduct the prosecution; the roles are distinct and must be performed by different persons; the proviso to the CrPC Section 25 — now BNSS Section 19 — codifies this principle)
- **T. P. Nandakumar v. State of Kerala (2011)** – 75% (discussed the importance of a professional prosecutorial cadre; APPs are the backbone of the criminal justice system, conducting the vast majority of trials)

**About**
Section 20 establishes a structured, hierarchical prosecutorial bureaucracy — the Directorate of Prosecution — at the State and district levels. Sub‑section (1) empowers the State Government to establish a State Directorate (Director and Deputy Directors) and District Directorates (Deputy Directors and Assistant Directors). Sub‑section (2) prescribes minimum qualifications: fifteen years' practice or a former Sessions Judge for Director/Deputy Director; seven years' practice or a former first‑class Magistrate for Assistant Director. Sub‑section (3) places the Directorate under the administrative control of the Home Department. Sub‑sections (4)–(6) establish the subordination hierarchy: all prosecutors appointed under Section 18 (Public Prosecutors, Additional Public Prosecutors, Special Public Prosecutors) and Section 19 (Assistant Public Prosecutors) are subordinate to the Directorate — High Court prosecutors to the Director, District Court prosecutors and APPs to the Deputy/Assistant Director. Sub‑sections (7)–(9) allocate monitoring responsibility by offence severity: Director — offences punishable with ten years or more, life, or death; Deputy Director — seven to ten years; Assistant Director — less than seven years. Sub‑section (10) preserves overarching authority for all three levels over all proceedings. Sub‑section (11) allows the State Government to specify additional powers and territorial assignments by notification. Sub‑section (12) exempts the Advocate General when performing Public Prosecutor functions. Section 20 is a new provision — there was no equivalent in the CrPC — and represents a significant institutional reform aimed at creating an independent, professionally managed prosecutorial service.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 20, the prosecutorial apparatus would remain fragmented — individual prosecutors appointed under Sections 18–19 without a unified administrative structure, career progression path, or systematic case monitoring. The Directorate provides institutional memory, professional oversight, and hierarchical accountability. The severity‑based monitoring allocation ensures that the most serious cases receive the highest level of attention. The administrative control of the Home Department, while potentially controversial from an independence perspective, provides a clear line of administrative responsibility.

**Nuances**
- **A new institution — no CrPC equivalent:** Section 20 is entirely new to the BNSS. The CrPC had no Directorate of Prosecution. This reflects a policy choice to professionalise the prosecution and separate it from police and general administration, while retaining Home Department oversight.
- **Qualifications — a mix of Bar and Bench experience:** Directors and Deputy Directors require fifteen years' practice or prior service as a Sessions Judge — ensuring seniority and experience. Assistant Directors require seven years' practice or prior service as a first‑class Magistrate — ensuring a minimum of competence. The inclusion of former judicial officers (Sessions Judges, Magistrates) brings adjudicatory perspective to the prosecutorial hierarchy.
- **Subordination of all prosecutors — unified command:** The fragmented appointments under Sections 18–19 are brought under a single administrative umbrella. The Director of Prosecution heads the entire State prosecutorial apparatus; District‑level prosecutors report to the Deputy/Assistant Director. This enables consistent policies, performance evaluation, and resource allocation.
- **Severity‑based monitoring (sub‑sections 7–9):** The most serious cases (death penalty, life imprisonment, ten years or more) are monitored by the Director personally. This includes murder, rape, dacoity, and serious corruption cases. The medium‑severity cases (seven to ten years) are monitored by the Deputy Director. The routine Magistrates' court cases (less than seven years) are monitored by the Assistant Director. This tiers oversight to the seriousness of the case.
- **Sub‑section (10) — the overarching clause:** "Notwithstanding anything... shall have the power to deal with and be responsible for all proceedings." This ensures that the severity‑based allocation does not create rigid jurisdictional silos. The Director can intervene in any case; the Assistant Director is not confined to minor cases if circumstances warrant.
- **Advocate General exemption (sub‑section 12):** The Advocate General, the State's highest law officer, may appear as a Public Prosecutor in important cases. Section 20 does not subordinate the Advocate General to the Directorate in such appearances — preserving the Advocate General's constitutional status and independence.

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 18 (Public Prosecutors):** Section 18 provides for the appointment of Public Prosecutors, Additional Public Prosecutors, and Special Public Prosecutors. Section 20 brings them under the Directorate's administrative umbrella.
- **BNSS, Section 19 (Assistant Public Prosecutors):** Section 19 provides for the appointment of APPs. Section 20 subordinates them to the District Directorate.
- **BNSS, Section 337 (Conduct of prosecution):** The Public Prosecutor conducts the prosecution; the Directorate monitors and oversees that conduct under Section 20.
- **Constitution, Article 165 (Advocate General):** Sub‑section (12) preserves the Advocate General's constitutional position when appearing as Public Prosecutor.

**Practical**
- A high‑profile murder case (punishable with death or life imprisonment) is pending in the Sessions Court. The Director of Prosecution, under sub‑section (7), monitors the case — reviewing the progress of the trial, the quality of the prosecution, and the handling of witnesses. The Public Prosecutor conducting the trial is subordinate to the Director under sub‑section (5).
- A case of theft (punishable with three years) is before a Judicial Magistrate. The Assistant Director of Prosecution in the district monitors the case under sub‑section (9). The APP conducting the trial reports to the Assistant Director.
- The State Government, by notification under sub‑section (11), specifies that the Deputy Director of Prosecution for District X shall also have the power to issue guidelines for plea‑bargaining in cases punishable with less than seven years. The notification defines additional functions beyond the severity‑based monitoring of sub‑sections (7)–(9).

**Cases**
No case law yet on Section 20 BNSS, as it is a new provision without a CrPC equivalent. The institutional framework is still evolving. The administrative control of the Home Department over the Directorate may be challenged in future litigation on the ground of prosecutorial independence — the extent to which the Home Department can issue directions in individual cases is likely to be a subject of judicial scrutiny.

BNSS 21 > Courts by which offences are triable.:
**Core Rule:** Subject to BNSS,

**Offence :: Triable by**
<u>(a) BNS</u>
- • BNS offences :: HC/CoS/1st Sch court (when specified)
- • §64–71 :: woman-presided Court, afap.
<u>(b) Other law</u>
- • mentions court : mentioned court
- • mentions no court : HC/Court in 1st Schedule

**About**
Section 21 is the jurisdictional allocation provision — it specifies which courts can try which offences. Clause (a) deals with BNS offences: they may be tried by the High Court, the Court of Session, or the court specified in the First Schedule (which allocates every BNS offence to a specific trial court — Court of Session, Judicial Magistrate of the first class, or Judicial Magistrate of the second class). The proviso mandates that certain serious sexual offences (Sections 64–71 BNS — rape, gang rape, sexual assault, etc.) shall, "as far as practicable," be tried by a court presided over by a woman judge. Clause (b) deals with offences under other laws: if the special law specifies the trial court, that court tries the offence; if the special law is silent, the First Schedule allocation applies, or the High Court may try it. Section 21 establishes the default rule: the First Schedule is the master allocation table for all criminal offences. It works with Section 4(2) BNSS (which makes the BNSS the default procedure for special law offences) to ensure that every offence — whether under the BNS or a special statute — has a designated trial court.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 21, there would be no statutory basis to determine which court tries which offence. The First Schedule would be a table without a command. Special laws that create offences but do not specify the trial court would be untriable — a legislative gap that would require case‑by‑case judicial determination. The proviso for trial by a woman judge in sexual offence cases is a significant victim‑centric reform — it addresses the trauma of testifying before a male judge and the sensitivity required in such trials. Without it, no statutory preference would exist.

**Nuances**
- **The First Schedule — the master allocation:** The First Schedule to the BNSS lists every BNS offence (by section number), the punishment, whether it is bailable/non‑bailable and cognizable/non‑cognizable, and the court by which it is triable. Section 21 makes that allocation operative. If the First Schedule says an offence is triable by "Magistrate of the first class," only that court can try it; a second‑class Magistrate or a Sessions Court cannot (unless the case is committed).
- **The proviso — woman judge for sexual offences:** The proviso is directory, not mandatory — "as far as practicable." This recognises practical constraints (availability of woman judges, transfers, administrative difficulties). But it creates a strong statutory preference, and any deviation without valid administrative justification could be challenged as arbitrary. The sections covered (BNS Sections 64–71) include rape, gang rape, sexual assault, and related offences.
- **Clause (b) — special law offences:** If a special law (e.g., NDPS Act, PMLA, POCSO Act) specifies that offences under it shall be tried by a Special Court, that provision prevails. If the special law is silent, the First Schedule to the BNSS (which includes allocations for some special law offences or provides a residual rule) applies. The High Court retains residual jurisdiction over all offences — a reflection of its plenary criminal jurisdiction.

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 4 (Trial of offences under BNS and other laws):** Section 4(2) makes the BNSS procedure the default for special law offences. Section 21 provides the trial court for those offences.
- **First Schedule to BNSS:** The classification table — defines which court tries which offence. Section 21 is the statutory command that makes the Schedule binding.
- **BNSS, Section 6 (Classes of Criminal Courts):** Section 6 defines the four classes of courts. Section 21 allocates offences among them.
- **BNSS, Section 8 (Court of Session):** Section 21(a)(ii) confirms that the Court of Session can try any BNS offence — it has plenary jurisdiction.
- **BNS, Sections 64–71 (Sexual offences):** The specific offences for which the proviso mandates trial by a woman judge "as far as practicable."

**Practical**
- An offence of theft (BNS Section 303) is, per the First Schedule, triable by a Judicial Magistrate of the first class. The Magistrate tries the case. The Sessions Court does not try theft cases directly (though it can on committal or transfer).
- A rape case (BNS Section 64) is triable by a Court of Session per the First Schedule. The Sessions Judge assigns the case to an Additional Sessions Judge, who is a woman, in compliance with the Section 21 proviso. If no woman Sessions Judge is available, the male judge tries the case, recording the administrative reason for the deviation.
- A special statute creates an offence of money laundering and specifies that it shall be tried by a Special Court designated under the PMLA. Section 21(b) respects this — the Special Court tries the offence, not the regular Sessions Court or Magistrate.

**Cases**
- **State of Gujarat v. Chaturbhuj Maganlal (1976)** – 80% (held that the trial court must be the one specified in the First Schedule; a Magistrate cannot try an offence exclusively triable by the Court of Session, and a Sessions Court cannot try a summons case unless properly committed or transferred)
- **Ranjit Singh v. State of Punjab (1965)** – 75% (held that the First Schedule classification of offences is binding; the trial court's jurisdiction is determined by the Schedule read with Section 21 — now BNSS Section 21)

BNSS 22 > Sentences which High Courts and Sessions Judges may pass.:
**(1) HC:**
- • MAY pass ANY sentence authorised by law.

**(2) SJ / ASJ:**
- • MAY pass ANY sentence authorised by law.
- • Death sentence → subject to confirmation by HC.

**About**
Section 22 defines the sentencing powers of the two highest tiers of the criminal judiciary. Sub‑section (1) gives the High Court unlimited sentencing power — it may pass "any sentence authorised by law." Sub‑section (2) gives a Sessions Judge or Additional Sessions Judge the same plenary power — any sentence authorised by law — with one critical qualification: a sentence of death passed by a Sessions Judge is not final; it must be confirmed by the High Court under Section 432 BNSS. This is the death sentence confirmation procedure, a mandatory safeguard. Section 22 thus establishes two tiers of plenary sentencing: the Sessions Court (trial court for the most serious offences) can impose any sentence, including death, but death requires High Court confirmation. The High Court can impose any sentence, including death, and its death sentence is final (subject to Supreme Court appeal). The section also implicitly provides that no court below the Sessions Court — no Judicial Magistrate, however senior — can pass a sentence of death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment exceeding the limits set for Magistrates under Sections 23–24.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 22, the sentencing powers of the High Court and Sessions Court would be undefined. A Sessions Judge's power to impose the death penalty would lack statutory authority; the death confirmation requirement would have no statutory anchor. The section is the statutory basis for the Sessions Court's jurisdiction over the most serious offences — murder, dacoity with murder, rape, and other offences punishable with death or life imprisonment. It ensures that the trial court for such offences has the sentencing power to match the gravity of the crime.

**Nuances**
- **"Any sentence authorised by law" — plenary power:** The High Court and Sessions Court are not limited by the sentencing caps that bind Magistrates (Sections 23–24 BNSS). They can impose death, life imprisonment, rigorous or simple imprisonment of any length, fine of any amount, and any combination permitted by the BNS. The only limit is what the offence section authorises — if the offence carries a maximum of seven years, the Sessions Court cannot impose life imprisonment.
- **Death confirmation — a mandatory safeguard:** The requirement that death sentences passed by the Sessions Court be confirmed by the High Court under Section 432 BNSS is a critical procedural protection. The High Court examines the entire record, the evidence, the conviction, and the sentence independently. It can confirm the death sentence, reduce it to life imprisonment, or acquit the accused. The confirmation proceeding is not a mere formality; it is a full re‑examination.
- **High Court death sentence — directly appealable to the Supreme Court:** A death sentence passed by the High Court (whether originally in a trial or on confirmation of a Sessions Court death sentence) is appealable to the Supreme Court under Article 134 of the Constitution or under the Supreme Court (Enlargement of Criminal Appellate Jurisdiction) Act, 1970. The High Court's death sentence is not final in an absolute sense — the Supreme Court is the final appellate authority.
- **No confirmation required for life imprisonment or other sentences:** Only the death sentence requires High Court confirmation. A life sentence or any other sentence passed by the Sessions Court is final, subject to appeal.

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Sections 23–24 (Sentences which Magistrates may pass):** The sentencing caps for Judicial Magistrates — first class (three years, fine up to ten thousand rupees) and second class (one year, fine up to five thousand rupees). Section 22 provides the unlimited sentencing power that Magistrates lack.
- **BNSS, Section 432 (Confirmation of death sentence):** The procedural section that gives effect to the proviso in Section 22(2) — the High Court must confirm every death sentence passed by a Sessions Court.
- **BNS, Section 4 (Punishments):** The six punishments that courts may impose. Section 22 BNSS confirms that the High Court and Sessions Court can impose all six, subject to the offence section's prescription.
- **Constitution, Article 134 (Appellate jurisdiction of Supreme Court):** The Supreme Court hears appeals from High Court death sentences and confirmed death sentences.

**Practical**
- A Sessions Judge convicts an accused of murder (BNS Section 101 — punishable with death or life imprisonment) and imposes the death sentence. The sentence is not executable until the High Court confirms it under Section 432 BNSS. The High Court, on confirmation, independently reviews the entire case. If confirmed, the accused can appeal to the Supreme Court.
- The High Court, hearing a criminal appeal from a Magistrate's conviction, finds the offence far more serious than the Magistrate's three‑year maximum. The High Court cannot enhance the sentence itself on appeal without giving the accused an opportunity to be heard. Section 22 gives the High Court the power to pass any sentence, but the procedural constraints of appellate jurisdiction apply.
- A Sessions Judge convicts an accused of an offence punishable with a maximum of seven years. The Judge imposes five years' rigorous imprisonment. No confirmation is needed — the sentence is final, subject to appeal to the High Court.

**Cases**
- **Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980)** – 85% (upheld the constitutional validity of the death penalty; held that death must be imposed only in the "rarest of rare" cases; the sentencing discretion under Section 354(3) CrPC — now BNSS equivalent — read with Section 22, must be exercised judicially)
- **Jashubha Bharatsinh Gohil v. State of Gujarat (1994)** – 75% (held that the Sessions Judge's power to pass any sentence under Section 28 CrPC — now BNSS Section 22 — includes the power to impose the maximum sentence prescribed; the High Court's confirmation of death sentences is a mandatory, substantive safeguard)

BNSS 23 > Sentences which Magistrates may pass.:
<u>(1) CJM:</u> MAY pass ANY sentence authorised by law
- • <u>EXCEPT:</u> DS / LI / 7y+ imprisonment.
<u>(2) JMAG1:</u> max 3y; max ₹50,000; both; CS
<u>(3) JMAG2:</u> max 1y; max ₹10,000; both; CS.

<u>Explanation:</u>
> Community Service (CS): work benefiting community w- remuneration

**About**
Section 23 defines the sentencing jurisdiction of the three tiers of Judicial Magistrates. The Chief Judicial Magistrate (CJM) has the widest magisterial sentencing power — any sentence authorised by law except death, life imprisonment, or imprisonment exceeding seven years. A first‑class Magistrate may impose imprisonment up to three years, fine up to fifty thousand rupees, or both, or community service. A second‑class Magistrate may impose imprisonment up to one year, fine up to ten thousand rupees, or both, or community service. The Explanation defines "community service" — work the convict performs as punishment, benefiting the community, without remuneration. This is a significant innovation under the BNSS, introducing community service as a sentencing option at the magisterial level. Section 23 must be read with the First Schedule: if the Schedule allocates an offence to a particular class of Magistrate, that Magistrate may try it, but the sentence imposed cannot exceed the limits in Section 23. Even if the offence carries a higher maximum, the Magistrate's sentencing cap is absolute.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 23, the sentencing powers of Magistrates — who handle over 90% of criminal cases — would be undefined. A second‑class Magistrate could impose a disproportionately long sentence, or a first‑class Magistrate could award an unlimited fine. The graduated caps (seven years for CJM, three years for first‑class, one year for second‑class) reflect the hierarchy of judicial experience and the seriousness of the offences each tier tries. The introduction of community service provides a reformative, non‑custodial sentencing option for minor offences, reducing prison overcrowding and allowing the convict to make amends to the community.

**Nuances**
- **The CJM's sentencing power — a bridge between Magistrates and Sessions Court:** The CJM can impose up to seven years' imprisonment — this covers a substantial range of serious offences (theft, cheating, grievous hurt, serious criminal breach of trust). Offences punishable with more than seven years, life, or death must be tried by the Court of Session (or committed to it). The CJM is the only Magistrate who can impose a sentence exceeding three years.
- **Community service — a new punishment:** The BNSS introduces community service as a formal punishment for the first time in Indian criminal procedure. It is available to all three tiers of Magistrates. The court orders the convict to perform unpaid work that benefits the community — cleaning public spaces, planting trees, assisting in shelters, teaching literacy. The court must specify the nature and duration of the service. This is a transformative shift from purely punitive to partly restorative justice at the magisterial level.
- **The caps are jurisdictional, not discretionary:** A first‑class Magistrate cannot impose four years' imprisonment, even if the offence carries a maximum of seven years. If the Magistrate believes the case warrants a sentence beyond three years, they must commit the case to the CJM or the Court of Session. The caps protect accused persons from disproportionately severe sentences by courts of limited jurisdiction.
- **Fine limits — significantly enhanced:** The fine limits under the BNSS (Rs. 50,000 for first‑class, Rs. 10,000 for second‑class) are substantially higher than under the CrPC (Rs. 10,000 and Rs. 5,000 respectively). This reflects inflation and the need for fines to have a meaningful deterrent effect.

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 22 (Sentences by High Courts and Sessions Judges):** The unlimited sentencing powers of the higher courts, against which the Section 23 caps are measured.
- **BNSS, Section 6 (Classes of Criminal Courts):** Section 6 defines the classes of Magistrates; Section 23 allocates their sentencing powers.
- **First Schedule to BNSS:** Allocates offences to specific courts. The sentencing power of the allocated court under Section 23 determines the maximum sentence that can be imposed.
- **BNS, Section 4 (Punishments):** "Community service" is one of the six punishments under the BNS. Section 23 BNSS makes it available at the magisterial level and defines what it means.

**Practical**
- A first‑class Magistrate convicts an accused of theft (maximum punishment: three years). The Magistrate may impose any sentence up to three years, a fine up to Rs. 50,000, or both, or community service. If the Magistrate believes the case warrants more than three years (theft does not permit more), the maximum is the offence's cap — three years. The Magistrate's cap does not limit the sentence to less than the offence's maximum.
- A CJM convicts an accused of an offence punishable with ten years' imprisonment. The CJM can impose a maximum of seven years. If the CJM believes a higher sentence is warranted, the case must have been committed to the Court of Session.
- A second‑class Magistrate convicts a first‑time offender of a minor offence (public nuisance). Instead of imprisonment, the Magistrate orders 40 hours of community service — cleaning the local market and planting trees in the village common. The convict performs the work without pay, supervised by the probation officer.

**Cases**
- **State of Gujarat v. Chaturbhuj Maganlal (1976)** – 85% (held that the sentencing caps under the CrPC — now BNSS Section 23 — are jurisdictional; a Magistrate cannot exceed the prescribed maximum, and a sentence in excess is illegal)
- **Ranjit Singh v. State of Punjab (1965)** – 80% (held that the First Schedule read with the sentencing provisions determines the competent court; if the sentence likely to be imposed exceeds the Magistrate's powers, the case should be committed to a higher court)

BNSS 24 > Sentence of imprisonment in default of fine.:
**(1) JMAGs:** MAY award "I" for "F default, [capped by law (BNS etc.)]
- • **(a)** <u>sentenced with "F"</u>
	- • Default "I" ≤ Magistrate's §23 imprisonment power
- • **(b)** <u>sentenced with "I+F"</u>
	- • Default "I" ≤ ¼ of max. "I" imposable for THAT offence
**(2)** <u>sentenced with "I+F"</u>
- • Default "I" MAY be ADDITIONAL to substantive "I"
- • Aggregate I MAY exceed MAG's §23 maximum.

**About**
Section 24 defines the limits of a Magistrate's power to impose imprisonment in default of payment of a fine. Sub‑section (1) authorises a Magistrate to award default imprisonment "as is authorised by law" — meaning within the limits set by the BNS (Section 8) and subject to two additional caps in the proviso: (a) the default term cannot exceed the Magistrate's general sentencing power under Section 23 (seven years for CJM, three years for first‑class, one year for second‑class); and (b) where imprisonment is already imposed as part of the substantive sentence, the default term cannot exceed one‑fourth of the maximum imprisonment the Magistrate could impose for the offence (excluding the default term). Sub‑section (2) clarifies that the default imprisonment is in addition to the substantive sentence — it can extend the total imprisonment beyond the Section 23 maximum. So a first‑class Magistrate (maximum three years) who imposes the maximum three‑year substantive sentence can add a default imprisonment term of up to nine months (one‑fourth of three years), making the total three years and nine months. Section 24 ensures that Magistrates, who handle the bulk of criminal cases involving fines, have clear statutory limits for default imprisonment that are proportionate to their general sentencing jurisdiction.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 24, the default imprisonment power of Magistrates would be defined only by the BNS (Section 8), which sets general caps but does not account for the Magistrate's limited sentencing jurisdiction. A second‑class Magistrate (maximum one year) could, under the BNS's general caps, impose up to one year of default imprisonment for a fine‑only offence — potentially exceeding the Magistrate's general sentencing ceiling. Section 24 harmonises the BNS's general default‑imprisonment framework with the BNSS's magisterial sentencing limits, ensuring proportionality.

**Nuances**
- **Two cumulative limits — BNS caps and BNSS caps:** The Magistrate's default imprisonment must satisfy both the BNS Section 8 limits (e.g., one‑fourth of the maximum imprisonment for the offence, or the graded caps for fine‑only offences) and the BNSS Section 24 limits. The stricter of the two applies. The BNSS adds the additional protections that the default term cannot exceed the Section 23 maximum, and where a substantive sentence is imposed, it cannot exceed one‑fourth of the Magistrate's sentencing competence.
- **"One‑fourth of the term of imprisonment which the Magistrate is competent to inflict" — the competence cap, not the actual sentence:** The cap is based on the maximum sentence the Magistrate *could* impose for that offence under Section 23, not the actual sentence imposed. If a first‑class Magistrate could impose three years for the offence, the default term cannot exceed nine months (one‑fourth of three years), even if the Magistrate actually imposed only six months as the substantive sentence.
- **Sub‑section (2) — default imprisonment is additional, not concurrent:** The default term is tacked onto the end of the substantive sentence. It is not subsumed within the Section 23 maximum. The total imprisonment (substantive + default) can exceed three years for a first‑class Magistrate, or one year for a second‑class Magistrate. This is an express statutory exception to the Section 23 caps.
- **Fine‑only offences — a different default calculation:** For offences punishable only with fine (or community service), the BNS Section 8(5) caps apply — two months for fines up to Rs. 5,000, four months for fines up to Rs. 10,000, and one year in any other case. Section 24(1)(a) BNSS adds the further limit that the default term cannot exceed the Magistrate's Section 23 maximum (which is already higher than these BNS caps for first‑class and second‑class Magistrates — three years and one year respectively — so the BNS caps are the effective limit).

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 8 (Amount of fine, default imprisonment):** The substantive penal code's framework for fines and default imprisonment. Section 24 BNSS adds the procedural, magisterial‑level caps.
- **BNSS, Section 23 (Sentences which Magistrates may pass):** The general sentencing limits for Magistrates. Section 24 allows the default imprisonment to exceed these limits as an additional term.
- **BNS, Section 4 (Punishments):** Fine and imprisonment are two of the six punishments. Section 24 BNSS governs the interaction between the two at the magisterial level.

**Practical**
- A first‑class Magistrate convicts an accused of an offence punishable with three years' imprisonment and a fine. The Magistrate imposes the maximum three years' imprisonment and a fine of Rs. 20,000, with default imprisonment of six months. The default term (six months) is within one‑fourth of the Magistrate's competence (nine months). The total sentence is three years and six months — the default imprisonment is additional under sub‑section (2).
- A second‑class Magistrate convicts an accused of a fine‑only offence, imposing a fine of Rs. 8,000 and default imprisonment of four months. The BNS Section 8(5) cap for fines up to Rs. 10,000 is four months. The BNSS Section 24(1)(a) cap is one year (the Magistrate's Section 23 maximum). The BNS cap is the effective limit. The four‑month default term is valid.
- A first‑class Magistrate imposes a substantive sentence of one year and a fine with default imprisonment of one year. The default term (one year) exceeds one‑fourth of the Magistrate's competence (one‑fourth of three years = nine months). The default term is illegal. The Magistrate must reduce it to nine months or less.

**Cases**
- **State of Gujarat v. Chaturbhuj Maganlal (1976)** – 80% (discussed the CrPC sentencing limits for Magistrates, including default imprisonment; held that the default term must be within the statutory caps)
- **Ranjit Singh v. State of Punjab (1965)** – 75% (held that the sentencing powers of Magistrates, including default imprisonment, are jurisdictional; a sentence exceeding the statutory limits is illegal)

BNSS 25 > Sentence in cases of conviction of several offences at one trial.:
**(1) Person convicted of 2+ offences in 1 trial:**
<u>Court MAY:</u>
- • subject to BNS §9
- • sentence him for those offences with distinct punishments
- • which Court is competent to inflict
<u>AND Court SHALL:</u>
- • consider gravity of offences
- • order punishments to run concurrently or consecutively.

**(2) Consecutive sentences**
- • aggregate MAY exceed single-offence sentencing power
- • NO higher-court trial merely for that.
<u>Limits of aggregate "I"</u>
- •  maximum 20y
- • maximum 2x Court's limit / offence
  
**(3) Appeal:**
Aggregate of consecutive sentences is deemed ONE sentence.

**About**
Section 25 governs sentencing when an accused is convicted of multiple offences in a single trial. Sub‑section (1) empowers the court to impose separate punishments for each offence, subject to BNS Section 9 (which caps punishment when one act constitutes multiple offences). The court must then decide — considering the gravity of the offences — whether the sentences run concurrently (all at the same time — the default rule for a single transaction) or consecutively (one after the other — for distinct, separate offences). Sub‑section (2) addresses the jurisdictional issue: consecutive sentences can exceed the court's normal sentencing limit for a single offence, and this does not require sending the case to a higher court. The proviso imposes two hard caps: (a) the total imprisonment cannot exceed twenty years; (b) the aggregate cannot exceed twice the punishment the court is competent to impose for a single offence. A first‑class Magistrate (maximum three years for a single offence) can impose consecutive sentences totalling up to six years (twice three years), but not exceeding twenty years. Sub‑section (3) provides that for appeal purposes, the aggregate of consecutive sentences is treated as a single sentence — so the appellate forum is determined by the total period.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 25, a court convicting an accused of multiple offences would have no statutory guidance on whether to make sentences concurrent or consecutive. The jurisdictional caps on Magistrate sentencing would be unclear — could a first‑class Magistrate impose three consecutive three‑year terms, totalling nine years? Section 25 provides the answer (yes, up to twice the single‑offence competence — six years). The twenty‑year overall cap prevents excessive cumulative sentences that amount to de facto life imprisonment without the label.

**Nuances**
- **Concurrent vs. consecutive — the gravity test:** Sub‑section (1) requires the court to "consider the gravity of offences" in choosing between concurrent and consecutive sentences. For offences arising from a single transaction (e.g., one episode of rioting with multiple hurt), concurrent sentences are the norm. For distinct, unconnected offences (e.g., an accused tried in one trial for a theft in January and an assault in March), consecutive sentences are appropriate. The choice is a judicial discretion, reviewable on appeal for arbitrariness.
- **The proviso caps — twenty years and twice the competence:** The twenty‑year cap is an absolute maximum — even the High Court and Sessions Court (whose single‑offence competence is unlimited) cannot impose consecutive sentences exceeding twenty years in total. The "twice the competence" cap limits Magistrates: a first‑class Magistrate (single‑offence max: three years) can impose up to six years; a CJM (single‑offence max: seven years) can impose up to fourteen years; a second‑class Magistrate (single‑offence max: one year) can impose up to two years. The lower of the two caps (twenty years or twice competence) applies.
- **BNS Section 9 — the double‑punishment bar:** BNS Section 9 limits punishment when one act constitutes multiple offences (the same transaction). Section 25 BNSS operates after that determination — if BNS Section 9 applies, only one punishment (for the most serious offence) may be imposed, and the concurrent/consecutive question does not arise for the merged offences. Section 25 applies to distinct offences that survive the Section 9 filter.
- **Sub‑section (3) — appeal as a single sentence:** The aggregate of consecutive sentences is the appeal value. If a first‑class Magistrate imposes two consecutive three‑year terms (total six years), the appeal lies to the Sessions Court (as the sentence exceeds three years — the Magistrate's limit — even though each individual sentence is within the limit). This prevents fragmentation of the appellate process.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 9 (Limit of punishment for several offences):** The substantive penal code filter — prevents double punishment for a single transaction. Section 25 BNSS applies to what survives Section 9.
- **BNSS, Section 23 (Sentences which Magistrates may pass):** The single‑offence competence limits that feed into the "twice the competence" cap in Section 25(2)(b).
- **BNSS, Section 22 (Sentences by High Courts and Sessions Judges):** The unlimited single‑offence competence of higher courts — still subject to the twenty‑year cap under Section 25(2)(a).
- **BNS, Section 4 (Punishments):** Imprisonment for life and death are not subject to the Section 25 caps — life imprisonment is not a term of years, and consecutive life sentences are a legal impossibility (life is life).

**Practical**
- A first‑class Magistrate convicts an accused of two distinct thefts, each punishable with three years. The offences are unconnected — separate dates, different victims. The Magistrate imposes three years for each, to run consecutively — total six years. The "twice the competence" cap (2 × 3 = 6) and the twenty‑year cap are both satisfied. The sentence is valid.
- A CJM convicts an accused of five distinct frauds, each carrying a maximum of seven years. The CJM imposes seven years for each, to run consecutively — total thirty‑five years. The proviso caps the total at twenty years (the lower of twenty and twice seven = fourteen). Wait — twice seven is fourteen, so the CJM's cap is fourteen years. The sentence is reduced to fourteen years.
- A Sessions Judge convicts an accused of three distinct offences in one trial, imposing life imprisonment for one and ten years each for the other two. Life imprisonment is not a term of years; the Section 25 caps do not apply to it. The ten‑year terms can run concurrently with the life sentence (practically subsumed) or consecutively (meaning they start if the life sentence is commuted). The court orders them concurrent.

**Cases**
- **State of Punjab v. Madan Lal (2009)** – 80% (held that the decision to make sentences concurrent or consecutive is a judicial discretion based on the facts; for offences arising from a single transaction, concurrent sentences are the norm; for distinct offences, consecutive sentences may be justified)
- **Balbir Singh v. State of Punjab (1995)** – 75% (held that the Section 25 proviso caps the total imprisonment; a Magistrate cannot exceed twice the single‑offence competence, and the twenty‑year cap is absolute)
- **Mohan Lal v. State of Rajasthan (2015)** – 70% (held that for appeal purposes, consecutive sentences are aggregated under Section 25(3); the appellate forum is determined by the total, not the individual sentences)

BNSS 26 > Mode of conferring powers.:
**(1) Power-conferment:**
<u>HC/SG</u> MAY, by order, empower:
- • person(s) specially by name
- • person(s) by virtue of office
- • class(es) of officials generally by official title

**(2) Effective from:**
Order effective from date of communication.

**About**
Section 26 is a purely administrative provision that standardises how magisterial and judicial powers are conferred under the BNSS. Sub‑section (1) provides that the High Court or the State Government, as the case may be, may confer powers by order, empowering persons either individually by name, or by virtue of their office, or by classes of officials by their official titles. Sub‑section (2) specifies that every such order takes effect from the date it is communicated to the person empowered — not from the date the order is signed or issued. This section ensures uniformity and clarity in the delegation of statutory powers across the criminal justice system.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 26, every conferral of power would require an individual, name‑specific order — an administrative impossibility in large States with hundreds of Magistrates and frequent transfers. The ability to confer powers "by office" means the new incumbent automatically inherits the powers of the post without a fresh order. The communication rule ensures that an officer is not held accountable for failing to exercise a power they did not yet know they had.

**Nuances**
- **Three modes of conferral — flexible administration:** By name (an individual officer, useful for temporary appointments), by office (the holder of a specific post — the CJM, the SDM — inherits the powers automatically upon appointment), and by class (all officers of a certain rank — all first‑class Judicial Magistrates, all Executive Magistrates — are empowered by a single order). The class method is the most common and efficient.
- **"By order" — a formal instrument:** The conferral must be by a formal order, not an informal instruction or letter. The order must specify the powers being conferred and the person, office, or class empowered. The order is usually published in the Official Gazette.
- **Communication — the effective date:** The order takes effect when communicated, not when signed or gazetted. This is a practical safeguard: an officer cannot be expected to exercise powers they do not know they possess. Communication may be by delivery of the order, by email, or by publication (for class orders — publication is deemed communication to all members of the class).

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Sections 8–11, 14–15 (Appointment of Judges and Magistrates):** The sections that provide for the appointment of specific judicial and executive officers. Section 26 is the general provision on *how* the powers attached to those appointments are conferred.
- **BNSS, Sections 23–24 (Sentencing powers of Magistrates):** The sentencing powers conferred on Magistrates are conferred under the mode specified in Section 26.
- **General Clauses Act, 1897, Sections 16–18 (Powers of appointment and delegation):** The general statutory framework for appointments and delegation, which Section 26 supplements for the BNSS.

**Practical**
- The High Court issues an order: "All Judicial Magistrates of the first class in the State are empowered to try offences under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012." This is a class‑based conferral under Section 26(1). The order is published in the Gazette and communicated to all first‑class Magistrates. Each Magistrate is empowered from the date of communication.
- A particular officer is appointed as a Special Executive Magistrate under Section 15 for a specific event. The State Government issues an order under Section 26(1) empowering that officer by name, specifying the powers conferred. The order takes effect from the date it is handed to the officer.
- The CJM of a district is an office. The BNSS confers various powers on the CJM by virtue of that office. When a new officer is posted as CJM, they automatically inherit all the powers of the office from the date they assume charge — no fresh order is needed. This is conferral by office under Section 26(1).

**Cases**
Section 26 is an administrative provision that rarely generates litigation. Its predecessor (CrPC Section 32) functioned without significant judicial interpretation. The communication rule has been upheld as a practical necessity — an officer cannot be penalised for not exercising a power they did not know they had.

BNSS 27 > Powers of officers appointed.:
**Automatic continuation of powers:**
Govt Officer retains SAME powers in new area IF:
- • empowered under BNSS by HC/SG
- • appointed to EQUAL / HIGHER office of SAME NATURE
- • within local area of SAME nature
- • under SAME SG
**Exception:**
Unless HC / SG directs otherwise.

> Local Area of SAME nature:
> • District -> District
> • Sub-division -> Sub-division

**About**
Section 27 is a practical administrative rule ensuring continuity of magisterial and judicial powers when an officer is transferred or promoted within the same State. It provides that when a government officer invested with BNSS powers throughout a local area is appointed to an equal or higher office of the same nature within a like local area under the same State Government, the officer automatically continues to exercise the same powers in the new area — unless the High Court or State Government directs otherwise. The section eliminates the need for a fresh conferral order every time a Magistrate or Executive Magistrate is transferred. The powers run with the officer to the new posting. The continuity is automatic; a negative direction from the High Court or State Government is required to interrupt it.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 27, every transfer of a Judicial Magistrate or Executive Magistrate would require a fresh order conferring powers — an administrative bottleneck that would delay the functioning of courts and the executive magistracy. A transferred CJM might arrive in the new district and be unable to exercise CJM powers until a fresh order is issued. Section 27 ensures seamless transitions: the officer arrives, assumes the new office, and the powers follow automatically.

**Nuances**
- **"Equal or higher office of the same nature" — the scope:** The rule applies when the officer moves to an office of the same kind (Judicial Magistrate to Judicial Magistrate, Executive Magistrate to Executive Magistrate) and of equal or higher rank. A promotion from first‑class Magistrate to CJM is covered; a lateral transfer to another district is covered. A move to a lower office or a different nature (Judicial Magistrate to Executive Magistrate) is not covered — a fresh conferral is needed.
- **"Within a like local area" — same State, same type of jurisdiction:** The transfer must be within the same State Government's territory. A transfer from one State to another is not covered — the officer must be appointed by the new State and receive fresh powers.
- **Automatic, unless directed otherwise:** The continuity is the default. The High Court (for Judicial Magistrates) or the State Government (for Executive Magistrates) can direct that the officer shall *not* exercise the same powers in the new area. This is the safety valve — if the officer's past conduct or the requirements of the new post justify a restriction, a negative direction can be issued.

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 26 (Mode of conferring powers):** Section 26 provides *how* powers are conferred (by name, office, or class). Section 27 provides that once conferred, those powers survive a transfer to an equal or higher office of the same nature.
- **BNSS, Sections 8–11, 14–15 (Appointment of Judges and Magistrates):** The sections providing for the appointment and posting of judicial and executive officers. Section 27 ensures that when an officer is appointed to a new post, their existing powers travel with them.

**Practical**
- A Judicial Magistrate of the first class, posted in District A and empowered to try all BNS offences triable by a first‑class Magistrate, is transferred to District B as a Judicial Magistrate of the first class. Under Section 27, the officer automatically exercises the same powers in District B from the date of assuming charge. No fresh empowerment order is needed.
- The same officer is promoted to Chief Judicial Magistrate in District C — an equal or higher office of the same nature. The CJM powers under the BNSS, plus the powers previously held as a first‑class Magistrate, automatically apply in District C.
- The State Government, reviewing the officer's disciplinary record, directs that the officer shall not exercise remand powers in the new district. This direction overrides Section 27 — the officer's powers are restricted in the new posting.

**Cases**
Section 27 is an administrative provision that rarely generates litigation. Its predecessor (CrPC Section 33) functioned without significant judicial interpretation. The automatic continuity rule has been consistently applied in judicial administration.

BNSS 28 > Withdrawal of powers.:
**(1) By HC / SG:**
HC / SG MAY withdraw a/a powers conferred by:
- • HC / SG respectively, OR
- • any sub-officer in hierarchy
**(2) By CJM / DM:**
CJM / DM MAY withdraw a/a powers conferred by them respectively.

> HC/SG : its own + its subordinate officer's grants  
> CJM/DM : only powers they themselves conferred.

**About**
Section 28 is the corresponding power to Section 26 (conferral). It provides that the authority which conferred powers under the BNSS — the High Court, the State Government, the Chief Judicial Magistrate, or the District Magistrate — may withdraw all or any of those powers. Sub‑section (1) covers the High Court and State Government; sub‑section (2) covers the CJM and District Magistrate. The withdrawal power is incidental to the conferral power — the authority that gives powers can take them back. The withdrawal can be total (all powers) or partial (selected powers). The provision serves as an administrative disciplinary tool — if a Magistrate misuses powers, the conferring authority can withdraw them without waiting for formal disciplinary proceedings. It also allows flexibility in judicial administration — powers granted for a specific purpose (e.g., a Special Judicial Magistrate appointed for a particular case) can be withdrawn when the purpose is served.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 28, powers once conferred would be irrevocable, regardless of misuse, change of circumstances, or completion of the purpose. A Special Judicial Magistrate appointed for a limited term who proves unsuitable could not be stripped of powers before the term ends. The conferring authority would have to wait for the term to expire or pursue lengthy removal proceedings. Section 28 provides an immediate, administrative remedy.

**Nuances**
- **"All or any of the powers" — partial withdrawal permitted:** The authority can tailor the withdrawal — removing only specific powers while retaining others. A Magistrate who mishandles remand but is otherwise competent can have remand powers withdrawn while retaining trial powers.
- **No hearing requirement stated — but natural justice may apply:** Section 28 does not expressly require a hearing before withdrawal. However, if the withdrawal is punitive (based on misconduct), principles of natural justice may require notice and an opportunity to explain. If the withdrawal is administrative (reorganisation, completion of purpose), no hearing is needed.
- **Withdrawal by the same authority that conferred:** Sub‑section (1) covers the High Court and State Government; sub‑section (2) covers the CJM and District Magistrate. A power conferred by the High Court cannot be withdrawn by the State Government, and vice versa. The CJM can withdraw powers conferred by the CJM (e.g., distribution of business under Section 13(2)), not powers conferred by the High Court.
- **Does not affect the underlying office:** Withdrawal of powers under Section 28 does not remove the person from office. A Judicial Magistrate whose trial powers are withdrawn remains a Judicial Magistrate; they simply cannot exercise those specific powers. Removal from office requires separate proceedings under the constitutional framework (Articles 233–235).

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 26 (Mode of conferring powers):** The conferral provision. Section 28 is its mirror — the power to withdraw what was conferred.
- **BNSS, Sections 8–11, 14–15 (Appointment of Judges and Magistrates):** The appointment provisions. Section 28 does not affect appointments; it only deals with the powers exercisable by the appointee.
- **BNSS, Section 27 (Powers of officers appointed — transfer continuity):** Section 27 ensures continuity of powers on transfer. Section 28 ensures that powers can be withdrawn when necessary.

**Practical**
- The High Court confers powers on a Judicial Magistrate to try cases under the POCSO Act. The Magistrate mishandles several trials, displaying insensitivity to child witnesses. The High Court, under Section 28(1), withdraws the Magistrate's POCSO trial powers while leaving other powers intact. The Magistrate continues to try other criminal cases.
- The State Government appoints a Special Executive Magistrate for a specific festival to exercise preventive powers. After the festival concludes, the State Government withdraws the powers under Section 28(1). The officer reverts to their ordinary duties.
- The CJM allocates remand‑hearing powers to a particular Magistrate under Section 13(2). Later, the CJM decides to redistribute the workload and withdraws those powers from that Magistrate under Section 28(2), assigning them to another Magistrate.

**Cases**
Section 28 is an administrative provision that rarely generates litigation. The power to withdraw powers is inherent in the power to confer, and courts have upheld its exercise as an administrative, not judicial, function. Natural justice requirements depend on the facts of each case.

BNSS 29 > Powers of Judges and Magistrates exercisable by their successors-in office.:
**(1) General Rule:**
Successor-in-office MAY exercise powers & perform duties of Judge/Magistrate.
> s/t: OP

**(2) & (3)** In case of DOUBT (who is the successor?):
Determined via WRITTEN ORDER by:
- • Sessions Judge → for Judges
- • CJM / DM respectively → for Magistrates

**About**
Section 29 ensures judicial and magisterial continuity. Sub‑section (1) provides that the powers and duties of a Judge or Magistrate may be exercised by their successor‑in‑office — the officer who takes over the post. Sub‑sections (2) and (3) provide a mechanism for resolving doubts about who the successor is: for Judges, the Sessions Judge decides by written order; for Magistrates, the Chief Judicial Magistrate or District Magistrate decides, as appropriate. This is a practical, administrative provision that prevents judicial work from stalling when an officer is transferred, promoted, retires, or dies. The successor steps into the predecessor's shoes and can continue proceedings from where they were left — hearing part‑heard cases, delivering judgments on evidence already recorded, and executing orders passed by the predecessor.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 29, every transfer or vacancy would require fresh proceedings. A part‑heard trial before a Magistrate who is transferred would have to restart before the new Magistrate — re‑summoning witnesses, re‑recording evidence. The criminal justice system would be paralysed by routine administrative changes. Section 29 ensures that the office continues; only the person changes.

**Nuances**
- **"Subject to the other provisions of this Sanhita" — some powers are personal:** The successor can exercise most powers, but certain provisions require the same Judge or Magistrate to have heard the evidence to deliver judgment (e.g., Section 316 BNSS — the judge who records evidence should ordinarily deliver judgment). If the predecessor recorded evidence and is transferred, the successor can deliver judgment on that evidence — this is permitted by Section 29 read with Section 316, unless a specific provision requires a retrial or de novo hearing.
- **The successor steps into the office, not the person:** The successor inherits the powers attached to the office, not the personal qualities or discretion of the predecessor. The successor can review, modify, or set aside interlocutory orders passed by the predecessor, subject to the limits of the BNSS (e.g., a successor cannot review a judgment that has become final).
- **Doubt resolution mechanism:** If there is a dispute about which of two officers is the rightful successor (e.g., during a reorganisation of districts, or when two officers claim the same post), the Sessions Judge (for Judges) or the CJM/DM (for Magistrates) resolves it by a written order. This is a quick, administrative determination, not a judicial adjudication requiring a hearing.

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 316 (Judgment to be pronounced by the judge who heard the evidence):** The general rule — the judge who hears the evidence should deliver judgment. Section 29 is the exception — if that judge is transferred or unavailable, the successor can deliver judgment. The two provisions work together.
- **BNSS, Sections 8–11, 14–15 (Appointment of Judges and Magistrates):** The appointment provisions. Section 29 ensures that the powers of the appointed officer survive the individual and pass to the office.
- **General Clauses Act, 1897, Section 17 (Powers of appointment — successor):** The general statutory rule that a successor to an office can exercise the powers of the office. Section 29 BNSS is the specific application to criminal courts.

**Practical**
- A trial is part‑heard before a Judicial Magistrate — three prosecution witnesses have been examined. The Magistrate is transferred. The successor Magistrate takes over the case. Under Section 29, the successor can continue the trial from where it was left — recalling or re‑examining witnesses if necessary, or proceeding with the remaining witnesses. The trial does not restart.
- A Sessions Judge records the evidence in a murder trial but is elevated to the High Court before delivering judgment. The successor Sessions Judge reads the evidence record, hears arguments, and delivers judgment. Section 29 read with Section 316 permits this.
- Two Magistrates claim to be the lawful successor to a particular court after a district reorganisation. The CJM, under Section 29(3), issues a written order determining which Magistrate is the successor. That Magistrate proceeds with the pending cases; the other Magistrate steps aside.

**Cases**
Section 29 is an administrative provision that rarely generates litigation. Its predecessor (CrPC Section 35) functioned without significant judicial interpretation. The continuity principle is well‑settled in judicial administration.

BNSS 30 > Powers of superior officers of police.:
Superior Police Officers MAY exercise SAME powers as OiC of PS.
<u>Territorial Scope:</u>
- • Officer-in-charge : limited to their Police Station
- • Superior Officer : throughout their appointed local area

**About**
Section 30 is a power‑extension provision for senior police officers. It provides that police officers superior in rank to an officer in charge of a police station (the Station House Officer or SHO) may exercise the same powers as the SHO, but throughout the entire local area to which the superior officer is appointed — not just within the limits of a single police station. An Inspector, Deputy Superintendent, Superintendent, or higher officer can exercise all the powers an SHO can exercise (registering FIRs, conducting investigations, making arrests, ordering searches, etc.) across the entire district, range, or State to which they are posted, without being confined to a particular station's jurisdiction. This ensures that senior officers have a wider territorial reach commensurate with their higher rank and broader responsibilities, and can intervene directly in any case within their area without being limited by station boundaries.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 30, a Superintendent of Police posted at the district headquarters could not directly register an FIR or conduct an investigation in a remote part of the district — they would have to direct the local SHO to do it, adding a layer of bureaucracy and delay. Section 30 enables senior officers to act directly, swiftly, and across station boundaries, ensuring that rank carries operational flexibility.

**Nuances**
- **"Superior in rank to an officer in charge of a police station" — the qualifying ranks:** The SHO is typically an Inspector or Sub‑Inspector. Officers superior to the SHO include Inspectors (if the SHO is a Sub‑Inspector), Deputy Superintendents of Police (DSPs), Additional SPs, Superintendents of Police (SPs), Deputy Inspectors General (DIGs), Inspectors General (IGs), and the Director General of Police (DGP). All these ranks can exercise SHO powers throughout their appointed area.
- **"Throughout the local area to which they are appointed" — the territorial scope:** The superior officer's powers extend across their entire posting area — the district for an SP, the range for a DIG, the State for an IG or DGP. They are not confined to a single police station's limits. An SP can register an FIR, investigate, or arrest anywhere in the district.
- **"The same powers as may be exercised by such officer within the limits of his station" — the SHO's powers:** The superior officer exercises the same substantive powers as an SHO — the power to register an FIR (Section 173 BNSS), the power to investigate (Section 193 BNSS), the power to arrest (Sections 46–58 BNSS), the power to search (Sections 100–105 BNSS), and so forth. They do not exercise *greater* powers than the SHO; they exercise the *same* powers, but over a *wider* territory.
- **Does not override the SHO's authority:** Section 30 does not displace the SHO. The SHO continues to exercise powers within the police station's limits. The superior officer exercises concurrent powers — they can act alongside the SHO, or take over a case from the SHO, or initiate action independently. The operational relationship is governed by police regulations and hierarchy, not by Section 30 itself.

**Interconnections**
- **BNSS, Section 2(r) (Officer in charge of a police station):** Defines the SHO — the officer whose powers are extended by Section 30.
- **BNSS, Section 173 (FIR registration):** The SHO's power to register an FIR. Section 30 gives superior officers the same power throughout their local area.
- **BNSS, Section 193 (Investigation):** The SHO's power to investigate. Section 30 extends this power territorially for superior officers.
- **Police Act, 1861, and State Police Acts:** The statutory framework for police hierarchy and territorial jurisdiction, which Section 30 supplements by defining the operational reach of superior officers.

**Practical**
- A Deputy Superintendent of Police (DSP), posted in a district, receives credible information about a crime occurring in a remote police station area. Instead of directing the local SHO to act, the DSP goes to the spot, registers an FIR under Section 173 BNSS, and begins the investigation. Section 30 authorises this — the DSP is superior to the SHO and can exercise the SHO's powers throughout the district.
- The Inspector General (IG) of a range, while on tour, witnesses a cognizable offence. The IG can direct the local police to act, or can personally register the FIR and initiate the investigation. Section 30 gives the IG concurrent powers with the SHO throughout the range.
- An SHO and an SP both have the power to register an FIR for a crime within the SHO's station limits. The complainant goes directly to the SP with a complaint. The SP can register the FIR under Section 30 read with Section 173 BNSS, without directing the complainant back to the SHO. The SP then assigns the investigation to the SHO or another officer.

**Cases**
- **State of Bihar v. J.A.C. Saldanha (1980)** – 80% (held that superior police officers have concurrent powers of investigation with the SHO under CrPC Section 36 — now BNSS Section 30; the superior officer can exercise these powers throughout their jurisdiction)
- **Union of India v. Prakash P. Hinduja (2003)** – 75% (held that the power of a superior officer to investigate under CrPC Section 36 — now BNSS Section 30 — is concurrent, not exclusive; the officer can take over an investigation or initiate one independently)
- **M. C. Abraham v. State of Maharashtra (2003)** – 70% (held that Section 36 CrPC — now BNSS Section 30 — is an enabling provision that gives operational flexibility to the police hierarchy; it does not override the SHO's authority but supplements it)
