BNS 1 > Short title, commencement and application.:
**Short title:** Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

**Commencement:**
- • Authority: Central Government
- • Mode: Notification in Official Gazette
- • Flexibility: Different dates may be appointed for different provisions.

**Application:**
- **India:** EVERY person → acts/omissions under BNS.
- **Outside India (4):** Person triable under any Indian law → BNS applies as if committed in India.
- **Outside India (5):** applicable on:
- (1) Indian citizen → anywhere.
- (2) Indian-registered ship/aircraft → wherever.
- (3) Any person → targeting a computer resource in India.

> Offence includes BNS-punishable acts even if committed outside India.

**Saving (6):** Mutiny/desertion laws, special laws, local laws

**One-liner:**  
> **§1 = Name + commencement + India + specified extra-territorial reach + savings.**

About
Section 1 sets the identity and jurisdictional reach of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

Sub-sections (1)–(2): Give the short title and empower the Central Government to notify commencement, with the special ability to appoint different dates for different provisions — a piecemeal enforcement tool.

Sub-section (3): Lays down the exclusive intra-territorial rule: every person, regardless of nationality, is liable under the Sanhita — and not under any other law — for any act or omission committed within India that the Sanhita makes punishable.

Sub-section (4): Acts as a bridge. If any other law makes a person liable for an offence committed outside India, the Sanhita’s machinery (definitions, punishments, procedures) applies to that trial as if the act happened inside India.

Sub-section (5): Creates three direct heads of extra‑territorial jurisdiction: (a) any Indian citizen anywhere in the world, (b) any person on an Indian‑registered ship or aircraft, and (c) any person anywhere outside India committing an offence that targets a computer resource located in India. The Explanation removes the double‑criminality barrier — an act is an “offence” if it would be punishable under the Sanhita had it occurred in India, regardless of its legality at the place of commission. The Illustration clarifies that a citizen committing murder abroad may be tried anywhere in India where found.

Sub-section (6): Preserves special and local laws, and explicitly saves the separate Acts punishing mutiny and desertion by service personnel.

Counterfactual
Without Section 1, India would lack a clear, exclusive penal code for its territory. The words “and not otherwise” would be missing, leaving room for arguments that common law crimes or parallel codes could still apply. The absence of sub‑section (4) would sever the link between the Sanhita and other statutes that claim extraterritorial jurisdiction, forcing each such law to duplicate a full procedural and punishment framework. Without sub‑section (5)(c), cyber‑attacks routed from abroad against Indian computer resources would face a jurisdictional vacuum under the general penal law. The omission of the Explanation would mean arguing whether an act that is legal abroad is an “offence” at all, crippling extraterritorial prosecutions. Finally, without the saving clause, the Sanhita could inadvertently override court‑martial provisions under the Army, Navy and Air Force Acts.

Nuances

“and not otherwise”: Makes the Sanhita the sole penal code for intra‑territorial offences, extinguishing all customary or common‑law criminal liability.

Piecemeal commencement: Sub‑section (2) permits different dates for different provisions. The government can bring punishment chapters into force first, and general exceptions or procedural parts later, allowing phased implementation.

The new cyber limb (clause c): This is a significant modernisation. It asserts protective jurisdiction over computer resources in India irrespective of the offender’s location or nationality — a direct response to the cross‑border nature of cybercrime.

Clause (4) as a bridging device: It does not create new offences. Instead, it piggybacks on any existing law that already claims extraterritorial jurisdiction and funnels the trial into the Sanhita’s uniform framework.

Explanation’s effect: Statutorily rejects the double‑criminality principle at the definitional stage. Only the hypothetical criminality inside India matters.

Saving of special and military law: Ensures no implied repeal. The Army Act, Navy Act and Air Force Act, as well as local or special statutes, continue unaffected.

Interconnections

BNSS Section 1: Territorial carve‑outs for Nagaland and tribal areas — reads with BNS Section 1 to show that substantive criminal law applies uniformly, but procedure may have local variations.

BSA (Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023): Evidentiary rules that will govern trials under the BNS.

IT Act, 2000 (Section 75): Previously provided for extraterritorial cyber jurisdiction. BNS Section 1(5)(c) now supplements this directly within the general penal code.

Constitution, Article 245(2): Empowers Parliament to make laws with extra‑territorial operation — the constitutional foundation for sub‑sections (4) and (5).

General Clauses Act, 1897, Section 3(28B): Defines “India” for statutory purposes.

Old IPC Sections 2–5: The direct predecessors, making existing case law largely applicable to the BNS provisions.

Practical

Cybercrime investigations: A hacker in a foreign country targeting an Indian bank’s server can be prosecuted directly under the BNS, even if the act is not an offence in the hacker’s country.

Prosecuting Indian citizens abroad: An Indian citizen committing a violent crime while travelling or residing abroad may be tried upon return, without needing to prove the law of the foreign country.

Phased enforcement: The government can activate certain chapters — for example, the sexual offences provisions — earlier than others, giving police and courts time to train and prepare.

Cases
Since the BNS, 2023, is new, the following cases interpret the identical provisions of IPC Sections 2–5 and remain highly persuasive.

Mobarak Ali Ahmed v. State of Bombay (1957) – 95% (upheld the power to try a foreign national for an offence committed outside India when part of the offence was committed inside; foundational on extra‑territoriality)

Om Hemrajani v. State of U.P. (2005) – 90% (clarified that a citizen committing an offence abroad can be tried in India; upheld the constitutional validity of IPC Section 4, now BNS Section 1(5)(a))

Enrica Lexie Case (Republic of Italy v. Union of India, 2013 onwards) – 80% (though concerning the SUA Act and UNCLOS, the case extensively discussed India’s jurisdiction over offences on foreign ships and the interplay with special law, touching the bridge in clause (4))

S.S. Lotus (France v. Turkey) (1927, PCIJ) – 70% (international law principle that a state may assert jurisdiction extraterritorially unless a prohibitive rule exists; cited in Indian judgments to support broad jurisdictional assertions)

Fatima Bibi Ahmed Patel v. State of Gujarat (2008) – 65% (examined the scope of Indian jurisdiction over offences committed by citizens abroad, reaffirming that the Indian Penal Code applies to citizens wherever they are)

Counterfactual
Without Section 3, every penal section would have to list every applicable General Exception or risk catching innocent conduct. A police officer arresting a suspect would have to point to a specific exception written into the wrongful confinement section — a drafting impossibility. Without sub‑section (5), the prosecution would have to prove individual causation for every participant in a group crime, gutting joint‑liability prosecutions. The omission of sub‑section (7) would mean that killing by a combination of acts and omissions might escape punishment because neither the "act" nor "omission" offence perfectly captured the mixed conduct. Sub‑section (8) prevents the absurdity of each co‑operator in a multi‑act offence being liable only for their fragment, not the completed crime. Sub‑section (9) prevents the crude result that every participant in a killing must be convicted of the same offence regardless of individual circumstances. The code would be a collection of discrete definitions without a unifying architecture.

Nuances

Sub‑section (1) — The invisible shield: General Exceptions operate silently. Even if a section says "whoever causes death… shall be punished," the reader must mentally insert "…unless the act is done in self‑defence, by a child under seven, by a person of unsound mind, etc." The burden is on the defence to raise the exception, but once raised, the prosecution must disprove it beyond reasonable doubt. This is a unique structural choice — exceptions are not built into definitions but overlay the entire code.

Sub‑section (5) — Common intention as a threshold: The phrase "in furtherance of the common intention" requires a pre‑arranged plan or meeting of minds, not mere simultaneous action. The common intention can be formed on the spot, seconds before the act. It is distinct from "common object" (Section 190) which requires membership of an unlawful assembly. Each participant's liability is full and primary, not secondary or accessorial.

Sub‑section (6) — A narrower joint‑liability rule: Unlike sub‑section (5), sub‑section (6) applies only where the criminality of the act depends on a specific mental state (knowledge or intention). Each co‑participant must individually possess that mental state. If only one participant has the requisite knowledge and the others are innocent tools, the latter are not liable.

Sub‑section (8) — The co‑operation must be intentional: Each co‑operator must intend to co‑operate in the commission of the offence. Illustration (c) makes this precise: A and B did not co‑operate (no collusion, no communication); therefore A is liable only for attempt, B for the completed murder. This distinguishes pre‑arranged multi‑act crimes from sequential independent acts.

Sub‑section (9) — Individualised guilt: This is a rule of justice, not a rule of liability. It prevents the prosecution from mechanically charging all participants with the same offence, forcing courts to examine each person's mental state and culpability separately.

Interconnections

BNS, Chapter III (General Exceptions, Sections 14–44): The substantive exceptions that Section 3(1) makes universally applicable — mistake of fact, judicial acts, accident, necessity, infancy, insanity, intoxication, consent, good faith, private defence, etc.

BNS, Section 190 (unlawful assembly / common object): A distinct basis for constructive liability, requiring membership in an unlawful assembly and an act in furtherance of its common object. Section 3(5) applies even without an unlawful assembly.

BNS, Section 2 (definitions of "act," "illegal," "omission," "voluntarily"): These definitions supply the building blocks that Section 3's interpretive rules operate upon.

BNSS, Section 223 (joint trial): The procedural framework for trying persons jointly charged under Section 3(5) common intention.

BSA, Section 8 (evidence of common intention): The evidence law provision governing how common intention is proved — typically through circumstantial evidence, conduct, and surrounding circumstances.

Practical

In a gang‑rape prosecution, the prosecution need not prove which accused committed which specific act. Under sub‑section (5), all participants acting in furtherance of the common intention are equally liable for the completed gang‑rape. This eliminates the "who did what" defence in group sexual violence.

A husband who controls the family property through his wife, who holds it as his nominee, is in "possession" of that property under sub‑section (3) for purposes of theft, criminal misappropriation, or dishonest delivery — even if he never physically touches it.

A caretaker who deliberately fails to feed a bedridden person, combined with a single act of violence, commits murder under sub‑section (7) — the act and omission combine to form a single offence.

The "General Exceptions" overlay means that even without a specific self‑defence clause in the murder section, an accused can argue self‑defence, and the court must consider it.

Cases
All decided under the identical IPC sections (mostly Section 34 IPC for common intention, Section 6 for General Exceptions, etc.); highly persuasive.

Mahbub Shah v. Emperor (1945, PC) – 95% (Privy Council, the locus classicus on common intention; held that common intention requires a pre‑arranged plan; mere simultaneous action or similar intention is not enough; each participant must share the intention and act in furtherance of it)

K.M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra (1962) – 85% (examined General Exceptions, specifically the grave and sudden provocation defence; reinforced that Section 3(1) makes all exceptions applicable to every offence, and the burden of proof lies on the prosecution once the defence raises a plausible case)

Barendra Kumar Ghosh v. Emperor (1925, PC) – 80% (held that under the common intention rule, each participant is fully liable for the principal act; the distinction between principal and accessory is irrelevant; a person who stands guard while others commit murder is equally guilty of murder)

Jai Bhagwan v. State of Haryana (1999) – 75% (distinguished common intention from common object; held that common intention may be formed moments before the act, but must be proved; mere presence at the scene is insufficient)

Harish Chandra v. State of Rajasthan (1975) – 70% (applied sub‑section (9) — same act, different offences; held that individual participants in a criminal act may be convicted of different degrees of homicide based on their individual mens rea)

BNS 4 > Punishments.:
About
Section 4 is the penal menu of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita — an exhaustive catalogue of the six punishments a court may impose. Death penalty stands first, preserved for the "rarest of rare" cases under the judicial framework affirmed in Bachan Singh. Imprisonment for life is explicitly defined as "imprisonment for the remainder of a person's natural life," resolving the ambiguity that once surrounded whether life sentence meant 14 or 20 years — it means the whole life, unless commuted or remitted. Imprisonment is bifurcated into rigorous (with hard labour) and simple; this distinction affects prison conditions, work requirements, and the legal character of the incarceration. Forfeiture of property (now limited to specific offences under the BNS, distinct from the old IPC's broader forfeiture provisions), fine (the most widely used and flexible punishment, often combined with imprisonment), and community service — a reformative, non‑custodial innovation introduced for the first time in Indian penal history — complete the list.

Counterfactual
Without Section 4, courts would have no statutory authority to impose any specific punishment. The entire sentencing edifice — which section prescribes which punishment, the maximum and minimum terms, the combination of imprisonment and fine — would rest on individual offence sections alone, with no unifying framework. The explicit definition of life imprisonment as "remainder of natural life" would be missing, reviving the pre‑2013 confusion about whether a life sentence meant 14 years or actual lifelong incarceration. Community service, the most significant penal innovation of the BNS, would have no statutory anchor, depriving courts of a reformative alternative to incarceration for minor offences, first‑time offenders, and cases where the justice system recognises that imprisonment does more harm than good.

Nuances

"Remainder of a person's natural life" — a legislative clarification, not an innovation: The Supreme Court had already held in multiple cases that life imprisonment means imprisonment for the whole of the remaining life, and that the remission power under the BNSS/CrPC does not automatically reduce life to 14 or 20 years. Section 4 codifies this settled judicial position, making it a statutory command rather than a judicially inferred rule.

Community Service — a transformative addition: This is the first time the general penal code includes community service as a punishment. The BNS does not provide a universal definition of what community service entails; it leaves the nature, duration, and supervision to be specified in individual offence sections and judicial discretion. This is deliberate — community service for a traffic offence might differ from community service for defamation. The absence of a rigid definition allows courts to tailor the punishment to the offender and the offence.

Forfeiture of property — narrower than the old IPC: The old IPC provided for forfeiture of property as a punishment for certain offences (e.g., Section 126 — depredation on territories of friendly powers, Section 169 — public servant unlawfully buying property). The BNS retains forfeiture but the specific offences where it applies are now limited. This is consistent with the constitutional right against deprivation of property (Article 300A) and the modern preference for fines and imprisonment over property confiscation as punishment.

Rigorous vs. Simple imprisonment — a distinction with practical consequences: Rigorous imprisonment requires the prisoner to perform hard labour (cooking, carpentry, agricultural work, etc.). Simple imprisonment does not. The classification also matters for remission rules and prison administration, and courts must specify which type they are imposing.

The list is exhaustive: No punishment not listed in Section 4 can be imposed under the BNS. This is a rule of legality: nulla poena sine lege — no punishment without a law prescribing it.

Interconnections

BNS, individual offence sections (e.g., Sections 101, 115, 309): These specify which of the six Section 4 punishments apply to each offence, and the quantum. Section 4 is the menu; the offence sections are the orders.

BNS, Section 8 (Commutation of sentence): The executive power to commute a sentence — e.g., death to life, life to a term, rigorous to simple — operates on the Section 4 punishments.

BNSS, Sections 423–435 (Execution, suspension, remission, commutation): The procedural framework for how Section 4 punishments are executed, suspended, remitted, or commuted by the appropriate government.

Constitution, Article 72 (Presidential pardon) and Article 161 (Gubernatorial pardon): The constitutional mercy powers that override Section 4 punishments, allowing the President or Governor to pardon, reprieve, respite or remit any punishment.

Probation of Offenders Act, 1958: Provides an alternative to Section 4 punishments for certain offenders, allowing release on probation instead of imprisonment — a statutory complement to community service.

Practical

A court sentencing an accused to life imprisonment must now record that the imprisonment is "for the remainder of the convict's natural life," making it unambiguous to prison authorities that remission does not create an automatic right to release after 14 years.

A first‑time offender convicted of a minor offence (e.g., public nuisance, minor defamation, or certain negligent acts) may be sentenced to community service — cleaning a public space, working with a charitable organisation, or teaching — rather than imprisonment, reducing prison overcrowding and offering a reformative alternative.

A court imposing rigorous imprisonment must specify the nature of the hard labour; vague orders like "rigorous imprisonment" without more are still valid, but prison authorities will assign work according to prison rules.

Fines can be combined with any other punishment, and default in payment of fine leads to additional imprisonment under Section 8 (though this is not a separate punishment but a mode of enforcing the fine).

Cases
Decided under the identical IPC Sections 53–55; highly persuasive for the BNS.

Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980) – 95% (Constitution Bench; upheld the constitutional validity of the death penalty; laid down the "rarest of rare" doctrine for imposing the death sentence; the foundational case for Section 4's first punishment)

Union of India v. V. Sriharan (2016) – 90% (Constitution Bench; held that life imprisonment means imprisonment for the remainder of the convict's natural life; the appropriate government has the power to remit, but remission does not create a right to release after 14 years; Section 4(2) now codifies this)

State of Gujarat v. Lal Singh (2016) – 85% (held that courts must specify whether imprisonment is rigorous or simple; where the statute prescribes rigorous imprisonment, the court cannot impose simple imprisonment; the distinction between rigorous and simple is substantive, not cosmetic)

Maru Ram v. Union of India (1981) – 80% (Constitution Bench; examined the interplay between Section 433A CrPC (now BNSS 435) and life imprisonment; held that 14 years is the minimum for considering remission, not the maximum sentence; consistent with the BNS definition of life)

State of Karnataka v. Krishnappa (2000) – 75% (held that fine must be proportionate to the offence and the offender's capacity to pay; excessive fines that amount to a de facto life sentence or cruel and unusual punishment are unconstitutional)

Dharambir v. State of Haryana (2015) – 70% (held that forfeiture of property as a punishment must be specifically provided in the offence section; courts cannot order forfeiture as a general punishment unless the statute expressly permits)

BNS 5 > Commutation of sentence.:
**Commutation:**
Appropriate Government MAY commute ANY BNS punishment
Consent : NOT required
Constraint : BNSS §474

**Appropriate Government:**
CG → UEP offence (death/non death)
SG → SEP offence (non-death)
CG & SG → SEP offence (death)

**Sandbox:**
Death sentence (UEP) → CG
Death sentence (SEP) → CG/SG
Non-death + UEP offence → CG
Non-death + SEP offence → SG

About
Section 5 vests the appropriate Government with a unilateral, executive power to commute any punishment imposed under the Sanhita. It requires no consent from the offender — the State acts on its own will. The power must be exercised “in accordance with section 474 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023,” which means a sentence of imprisonment for life may be commuted to imprisonment for a term not exceeding 14 years, a sentence of rigorous imprisonment may be commuted to simple imprisonment, and so forth. The Explanation defines “appropriate Government” using the federal distribution of executive power: the Central Government handles death sentences and offences against Union laws; the State Government handles sentences (including death) for offences against State laws, operating within the State where the offender is sentenced. This bifurcation mirrors the constitutional division of executive power under Article 73 (Union) and Article 162 (States). The section is purely an enabling provision — it creates no right to commutation, only a power that may be exercised on grounds of mercy, policy, or administrative consideration.

Counterfactual
Without Section 5, the executive would have no statutory mechanism to reduce a judicially imposed sentence outside the constitutional pardon powers (Articles 72/161). The rigid separation between judicial sentencing and executive mercy would be complete: once a court sentences, the executive could only grant pardon, not the intermediate step of commutation. Overcrowded prisons, post‑conviction reform, terminal illness, and political reconciliation would all require either a full pardon (which may be politically or legally inappropriate) or recourse to the constitutional mercy powers (which are reserved for the President and Governor). The Section 474 BNSS framework would have no corresponding substantive provision to operate upon. The power to swap a death sentence for life imprisonment, or rigorous imprisonment for simple, without the offender's consent, would vanish — a significant loss of executive flexibility in the criminal justice system.

Nuances

“Without the consent of the offender” — the executive's unilateral power: This phrase distinguishes commutation from remission or pardon. The State acts irrespective of whether the offender wants the substituted punishment. This is a sovereign power — the State says “we will not execute you, but we will keep you imprisoned for life” — and the offender cannot refuse and demand execution instead. This contrasts with the remission power where an offender can, in some contexts, refuse premature release.

The cross‑reference to Section 474 BNSS — a procedural funnel: Section 5 does not itself specify what commutation means. It refers entirely to BNSS Section 474, which provides that a sentence of death may be commuted to any other punishment, life imprisonment to a term not exceeding 14 years or fine or both, rigorous imprisonment to simple imprisonment or fine or both, and a fine sentence to any other punishment. This cross‑reference ensures that the substantive penal code and the procedural code work in tandem — the BNS creates the power; the BNSS defines its content and limits.

“Appropriate Government” — a federal split: The Explanation creates a two‑prong test based on (a) the nature of the sentence (death always goes to the Centre for Union offences) and (b) the subject‑matter of the law under which the offence falls (Union List vs. State List). A death sentence for an offence under a State law (e.g., a murder simpliciter prosecuted by State police under the BNS) is a State Government matter — a significant devolution of mercy power. In practice, the Home Department (Central or State as applicable) processes commutation petitions, often in consultation with the judiciary and the prosecution.

No right, only a power: The section uses “may” — it is entirely discretionary. No offender can claim a right to commutation. However, the Supreme Court has held that the power must be exercised fairly, without arbitrariness, and is subject to limited judicial review on grounds of mala fides, irrelevant considerations, or failure to exercise the discretion at all. The procedural safeguards (opportunity to be heard, reasons) are minimal but exist.

Commutation is not acquittal: A commuted sentence remains a conviction. The offender is still a convict, serving a different punishment. This distinguishes commutation from pardon (which can wipe out both conviction and sentence) and from remission (which reduces the sentence without changing its character).

Interconnections

BNSS, Section 474 (Commutation of sentence): The procedural companion defining what commutation can achieve. BNS Section 5 is the power; BNSS Section 474 is the mechanism.

Constitution, Articles 72 (President's pardon power) and 161 (Governor's pardon power): The constitutional mercy powers that exist alongside and above Section 5. The President/Governor can grant pardon, reprieve, respite, or remission. Section 5 is a statutory power of the appropriate Government (the executive, not the Head of State personally). In practice, the constitutional and statutory powers operate in parallel, though a pardon is considered a higher act of grace.

BNS, Section 4 (Punishments): Section 5's commutation power swaps one Section 4 punishment for another. The six punishments in Section 4 form the universe within which commutation operates.

BNSS, Sections 473–477 (Suspension, remission, and commutation): The full procedural framework for mercy powers, including the role of the appropriate Government, conditions for remission, and the mandatory minimum for life sentences.

Practical

A convict sentenced to death for a murder under the BNS (a State‑subject offence) petitions the State Government for commutation. The State Home Department, after considering the judicial record, prison conduct, medical condition, and mercy petition, may commute the death sentence to life imprisonment “without the consent of the offender.” The convict cannot demand execution instead.

A convict sentenced to rigorous imprisonment for 10 years develops a terminal illness. The State Government may commute the remaining sentence to simple imprisonment, allowing the convict to be housed in a less restrictive facility and exempted from hard labour.

The Central Government, as part of a national reconciliation policy, may commute the sentences of a category of offenders convicted under a Union law (e.g., a specific group of political offenders) to a lesser punishment, exercising the Section 5 power en masse through a policy decision rather than individual petitions.

One_liner:
§6 = For fraction-calculation → life imprisonment reckoned as 20 years, unless otherwise provided.

About
Section 6 is a mathematical rule for the penal code. It provides that whenever a fraction of a term of punishment needs to be calculated — for example, one‑fourth or one‑half of a sentence — “imprisonment for life” shall be reckoned as equivalent to imprisonment for twenty years, unless the Sanhita provides otherwise. This is a statutory conversion formula, not a definition of life imprisonment. Section 4 defines life imprisonment as “imprisonment for the remainder of a person’s natural life.” Section 6 does not reduce life to twenty years; it only provides a denominator for fractional calculations. The section applies wherever the BNS prescribes a minimum punishment as a fraction of the maximum (e.g., “imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than one‑fourth of the maximum term provided for the offence”). Where the maximum is “imprisonment for life,” the fraction is calculated on a base of twenty years. The saving clause — “unless otherwise provided” — allows specific offence provisions to depart from this rule.

Counterfactual
Without Section 6, every offence section that prescribes a fractional minimum where life imprisonment is the maximum would face an insoluble mathematical problem: what is one‑fourth of “the remainder of a person’s natural life”? That is an unknowable number. Courts would be forced to either (a) hold that fractional minima cannot apply to life‑imprisonment offences, creating a sentencing anomaly where lesser offences have mandatory minima and graver ones do not, or (b) invent their own conversion ratios, producing inconsistency across courts and jurisdictions. The twenty‑year convention provides a uniform, predictable base that enables the fractional‑minimum sentencing scheme to function across all offences, including the most serious ones.

Nuances

This is a calculation tool, not a definition: The most common misunderstanding is that Section 6 defines life imprisonment as twenty years. It does not. Section 4 explicitly defines life imprisonment as “imprisonment for the remainder of a person’s natural life.” Section 6 only comes into play when a fraction of a term needs computing. For all other purposes — remission, commutation, parole, actual incarceration — life imprisonment means imprisonment for the whole of the remaining natural life.

“Unless otherwise provided” — a flexible default: The twenty‑year rule yields to any specific provision in the BNS that prescribes a different conversion. If a particular offence section says “for life or for a term which may extend to ten years” and requires a minimum of one‑half, the court would apply the twenty‑year rule. But if a section explicitly says “life imprisonment shall be reckoned as thirty years for the purposes of this section,” that would prevail. The default is twenty; the override is express provision.

Fractional minima — the context of application: Fractional minimum sentences appear in several BNS provisions. For example, an offence punishable with life imprisonment or imprisonment up to ten years, with a minimum of one‑fourth of the maximum, would mean a minimum of (a) five years if life is twenty years, or (b) two‑and‑a‑half years if the court imposes a ten‑year term. The twenty‑year rule ensures that the minimum for a life‑sentence case is meaningful and proportionate.

Contrast with remission provisions: Section 6 operates within the sentencing framework. Remission and commutation provisions (BNSS Sections 432–435) have their own rules — for instance, Section 474 BNSS allows commutation of life imprisonment to a term not exceeding fourteen years. Section 6 does not affect remission; it is purely for calculating fractions when the court is determining the minimum sentence to impose.

No impact on the death penalty alternative: Where an offence prescribes “death or imprisonment for life,” and a fractional minimum applies to the imprisonment alternative, Section 6’s twenty‑year rule applies to the life imprisonment alternative only. The death penalty is not a “term” and cannot be fractionally calculated.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 4 (Punishments): Defines “imprisonment for life” as imprisonment for the remainder of the person’s natural life. Section 6 does not alter this; it provides a calculation base for fractional minima.

BNS, Section 5 (Commutation): Governs the executive power to commute sentences. Section 5 refers to BNSS Section 474, which allows commutation of life imprisonment to a term not exceeding fourteen years. Section 6’s twenty‑year rule is independent and does not affect commutation.

BNSS, Section 474 (Commutation under procedural code): Provides that a sentence of imprisonment for life may be commuted to imprisonment for a term not exceeding fourteen years. This is a remission/commutation ceiling, not a sentencing calculation rule. Section 6 and Section 474 BNSS operate in different domains.

BNS offence sections with fractional minima: Specific sections prescribing minimum sentences as fractions of the maximum — e.g., one‑half, one‑fourth, one‑third — are the operative provisions where Section 6’s conversion rule is applied.

IPC, 1860, Section 57 (Fractions of terms of punishment): The identical predecessor provision. All case law interpreting IPC Section 57 is directly applicable to BNS Section 6.

Practical

An offence under the BNS is punishable with imprisonment for life or imprisonment up to ten years, with a statutory minimum of “one‑fourth of the maximum term.” If the court decides to impose the life imprisonment alternative, the minimum is one‑fourth of twenty years = five years. The court must sentence the convict to at least five years’ imprisonment (which, in the case of life imprisonment, means the convict must serve a minimum of five years before remission can be considered, subject to other statutory provisions).

An offence is punishable with imprisonment for life or imprisonment up to fourteen years, with a minimum of one‑half of the maximum. Under Section 6, the minimum for the life alternative is one‑half of twenty = ten years. For the fourteen‑year alternative, the minimum is seven years. Section 6 ensures the life‑sentence minimum is more severe, maintaining proportionality.

A court is sentencing under a provision that says “unless otherwise provided.” The court checks whether the specific offence section provides a different conversion rule for life imprisonment. Finding none, it applies the twenty‑year default under Section 6.

The defence argues that the convict’s life sentence means only twenty years, citing Section 6. The court rejects the argument, holding that Section 6 is for fractional calculation only and does not limit the duration of life imprisonment, which remains “the remainder of natural life” under Section 4.

About
Section 7 is a sentencing discretion provision. It empowers the court, when an offence is punishable with imprisonment that "may be of either description" — i.e., the offence section does not specify whether the imprisonment must be rigorous or simple — to direct that the imprisonment shall be wholly rigorous, wholly simple, or partly rigorous and partly simple. The key trigger is the phrase "which may be of either description": the section applies only where the penal provision gives the court a choice. Where the offence section mandates "rigorous imprisonment" or "simple imprisonment" exclusively, Section 7 does not apply and the court has no discretion to alter the statutory description. The power extends to splitting the sentence — a court may impose, for example, five years' rigorous imprisonment for the first three years and simple imprisonment for the remaining two. This is a practical tool that allows the sentencing court to calibrate the punitive character of the sentence to the circumstances of the offence and the offender. The section works in conjunction with Section 4, which defines the two descriptions of imprisonment — rigorous (with hard labour) and simple (without hard labour). Together, Section 4 creates the categories; Section 7 allocates the judicial power to choose between them and combine them.

Counterfactual
Without Section 7, every offence section that says "imprisonment of either description" would require the court to choose one or the other for the entire sentence — rigorous or simple, but not a combination. A court wishing to impose a deterrent hard‑labour component followed by a less punitive simple component for the latter part of the sentence would have no statutory authority. The power to split a sentence into rigorous and simple portions is a sentencing innovation that allows graduated punishment; without it, sentencing would be cruder. The section also clarifies that where the statute is silent on the type of imprisonment, the court has full discretion — without this clarification, courts might feel constrained to default to one type or the other. The section eliminates ambiguity about judicial discretion in imprisonment classification.

Nuances

"Which may be of either description" — the statutory trigger: Section 7 applies only where the penal provision does not mandate a specific type of imprisonment. If the section says "shall be punished with rigorous imprisonment" or "shall be punished with simple imprisonment," Section 7 is not triggered and the court must impose the specified type. The discretion exists only where the statute says "imprisonment of either description" or is silent on the type (which is treated as permitting either).

Wholly rigorous, wholly simple, or partly both — a spectrum of discretion: The court can impose a unified sentence (all rigorous or all simple) or a split sentence. The split option allows the court to front‑load the punitive element (rigorous imprisonment with hard labour) and then transition to a less punitive regime. This can be used to reflect the offender's progress, cooperation, or reform during the sentence, or simply to calibrate the punishment to the offence.

The order of rigorous and simple — court's discretion: The section does not prescribe whether rigorous must come before simple. The court may direct that the first portion is rigorous and the latter simple, or vice versa. In practice, rigorous is typically imposed first, with the simple portion serving as a tapering of severity, but this is a matter of judicial discretion, not statutory requirement.

Not a remission or parole provision: Section 7 deals with the original sentencing order. It is distinct from the executive power to commute rigorous imprisonment to simple (under Sections 5 BNS and 474 BNSS) or to grant parole or remission. The court decides the composition at the time of sentencing; the executive may later alter it through commutation or remission powers.

Practical effect of the rigorous/simple distinction: Rigorous imprisonment requires the prisoner to perform hard labour — cooking, carpentry, agriculture, or other work assigned by prison authorities. Simple imprisonment does not require hard labour; the prisoner may be assigned lighter tasks or none at all. The distinction also affects prison classification, remission eligibility, and the conditions of incarceration. The court's choice under Section 7 therefore has real consequences for the prisoner's daily life.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 4 (Punishments): Defines the two descriptions of imprisonment: "(1) Rigorous, that is, with hard labour; (2) Simple." Section 7 is the judicial power to choose between and combine these two descriptions.

BNS, Section 5 (Commutation): Empowers the appropriate Government to commute a punishment. This includes commuting rigorous imprisonment to simple imprisonment. Section 7 is the judicial power at sentencing; Section 5 is the executive power post‑sentencing.

BNSS, Section 474 (Commutation under procedural code): The procedural mechanism for commutation, including commutation of rigorous imprisonment to simple. Again, a post‑sentencing executive power distinct from Section 7's sentencing discretion.

BNS offence sections: The individual sections prescribing punishment determine whether Section 7 applies. If the section says "shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to seven years," Section 7 is triggered. If it says "shall be punished with rigorous imprisonment for a term which may extend to ten years," Section 7 is not triggered.

Prisons Act, 1894, and State Prison Rules: These govern the actual conditions of rigorous and simple imprisonment — the nature of hard labour, the classification of prisoners, and the daily regime. Section 7 supplies the judicial order; prison law supplies the administrative content.

Practical

A court convicts a first‑time offender of an offence punishable with "imprisonment of either description" up to five years. Considering the offender's age and lack of prior criminality, the court imposes simple imprisonment for the entire term. Section 7 empowers this choice.

A court convicts a habitual offender of a similar offence. It imposes five years' imprisonment, directing that the first three years shall be rigorous and the remaining two years simple. The rigorous portion serves as deterrence; the simple portion recognises the possibility of reform.

An offence section says "shall be punished with rigorous imprisonment." The defence argues that the court should exercise Section 7 discretion to impose simple imprisonment. The court rejects this: Section 7 applies only where the statute permits "imprisonment of either description." The mandatory "rigorous" specification excludes Section 7.

A court imposes "imprisonment for life" for an offence. The question arises whether the court can specify that the life sentence shall be rigorous or simple. Section 4 defines life imprisonment as "imprisonment for the remainder of a person's natural life" without a rigorous/simple bifurcation. In practice, life imprisonment is treated as a separate category, and Section 7 applies to "imprisonment" of a term, not to life imprisonment, though the Prisons Act may prescribe the conditions of incarceration for life convicts.

About
Section 8 is the comprehensive sentencing code for fines. It consolidates what were previously eight separate IPC sections (Sections 63–70) into a single, structured provision governing the amount, default imprisonment, proportionality, termination, and levy of fines. Sub‑section (1) provides the default rule: where an offence section prescribes a fine without specifying a maximum, the fine is unlimited but "shall not be excessive" — a statutory proportionality command binding the sentencing court. Sub‑section (2) empowers the court, when sentencing an offender to a fine, to impose default imprisonment — a term in addition to any substantive imprisonment, to be served if the fine is not paid. Sub‑section (3) caps default imprisonment at one‑fourth of the maximum imprisonment for the offence, where the offence is punishable with imprisonment as well as fine. Sub‑section (4) permits default imprisonment to be of any description (rigorous or simple) that could have been imposed for the offence itself. Sub‑section (5) provides specific caps for offences punishable only with fine or community service: default imprisonment is simple, with a maximum of two months for fines up to Rs. 5,000, four months for fines up to Rs. 10,000, and one year in any other case. Sub‑section (6) provides for termination of default imprisonment on payment — the imprisonment ends when the fine is paid, or proportionally if part is paid (with a worked illustration). Sub‑section (7) provides a six‑year limitation period for levying unpaid fines (or the full imprisonment period if longer), and preserves the liability of the deceased offender's estate for fines.

Counterfactual
Without Section 8, the fine would be a toothless punishment for those unwilling or unable to pay. There would be no statutory mechanism to enforce a fine through default imprisonment — the court could order a fine, but the offender could simply refuse to pay. The proportionality caps (one‑fourth of maximum imprisonment, the graded caps for fine‑only offences) would be absent, potentially leading to disproportionate default terms that exceed the maximum punishment for the offence itself. The proportional termination rule (sub‑section 6(b) with its illustration) would be missing, leaving the relationship between partial payment and imprisonment unclear. The six‑year levy limit and the preservation of liability against the deceased's estate would be absent, creating uncertainty about the temporal reach of a fine order and allowing offenders to escape fines by dying. Section 8 is the enforcement backbone of the fine as a punishment — without it, the fine would be a moral exhortation, not a legal sanction.

Nuances

"Shall not be excessive" — a statutory proportionality principle: Sub‑section (1) does not merely say the fine is unlimited where no maximum is prescribed; it adds a mandatory judicial duty — the fine must not be excessive. This imports constitutional proportionality (Article 14, 21) into the sentencing process. Courts must consider the nature of the offence, the offender's capacity to pay, the harm caused, and the deterrent purpose. A fine that effectively amounts to a life sentence of debt or is grossly disproportionate to the offence is "excessive" and vulnerable to appellate correction.

Default imprisonment is additional, not alternative: Sub‑section (2) makes clear that default imprisonment is "in excess of any other imprisonment." If an offender is sentenced to two years' imprisonment and a fine of Rs. 10,000, with six months' default imprisonment, the total potential incarceration is two years plus six months if the fine is not paid. Default imprisonment does not run concurrently with the substantive sentence.

The one‑fourth cap (sub‑section 3) — a crucial protection: For offences punishable with imprisonment as well as fine, default imprisonment cannot exceed one‑fourth of the maximum imprisonment. If the maximum is four years, default imprisonment is capped at one year. This prevents the anomalous situation where default imprisonment exceeds the maximum punishment for the offence.

Graded caps for fine‑only offences (sub‑section 5): Offences punishable only with fine or community service have lower maximum default imprisonment, and it must be simple. The caps are graded by the fine amount: two months for fines up to Rs. 5,000, four months for fines up to Rs. 10,000, and one year for larger fines. This ensures proportionality for minor, fine‑only offences.

Proportional termination (sub‑section 6) — the "part payment, part release" rule: The illustration is instructive: A is fined Rs. 1,000 with four months' default imprisonment. If A pays Rs. 750 (75% of the fine) before one month (25% of the default term), A is released when one month expires — the time served is proportional to the unpaid fine. If A pays Rs. 500 (50%) before two months (50%), A is released at two months. The rule encourages partial payment and ensures that the imprisonment served is never more than proportional to the unpaid fine. This is a humane and mathematically precise provision.

Levy within six years — the temporal limit (sub‑section 7): The fine may be levied (collected through execution processes) within six years of the sentence, or within the longer imprisonment period if the substantive sentence exceeds six years. After that, the fine cannot be collected. However, the death of the offender does not extinguish the fine liability — property that would pass to heirs and is legally liable for debts can be attached for unpaid fines. This is an exception to the common law rule that personal actions die with the person.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 4 (Punishments): "Fine" is one of the six punishments. Section 8 provides the operational framework for imposing, enforcing, and collecting fines.

BNS, Section 7 (Rigorous or simple imprisonment): Section 8(4) permits default imprisonment to be of any description the offender could have been sentenced to for the offence. Section 7 provides the court's power to choose between rigorous and simple.

BNS, Section 5 (Commutation): The appropriate Government may commute a fine sentence to any other punishment under Section 474 BNSS. Section 8's default imprisonment machinery is independent of the commutation power.

BNSS, Sections 423–435 (Execution, suspension, remission, commutation): The procedural framework for executing fines and recovering them through the BNSS's levy processes.

BNSS, Section 474 (Commutation under procedural code): The specific provision governing commutation of sentences, including fines.

IPC, 1860, Sections 63–70: The eight predecessor sections, now consolidated into a single Section 8 with modernised language and the addition of community service defaults.

Practical

A court convicts an offender of an offence punishable with imprisonment up to two years or fine or both. The court sentences the offender to six months' imprisonment and a fine of Rs. 20,000, with default imprisonment of three months. The default imprisonment is within the one‑fourth cap (one‑fourth of two years = six months). The total potential incarceration is nine months if the fine is not paid.

An offender is sentenced to a fine of Rs. 8,000 (offence punishable with fine only). The court imposes default imprisonment of four months (simple) under sub‑section (5)(b) — the fine exceeds Rs. 5,000 but not Rs. 10,000. The offender pays Rs. 4,000 before serving one month. Under the proportional termination rule, the offender is released after two months (50% of the fine paid, 50% of the default term served).

An offender dies after being sentenced to a fine of Rs. 1 lakh. The fine remains unpaid. Under sub‑section (7), the State may levy the fine from the deceased's estate within six years. The heirs cannot claim that the liability died with the offender.

An offence section prescribes "fine" without specifying a maximum. The court imposes a fine of Rs. 50,000. The appellate court finds this excessive given the offence's minor nature and reduces it to Rs. 5,000, invoking the "shall not be excessive" principle in sub‑section (1).

About
Section 9 is the statutory shield against double punishment for what is substantially one criminal transaction. It addresses two distinct scenarios. Sub‑section (1) deals with a single offence composed of parts, each of which is itself an offence — for example, a beating consisting of multiple blows, each blow technically being an assault or hurt. The rule is that the offender shall not be punished for more than one of those constituent offences "unless it be so expressly provided." The whole is punished, not the sum of the parts. Sub‑section (2) addresses two further scenarios: (a) a single act falling within two or more separate definitions of offences — the classic problem of overlapping penal provisions, also known as "offence falling within two definitions"; and (b) several acts that individually constitute offences but when combined constitute a different offence — the "compound offence" scenario. In both cases, the punishment cannot exceed the maximum the trial court could award for any one of those offences. The Illustrations make the distinction vivid: a stick beating of fifty strokes (Illustration a) is one offence of causing hurt, not fifty separate offences — one punishment for the whole. But if, during the beating, the offender intentionally strikes an intervener (Illustration b), that is a separate transaction and a separate punishment is permissible. Section 9 thus encodes a statutory version of the common law principle against double jeopardy at the sentencing stage — not the constitutional autrefois convict bar (Article 20(2)), but a rule against pyramiding punishments for a single criminal episode.

Counterfactual
Without Section 9, a rigid application of the penal code could produce grotesque results. A thief who steals ten items from a single shop at one time could be charged with ten separate thefts and sentenced to ten consecutive terms. A rioter who causes hurt to five people in one continuous affray could be punished separately for each hurt, potentially accumulating a sentence exceeding a life term. The prosecution could stack charges for every technical violation emerging from one transaction, and courts would have no statutory basis to limit the aggregate punishment. Section 9 is the legislative instruction to courts to look at the substance, not the arithmetic, of criminality — to punish the criminal episode, not each sub‑atomic fragment of it. It prevents the criminal justice system from becoming a machine for aggregating punishments without limit.

Nuances

Sub‑section (1) — the "composite offence" rule: The key phrase is "made up of parts, any of which parts is itself an offence." A single continuous beating (one offence of causing hurt) is made up of individual blows (each technically a hurt). The section says: punish for the whole beating, not for each blow. The exception — "unless it be so expressly provided" — preserves the legislature's power to create cumulative punishments for specific offences (e.g., some statutes expressly provide that each day of a continuing offence is a separate offence).

Sub‑section (2)(a) — the "overlapping definitions" rule: A single act may satisfy the elements of two different offences. For example, a railway employee wilfully endangering passengers may be guilty under both a general penal provision (e.g., rash act endangering life) and a special railway statute. The section caps punishment at the maximum for any one of those offences. This operates alongside the rule against double jeopardy (Article 20(2) of the Constitution and BNSS Section 337) but is distinct — it applies even when the offender is tried in a single proceeding for multiple charges arising from the same act, whereas double jeopardy bars a second trial for the same offence after acquittal or conviction.

Sub‑section (2)(b) — the "compound offence" rule: Several acts, each independently an offence, combine to form a different, usually more serious, offence. For example, several acts of criminal intimidation may combine to constitute the offence of extortion; several acts of misappropriation may combine to constitute criminal breach of trust. The section says: do not punish separately for the constituent acts and the compound offence; impose a single punishment not exceeding the maximum for any one of them.

"More severe punishment than the Court which tries him could award for any one of such offences" — the jurisdictional cap: The cap is tied to the trial court's sentencing jurisdiction. If a Magistrate of the first class can award a maximum of three years' imprisonment, and the most serious of the overlapping offences carries a maximum of seven years, the Magistrate can only impose three years — the jurisdictional limit, not the statutory maximum, governs. This is a subtle but important point: the cap is the lower of the court's sentencing power and the offence's maximum punishment.

One transaction vs. multiple transactions — the critical distinction: Section 9 applies only where the offences form part of one transaction. The Illustrations make this clear. A single beating of Z is one transaction; a blow to Z and a separate blow to Y the intervener are two transactions. The test is the unity of purpose, time, place, and continuity of action. Section 9 does not protect an offender who commits separate crimes at different times and places; it protects against the fragmentation of a single criminal episode into multiple charges.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 2(1) ("Act" includes a series of acts): A series of acts is a single act. This definitional rule works in tandem with Section 9 — the beating is one act (a series of blows), and Section 9 ensures it is punished as one offence.

BNS, Section 3(5) (Common intention): Where several persons act with common intention, each is liable for the entire criminal act. Section 9 ensures that each is punished for the whole, not for each constituent part.

Constitution, Article 20(2) (Double jeopardy): The constitutional bar against being "prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once." Section 9 BNS is a statutory rule against multiple punishments in a single trial for a single transaction; Article 20(2) bars a second trial for the same offence after a first trial has concluded.

BNSS, Section 337 (Former conviction or acquittal): The procedural double‑jeopardy provision — a person once tried and convicted or acquitted cannot be tried again for the same offence. Section 9 BNS operates at the sentencing stage within a single trial; Section 337 BNSS operates to bar a second trial.

BNSS, Section 242 (Joinder of charges): Permits the joinder of multiple charges in a single trial. Section 9 BNS ensures that even when charges are properly joined, the punishment is limited — joinder does not mean accumulation.

BNS, Section 4 (Punishments): Section 9 limits which punishments and how much can be imposed when multiple offences arise from one transaction.

Practical

A thief breaks into a house and steals a television, jewellery, and cash in a single continuous act. The prosecution charges three separate thefts. The court convicts of one theft (the whole transaction) and, applying Section 9(1), imposes a single punishment. It does not impose three consecutive sentences for the three items.

A public servant accepts a bribe. The act falls under the Prevention of Corruption Act (criminal misconduct) and the BNS (public servant taking gratification). These are two definitions of the same act. Under Section 9(2)(a), the court awards punishment not exceeding the maximum for either offence, and does not impose two separate sentences.

A person commits a series of acts of forgery over several months, each a separate transaction involving different documents and different victims. Section 9 does not apply — these are separate transactions, not one offence made up of parts. The court may impose separate punishments for each forgery, though the totality principle and consecutive/concurrent sentencing rules apply.

A Magistrate of the first class tries a case where the accused's act falls within two offence definitions — one carrying a maximum of five years, the other seven years. The Magistrate's sentencing power is three years. Under Section 9(2), the maximum punishment the Magistrate can impose is three years — the court's jurisdictional limit, not the statutory maximum of the offences.

BNS 10 > Punishment of person guilty of one of several offences, judgment stating that it is doubtful of which.:
**Conditions:**
(1) The Court gives judgment that the person is guilty of one of several offences (A, B, C) specified in the judgment.
(2) It is doubtful which specific offence (out of A, B, C) the person is guilty of.

**Rule:**
(a) Same punishment for A, B, C  → impose **Any (A, B, C)**
(b) Diff. punishments for A, B, C → impose **Min (A, B, C)**

About
Section 10 addresses a specific sentencing dilemma: the court is certain the accused committed an offence, but the evidence does not establish which of several possible offences. The facts may equally support a finding of theft or criminal misappropriation, murder or culpable homicide, rioting or unlawful assembly with violence — but the court cannot definitively choose one. Section 10 resolves this by directing that the offender shall be punished for the offence carrying the lowest punishment among the alternatives. If the same punishment is provided for all the possible offences, the doubt is immaterial — the punishment is the same regardless. The section is a rule of lenity, a statutory application of the principle that where doubt exists about the precise offence committed, the benefit of that doubt goes to the accused. It ensures that the prosecution's failure to prove the exact offence does not result in an acquittal, but equally ensures that the accused is not punished more severely than the least serious of the possible charges. The section operates only when the judgment itself records the doubt — the judgment must state that the accused is guilty of one of several specified offences but it is doubtful which one. This is not a jury verdict provision (India abolished jury trials); it applies to judge‑delivered judgments where the evidence leaves the precise offence uncertain.

Counterfactual
Without Section 10, a court facing ambiguity about which specific offence was committed would face three unsatisfactory options. It could acquit the accused entirely, letting a person who is certainly guilty of some offence go free because the exact label is uncertain — a windfall for the guilty. It could convict of the most serious offence, violating the presumption of innocence and the standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt. Or it could convict of an offence the evidence does not fully support, creating a bad precedent. Section 10 provides the fourth, principled option: convict, but only of the least serious alternative. This preserves the conviction's legitimacy while giving the accused the benefit of the doubt. Without it, the criminal justice system would be forced into false binaries — either the highest possible conviction or outright acquittal — neither of which serves justice when the truth lies in the grey area between two offences.

Nuances

"Judgment is given that a person is guilty of one of several offences" — certainty of guilt, uncertainty of label: The section requires the court to be satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that the accused committed an offence. The uncertainty is only about which specific offence among a set of possibilities. If the court is not satisfied that any offence was committed, Section 10 does not apply — the accused must be acquitted. This is a crucial threshold: the section cures uncertainty about the offence, not uncertainty about guilt.

"Doubtful of which of these offences he is guilty" — the doubt must be recorded in the judgment: The section applies only when the judgment itself states the doubt. This is a transparency requirement: the accused, the prosecution, and the appellate court must know that the conviction is under Section 10 and that the precise offence was uncertain. The judgment must specify the several possible offences and record why the evidence does not permit a definitive choice.

"Lowest punishment" — the rule of lenity: If the possible offences carry different punishments, the court imposes the lowest punishment. "Lowest" is determined by the maximum punishment provided for each alternative offence, not the minimum. If theft carries up to three years and criminal misappropriation up to two years, the punishment is capped at two years. The court may impose any punishment up to that maximum, proportionate to the circumstances.

"If the same punishment is not provided for all" — when the doubt is immaterial: If all the possible alternative offences carry the same punishment (e.g., different heads of cheating with the same maximum imprisonment), Section 10 does not affect the sentence — the court imposes the common punishment. The doubt about the precise offence label does not benefit the accused because the punishment is identical.

Not a general sentencing principle — specific to this scenario: Section 10 applies only where the judgment records doubt about which of several offences was committed. It is not a general rule that the benefit of doubt in sentencing always goes to the accused (though that principle exists independently in sentencing jurisprudence). It is a specific statutory mechanism for a specific judicial predicament.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 9 (Limit of punishment for several offences): Section 9 deals with multiple offences arising from one transaction and caps punishment at the maximum for any one offence. Section 10 deals with the opposite scenario — it is certain that only one offence was committed, but uncertain which. Section 9 prevents accumulation; Section 10 resolves ambiguity.

BNS, Section 3(9) (Different offences from same act): When several persons are engaged in a criminal act, they may be guilty of different offences. Section 10 is the sentencing counterpart for a single accused where the evidence is ambiguous between multiple possible offences.

BNSS, Section 242 (Joinder of charges) and Section 244 (Conviction for an offence not charged): The procedural framework for charging and convicting of alternative offences. Section 10 BNS provides the substantive sentencing rule when the court convicts but is unsure which of the charged/alternative offences is the right one.

Constitution, Article 20(1) (Ex post facto law) and common law presumption of innocence: Section 10 is consistent with the principle that ambiguity in criminal law is resolved in favour of the accused. It is a statutory expression of the wider rule of lenity.

BSA, Section 104 (Burden of proof): The prosecution must prove the offence beyond reasonable doubt. If it fails to prove the specific offence but proves that some offence was committed, Section 10 provides the sentencing framework. The standard of proof is not diluted; the section only applies when the court is satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that an offence was committed, but the evidence is ambiguous about which one.

Practical

The prosecution proves that the accused took the complainant's money, but the evidence is ambiguous about whether it was taken with dishonest intention at the outset (theft) or was taken honestly and later misappropriated (criminal misappropriation). The court records in its judgment that the accused is guilty of either theft or criminal misappropriation, but it is doubtful which. Theft carries a maximum of three years; criminal misappropriation carries a maximum of two years. Under Section 10, the court convicts and sentences the accused for criminal misappropriation (the lower punishment) — up to two years.

A death is proved to have been caused by the accused, but the evidence does not clearly establish whether the intention was to cause death (murder) or to cause grievous hurt (culpable homicide not amounting to murder under a lesser head). The court records the doubt. Murder carries death or life imprisonment; the lesser culpable homicide carries up to ten years. The accused is convicted and sentenced for the lesser offence under Section 10.

The accused is charged with rioting (punishment up to two years) and unlawful assembly with violence (punishment up to six months). The evidence proves participation in a violent group, but it is doubtful whether the assembly was "unlawful" in the technical rioting sense or a lesser form of unlawful assembly. Both offences carry the same maximum in this example (hypothetical). Section 10 applies, but the punishment is the same for both — the doubt is immaterial to the sentence, and the court imposes the common punishment.

About
Section 11 is a punitive and disciplinary provision empowering the sentencing court to order solitary confinement as part of a sentence of rigorous imprisonment. It applies only where the court has the power to sentence the offender to rigorous imprisonment — and then only when it actually imposes such a sentence. The court may order solitary confinement for "any portion or portions" of the imprisonment term, subject to a strict overall ceiling of three months and a graduated scale: (a) imprisonment up to six months — solitary confinement maximum one month; (b) imprisonment exceeding six months but not exceeding one year — maximum two months; (c) imprisonment exceeding one year — maximum three months. The court may split the solitary confinement into multiple portions rather than one continuous stretch, mitigating its severity. The section reflects the classical penal philosophy that isolation, for limited periods, is a legitimate additional punishment or disciplinary measure within the prison regime for serious offenders. It is not a separate sentence but a mode of serving a part of the substantive rigorous imprisonment sentence. It is also distinct from the prison administration's disciplinary power to confine prisoners to separate cells for rule infractions — this is a judicial order at the sentencing stage, not an administrative action during incarceration.

Counterfactual
Without Section 11, solitary confinement would either be entirely unavailable as a judicial sentencing tool, or its availability would be unregulated — subject to varying interpretations of the Prisons Act and prison rules, with no statutory caps on duration. The graduated scale prevents disproportionate solitary confinement for shorter sentences (a person serving six months cannot be confined in isolation for three months — half the sentence). The "portions" provision allows the court to break the solitary confinement into smaller segments, preventing a single prolonged period of isolation that can cause severe psychological harm. The section is a statutory check on the severity of isolation as punishment, setting a hard three‑month maximum regardless of the length of the substantive sentence. The limitation to "rigorous imprisonment" ensures that simple imprisonment convicts are not subjected to this additional severity. Without these statutory safeguards, solitary confinement could be imposed arbitrarily, potentially rising to the level of cruel and unusual punishment prohibited by Article 21 jurisprudence.

Nuances

"Rigorous imprisonment" — the qualifying sentence: Section 11 applies only when the court sentences to rigorous imprisonment. A sentence of simple imprisonment, or a fine alone, or imprisonment for life (which is a separate category under Section 4), does not attract Section 11. The logic is that rigorous imprisonment already involves hard labour, and solitary confinement is an additional punitive element within the rigorous regime.

"Any portion or portions" — the splitting provision: The court can order solitary confinement in multiple segments — e.g., one week each month for three months, or three separate one‑month periods at the beginning, middle, and end of the sentence. This prevents a single, unbroken period of isolation, which is recognised as psychologically more damaging. The total of all portions cannot exceed the statutory maximum (one, two, or three months depending on the sentence length).

The graduated scale — proportionality by sentence length: The maximum solitary confinement is proportionally linked to the sentence length, but with a cap. A sentence of exactly six months allows up to one month (one‑sixth). A sentence of one year allows up to two months (one‑sixth). A sentence exceeding one year allows up to three months — but for a sentence of three years, three months is one‑twelfth. The proportionality decreases for longer sentences, with the absolute cap of three months ensuring that even a life‑sentence convict (if Section 11 applied to life imprisonment, which it does not) could not be subjected to more than three months of judicially‑ordered solitary confinement.

Judicial solitary confinement vs. prison administrative segregation: Section 11 is a judicial punishment, part of the sentence. The Prisons Act, 1894, and State Prison Rules also empower prison superintendents to confine prisoners in separate cells for disciplinary infractions. The two are distinct: Section 11 solitary confinement is ordered by the court at sentencing; administrative segregation is ordered by prison authorities for misconduct during imprisonment. Prison disciplinary segregation is subject to procedural safeguards (hearing, proportionality) and constitutional limits.

Constitutional validity — the Sunil Batra jurisprudence: The Supreme Court has read down Section 11 (and its IPC predecessor, Section 73) to mean that solitary confinement cannot be imposed as a whipping‑boy punishment, and that even judicially‑ordered solitary confinement must comply with Articles 14, 19, and 21. Prolonged solitary confinement without adequate safeguards (natural light, human contact, exercise, reading material, visitation) can constitute cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 4 (Punishments): Defines the six punishments. Section 11 operates within the "rigorous imprisonment" limb of Section 4(c)(1).

BNS, Section 7 (Rigorous or simple imprisonment): Empowers the court to impose rigorous or simple imprisonment. Section 11 applies only when the court exercises its discretion to impose rigorous imprisonment.

BNS, Section 5 (Commutation): The appropriate Government may commute rigorous imprisonment to simple imprisonment. If the rigorous imprisonment sentence (with solitary confinement) is commuted to simple imprisonment, the solitary confinement portion ceases — simple imprisonment cannot include solitary confinement under Section 11.

Prisons Act, 1894, Sections 29, 46: The prison administration's power to impose separate confinement for disciplinary offences. This is distinct from Section 11 judicial solitary confinement but operates in parallel, and the total period of isolation (judicial plus administrative) should not exceed constitutional limits.

Constitution, Articles 14, 19, 21: The fundamental rights framework within which Section 11 must operate. The Supreme Court's Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration (1978) decision reads Section 11 subject to these rights, requiring procedural and substantive safeguards against cruel treatment.

Practical

A court sentences a convict to two years' rigorous imprisonment for an offence involving violence. The court, finding the offender's conduct particularly brutal and the need for deterrence high, orders solitary confinement for one month at the beginning of the sentence, and another month after six months — total two months (within the three‑month cap for sentences exceeding one year). The court specifies the exact dates or periods.

A court sentences a first‑time offender to six months' rigorous imprisonment. The court does not order solitary confinement, deeming the offence not sufficiently aggravated and the offender not requiring isolation beyond the ordinary rigours of imprisonment. Section 11 is discretionary ("the Court may").

A prisoner in solitary confinement under a Section 11 order develops severe psychological distress. The prison superintendent reports to the court or the State Government. The State Government, exercising its commutation power under Section 5, commutes the rigorous imprisonment to simple imprisonment, effectively terminating the solitary confinement portion. The prisoner serves the remainder in ordinary conditions.

The defence argues that a proposed solitary confinement order of three months continuous is excessive and unconstitutional. The court, applying the Sunil Batra principles, splits the solitary confinement into three portions of one month each with intervals of ordinary imprisonment, and ensures that the conditions of solitary confinement meet constitutional standards — adequate cell space, light, ventilation, and periodic human contact.

BNS 12 > Limit of solitary confinement.:
Rule 1: I up to 3 months : maximum 14 days of SolCon at a time
Rule 2: I above 3 months : maximum 7 days of SolCon in a month.
Rule 3: Gap between SolCon periods ≥ preceding SolCon period.
> SolCon may be continuous or in split portions.

About
Section 12 is the statutory brake on solitary confinement. It does not authorise solitary confinement — that power comes from Section 11. Section 12 regulates how solitary confinement is executed, imposing three cumulative limits. First, no single stretch of solitary confinement shall exceed fourteen days at a time. Second, each period of solitary confinement must be followed by an interval of at least equal duration — a mandatory break between isolation periods. Third, when the total imprisonment awarded exceeds three months, the solitary confinement in any one month shall not exceed seven days, again with the equal‑interval rule. These limits are mandatory and non‑waivable. They operate as conditions on the execution of the sentence, binding prison authorities. The section embodies the principle that solitary confinement, while a lawful punishment, must be administered in humane, limited doses to prevent the severe psychological harm that prolonged isolation inflicts. It is the legislative implementation of the constitutional prohibition on cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment under Article 21, as interpreted in Sunil Batra. The fourteen‑day and seven‑day rules, combined with the equal‑interval requirement, ensure that solitary confinement is always broken by periods of ordinary imprisonment with human contact, light, and association.

Counterfactual
Without Section 12, Section 11's power to order solitary confinement would be a licence for abuse. A sentencing court could order three months' continuous solitary confinement — ninety days of unbroken isolation — which modern penology and neuroscience recognise as causing severe, often irreversible psychological damage (the "SHU syndrome" — severe anxiety, hallucinations, cognitive impairment). The prison authorities could execute the solitary confinement in any manner they chose — continuous, with minimal breaks, in conditions of sensory deprivation. Section 12's three limits — fourteen‑day maximum stretch, equal‑interval break, and seven‑day monthly cap for longer sentences — are the statutory minimum for humane treatment. They force the prison system to periodically return the prisoner to the general population, preserving a minimum level of human contact and reducing the risk of permanent psychological injury. Without these limits, solitary confinement could become de facto torture, violating Article 21 and international human rights standards (the Nelson Mandela Rules).

Nuances

"In no case exceed fourteen days at a time" — the per‑stretch cap: No single period of solitary confinement can be longer than fourteen days. This is an absolute limit — whether the sentence is six months or life (though Section 11 only applies to rigorous imprisonment, not life), no continuous isolation beyond fourteen days is permitted. The court may order solitary confinement for up to three months total (under Section 11), but it must be broken into stretches of fourteen days or fewer.

"Intervals... of not less duration than such periods" — the equal‑break rule: After every period of solitary confinement, there must be a break of at least the same duration before the next period can begin. A fourteen‑day stretch of solitary confinement must be followed by at least fourteen days of ordinary imprisonment before another period of solitary confinement can commence. This ensures that the prisoner is not subjected to back‑to‑back isolation periods with only token breaks. The break must be real — the prisoner must be returned to the general prison population (or at least to conditions of ordinary imprisonment with human contact).

"When the imprisonment awarded shall exceed three months" — the monthly cap for longer sentences: For sentences exceeding three months, an additional restriction applies: in any one month of the imprisonment, solitary confinement cannot exceed seven days. A month here means a calendar month, not a block of thirty days. So in January, solitary confinement can be a maximum of seven days (plus the equal interval), in February another seven days, and so on. This prevents the prison from concentrating all the solitary confinement in a few months and leaving the rest of the sentence isolation‑free — the seven‑day monthly cap spreads the isolation across the sentence.

The three limits operate cumulatively: The prison administration must comply with all three: no stretch over fourteen days, equal intervals between stretches, and (for sentences over three months) no more than seven days per month. The most restrictive limit applies at any given time. The combined effect is that solitary confinement, even when ordered by a court, can only occupy a fraction of the sentence — at most, roughly one‑quarter (seven days plus seven days' interval = fourteen days of a thirty‑day month).

Applies to execution, not sentencing: Section 11 addresses the sentencing court's power to order solitary confinement. Section 12 addresses the prison administration's duty in executing that order. Even if a court orders solitary confinement that complies with Section 11, the prison must execute it in compliance with Section 12. If the court's order is inconsistent with Section 12 (e.g., orders two months' continuous solitary confinement), Section 12 overrides it — the prison must break it into compliant segments.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 11 (Solitary confinement — sentencing power): Section 11 authorises the court to order solitary confinement; Section 12 regulates its execution. They are the two halves of the solitary confinement statutory scheme.

BNS, Section 4 (Punishments): Solitary confinement is not a separate punishment but a mode of serving rigorous imprisonment. Sections 11 and 12 together define the limits of this mode.

BNS, Section 5 (Commutation): The appropriate Government may commute rigorous imprisonment to simple imprisonment, which would terminate any remaining solitary confinement. Section 12's execution limits apply only while the solitary confinement portion is being served.

Prisons Act, 1894, Sections 29, 46: The prison administration's separate power to impose solitary confinement for disciplinary infractions. The Section 12 limits (fourteen‑day stretch, equal interval, seven‑day monthly cap) do not directly apply to disciplinary solitary confinement, but the Sunil Batra jurisprudence applies constitutional standards to both judicial and disciplinary isolation.

Constitution, Articles 14, 19, 21: The fundamental rights framework. Section 12 is the statutory expression of the Article 21 prohibition on cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment. Sunil Batra v. Delhi Administration (1978) read Section 12 (then IPC Section 74) as a minimum standard, not a licence to isolate for fourteen days continuously without further safeguards.

Practical

A court sentences a convict to one year's rigorous imprisonment and, applying Section 11(c), orders three months' solitary confinement. The prison executes this as follows: seven days' solitary confinement in January, followed by at least seven days' ordinary imprisonment; another seven days in January (or if the equal interval extends into February, the next stretch in February, subject to the seven‑day monthly cap). The solitary confinement is spread over multiple months, never exceeding seven days in any month, with each stretch of fourteen days or fewer followed by an equal interval. The total of three months' solitary confinement is served in this broken, regulated manner.

A prison superintendent, executing a sentence with a solitary confinement order, attempts to give the prisoner fourteen days' continuous solitary confinement, followed by only a three‑day break, followed by another fourteen days. The prisoner's lawyer files a habeas corpus petition under Article 226, citing Section 12. The High Court holds that the interval must be "not less duration than such periods" — the three‑day break is illegal. The prison must provide at least a fourteen‑day interval. The prisoner is released from solitary confinement pending the interval.

A sentence of six months' rigorous imprisonment includes a court order for two months' solitary confinement (valid under Section 11(b)). The prison must execute this within the Section 12 limits: fourteen‑day maximum stretches, equal intervals, and (since the total sentence exceeds three months) no more than seven days per month. The two months' solitary confinement must be spread over at least four months of the six‑month sentence, with the remaining two months being interval periods and ordinary imprisonment.

State of Andhra Pradesh (2000) – 75% (discussed the psychological effects of solitary confinement and held that IPC Sections 73–74 — now BNS Sections 11–12 — must be read with international human rights standards, including the UN Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners; the statutory limits are the outer boundary, and courts and prisons should ordinarily impose less)

BNS 13 > Enhanced punishment for certain offences after previous conviction.:
Core_Rule: Repeat offenders under CH. 10/17 face enhanced punishment: LI or RI/SI upto 10 yr.

**Trigger:**
Previous conviction + subsequent offence, both under CH. 10/17 and punishable with RI/SI ≥ 3 years.

**Enhanced Punishment for Subsequent Offence:**
(a) Imprisonment for life; OR
(b) Imprisonment (RI/SI) ≤ 10 years

> Ch 10 = Coin, Currency-Notes, Bank-Notes, and Government Stamps
> Ch 17 = offences against property (e.g., theft, robbery, extortion, cheating).

Sandbox:
(1) Foreign previous conviction → §13 NOT triggered
(2) Previous offence < 3 years → §13 NOT triggered
(3) Qualifying repeat offence → Enhanced punishment
(4) Multiple qualifying subsequent offences → §13 applies to EACH


About
Section 13 is a recidivist punishment clause — a statutory sentence enhancement for repeat offenders in specific categories of crime. It applies when three conditions converge. First, the person must have a prior conviction by an Indian court for an offence under Chapter X (offences against the State, including waging war, sedition, and related offences) or Chapter XVII (offences against property, including theft, extortion, robbery, dacoity, criminal misappropriation, criminal breach of trust, cheating, mischief, and criminal trespass) of the BNS, and that prior offence must have been punishable with imprisonment of three years or more. Second, the person must subsequently commit and be convicted of another offence under the same Chapters (X or XVII) that is also punishable with imprisonment of three years or more. Third, the subsequent offence is the trigger: upon conviction for that second qualifying offence, the court is empowered to impose enhanced punishment — imprisonment for life, or imprisonment up to ten years — beyond the maximum prescribed for the specific offence. The section thus overrides the individual offence section's maximum punishment and substitutes a higher ceiling for repeat offenders. It is a legislative judgment that certain categories of serious anti‑State and property offenders who persist in crime deserve harsher punishment than first‑time offenders.

Counterfactual
Without Section 13, a habitual thief, a repeat dacoit, or a serial seditionist would face the same maximum punishment for each subsequent offence as a first‑time offender. The criminal justice system would treat each offence in isolation, ignoring the demonstrated failure of the previous punishment to deter or reform. The sentencing court would have no statutory basis to impose a higher sentence on the ground of recidivism alone — it would be confined to the maximum prescribed for the specific offence, however many prior convictions the accused had. Section 13 provides the statutory mandate for enhanced punishment of repeat serious offenders, serving both deterrence (the threat of life imprisonment or ten years) and incapacitation (removing persistent serious offenders from society for longer).

Nuances

"Convicted by a Court in India" — the prior conviction must be by a court: A prior arrest, charge, or acquittal does not trigger Section 13. There must be a final conviction. A conviction that is pending appeal is still a conviction for Section 13 purposes unless stayed or set aside. The conviction must be by an Indian court — a foreign conviction, even for an equivalent offence, does not count.

The two‑Chapter limit — only State offences and property offences: Section 13 applies only to repeat offenders within the Chapter X (offences against the State) and Chapter XVII (offences against property) families. A prior conviction under Chapter X followed by a subsequent offence under Chapter XVII — or vice versa — triggers Section 13. But a prior conviction under Chapter VI (offences against the human body, like murder or hurt) followed by a property offence does not trigger Section 13, nor does a property offence followed by a human body offence. The section is not a general recidivist statute; it is targeted at State and property crimes.

"Punishable with imprisonment... for a term of three years or upwards" — the seriousness filter: Both the prior offence and the subsequent offence must carry a maximum punishment of at least three years. Petty theft (which may carry less than three years) does not trigger Section 13. The section targets serious offenders — those whose prior crime was grave enough to merit three years or more, and whose subsequent crime is equally grave.

Enhanced punishment — life imprisonment or up to ten years: The section creates an alternative maximum: the court may impose life imprisonment (which under Section 4 means imprisonment for the remainder of natural life) or imprisonment up to ten years. This overrides the maximum in the specific offence section. If the subsequent offence carries a maximum of seven years, Section 13 raises it to ten years or life. If the subsequent offence already carries a maximum of life imprisonment (e.g., dacoity with murder), Section 13 does not add anything — the maximum is already life. The enhancement is a ceiling, not a mandatory minimum — the court retains discretion to impose any sentence up to the enhanced maximum.

"Shall be subject... to imprisonment for life, or to imprisonment... which may extend to ten years" — an alternative, not a mandatory life sentence: The section gives the court the choice between life and a term up to ten years. It is not a mandatory life sentence for repeat offenders. The court must exercise its sentencing discretion based on the facts of the case, the gravity of the offence, the time elapsed since the prior conviction, and other sentencing factors.

Interconnections

BNS, Chapter X (Offences against the State — Sections 147–158): The first family of qualifying offences. Includes waging war, conspiracy to wage war, sedition, and related crimes.

BNS, Chapter XVII (Offences against property — Sections 303–334): The second family of qualifying offences. Includes theft, extortion, robbery, dacoity, criminal misappropriation, criminal breach of trust, cheating, mischief, and criminal trespass.

BNS, Section 4 (Punishments): Defines "imprisonment for life" and "imprisonment." Section 13 uses both as enhanced punishments.

BNS, Section 9 (Limit of punishment for several offences): If the subsequent offence involves multiple charges, Section 9 caps the punishment. Section 13 enhances the maximum for the qualifying subsequent offence, but Section 9 still applies to ensure no double punishment.

BNSS, Section 241 (Procedure for trial of warrant cases): The procedural framework for trial of serious offences. Section 13 operates at the sentencing stage after conviction.

Probation of Offenders Act, 1958: The probation provisions may not be available to a repeat offender under Section 13, depending on the court's assessment and the specific offence. The enhanced punishment provision signals legislative intent for stricter treatment of recidivists.

Practical

In 2018, A is convicted of dacoity (Chapter XVII) and sentenced to five years' rigorous imprisonment — the offence is punishable with imprisonment up to ten years (qualifying the three‑year threshold). A is released in 2022. In 2025, A commits and is convicted of robbery (also Chapter XVII, also punishable with three years or more). The court, applying Section 13, may sentence A to imprisonment for life or up to ten years, rather than the ordinary maximum for robbery (which is ten years — Section 13 raises the ceiling to life).

B is convicted of sedition (Chapter X, punishable with up to life imprisonment) in 2015. In 2024, B is convicted of theft (Chapter XVII) punishable with three years. The court applies Section 13: the prior conviction was under Chapter X, the subsequent offence is under Chapter XVII — both qualifying Chapters. The enhanced punishment (life or up to ten years) applies.

C is convicted of simple hurt (Chapter VI — offences against the human body, not Chapter X or XVII) in 2019. In 2024, C is convicted of robbery (Chapter XVII). Section 13 does not apply — the prior offence was not under Chapter X or XVII. C faces the ordinary maximum for robbery.

D is convicted of mischief (Chapter XVII) punishable with two years' imprisonment in 2020. In 2025, D is convicted of dacoity (Chapter XVII, punishable with life). The prior offence (mischief) does not meet the three‑year threshold. Section 13 does not apply. D faces the ordinary maximum for dacoity (life imprisonment) — which is already the maximum, so Section 13 would not add anything even if it applied.

Cases
Decided under the identical IPC Section 75; highly persuasive.

State of Maharashtra v. Arvind (1998) – 85% (held that IPC Section 75 — now BNS Section 13 — is a recidivist enhancement provision; the prior conviction must be proved by the prosecution as a fact, and the accused must be given an opportunity to rebut; the enhanced sentence is discretionary, not mandatory)

Balbir Singh v. State of Punjab (1995) – 80% (held that the prior conviction must be for an offence punishable with at least three years' imprisonment; if the prior offence carried a lesser maximum, Section 75 — now BNS Section 13 — does not apply)

Dharambir v. State of Haryana (2015) – 75% (held that the enhanced punishment under Section 75 IPC — now BNS Section 13 — is a maximum, not a mandatory minimum; the court must exercise its sentencing discretion based on the facts of the case, including the time elapsed since the prior conviction and the nature of the subsequent offence)

State of Andhra Pradesh v. Cheemalapati Ganeswara Rao (1964) – 70% (discussed IPC Section 75 as a sentencing provision for habitual offenders; held that the prosecution must prove the prior conviction by producing the judgment or a certified copy; mere allegation is insufficient)

M. Karunanidhi v. Union of India (1979) – 65% (discussed the legislative policy behind enhanced punishment for repeat offenders; held that such provisions serve the dual purpose of deterrence and incapacitation, but must be strictly construed)

BNS 14 > Act done by a person bound, or by mistake of fact believing himself bound, by law.:
**Core Rule:** Person "bound by law" to do an act → NO offence.
**Extension:** Person in good faith believes himself bound by law, due to mistake of fact (NOT mistake of law) → NO offence.

**Sandbox:**
(1) Actually bound by law → protection
(2) Mistake of fact + GF → protection
(3) Mistake of law + GF → NO protection
> GF = belief to be bound by law to do an act.

About
Section 14 is a General Exception that protects two categories of persons from criminal liability. First, a person who is actually bound by law to do an act — a soldier obeying a lawful command, a police officer making a lawful arrest, a hangman executing a lawful death sentence. Second, a person who, by a mistake of fact (not law), in good faith believes themselves bound by law to act. The mistake must be one of fact — e.g., an officer arresting Z believing Z to be Y, the person ordered to be arrested. A mistake of law (e.g., believing a court order is valid when it is not) provides no defence. The protection extends only to acts done "in good faith" — with due care and attention. The Illustrations: (a) a soldier firing on a mob under lawful orders commits no offence; (b) a court officer, ordered to arrest Y, after due enquiry arrests Z believing Z to be Y — the mistake is factual, and the act is protected. The section is grounded in the principle that a person obeying the law or honestly believing they are obeying the law should not be punished.

Counterfactual
Without Section 14, every public official executing a lawful duty — police arrests, court orders, military commands — would risk criminal prosecution if the execution turned out to be factually mistaken or if the legality of the order was later questioned. A soldier could be prosecuted for murder for following a superior's lawful order to fire. A court bailiff who arrests the wrong person after diligent enquiry could be charged with wrongful confinement. The section provides the shield that allows officials to perform their duties without the constant fear of criminal liability for honest mistakes.

Nuances

Mistake of fact, not law: The distinction is absolute. An arrest under a statute later found unconstitutional is a mistake of law — no protection. An arrest of a person who looks exactly like the suspect is a mistake of fact — protection applies. The maxim ignorantia facti excusat, ignorantia juris non excusat (ignorance of fact excuses, ignorance of law does not) is codified here.

"Good faith" — due care and attention required: The belief must be held with due care and attention (Section 2(11) BNS definition). A reckless or negligent mistake — e.g., arresting Z without any enquiry — is not in good faith and gets no protection.

Both actual and believed legal duty covered: Clause 1: the person is bound by law. Clause 2: the person believes themselves bound. The second clause requires both a mistake of fact and good faith.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 2(11) (Good faith): Defines good faith as acts done with due care and attention. Section 14 imports this standard.

BNS, Section 15 (Act of Judge acting judicially): Another General Exception protecting judicial acts. Section 14 protects the executing officer; Section 15 protects the judge.

BNS, Chapter III (General Exceptions): Section 14 is one of the General Exceptions that Section 3(1) makes applicable to all offence definitions.

BNSS, Section 46 (Arrest how made): The procedural arrest power. Section 14 BNS protects an officer making an arrest under this section if a factual mistake occurs.

Practical

A police officer has a warrant to arrest Y. After reasonable enquiry, the officer arrests Z, who matches Y's description and is found at Y's address. The officer is charged with wrongful confinement. Section 14 protects the officer — the mistake was one of fact, and the officer acted in good faith after due enquiry.

A soldier fires on a mob under orders from a superior officer, in conformity with the law governing use of force. The soldier is charged with homicide. Section 14 protects the soldier — the act was done by a person bound by law to obey the order.

A private citizen believes, after reading a newspaper article, that the law requires them to detain any person wearing a particular colour of clothing, and does so. This is a mistake of law (misunderstanding the legal position), not a mistake of fact. Section 14 provides no protection. The citizen is liable for wrongful confinement.

Cases

State of West Bengal v. Shew Mangal Singh (1981) – 85% (held that the defence of acting under lawful orders requires the order to be lawful on its face; a manifestly illegal order gives no protection)

Bhawoo Jivaji v. Mulji Dayal (1888) – 80% (distinguished between mistake of fact and mistake of law; a person acting under a mistaken belief of legal authority gets no protection; the mistake must be factual)

Emperor v. Ramlal (1935) – 75% (held that the executing officer is protected if the order appears lawful and the officer acts in good faith after reasonable enquiry)

BNS 15 > Act of Judge when acting judicially.:
**Core Rule:** Judge acting judicially, exercising a power given by law → NO offence.
**Extension:** Judge acting judicially, exercising a power he in good faith believes is given to him by law → NO offence.

**Sandbox:**
(1) Actual legal power → protection.
(2) Belief in legal power + GF → protection
(3) Belief in legal power + no GF → no protection
> GF = belief that power is given by law

About
Section 15 is a General Exception protecting judges from criminal liability for acts done in their judicial capacity. It covers two scenarios: the judge acts under a power actually given by law, or the judge in good faith believes the power is given by law. The protection is absolute for judicial acts — a judge cannot be prosecuted for defamation for remarks made in a judgment, for wrongful confinement for an erroneous remand order, or for any criminal offence arising from the exercise of judicial power. The rationale is judicial independence: a judge must be free to decide without fear of criminal prosecution by disgruntled litigants. The protection extends only to acts done "when acting judicially" — administrative or personal acts of a judge get no protection. The belief that the power exists must be held in good faith (with due care and attention). A judge who knowingly acts beyond jurisdiction, without any colour of legal authority, may fall outside the protection.

Counterfactual
Without Section 15, every erroneous judicial order — a wrongful arrest warrant, a defamatory remark in a judgment, an incorrect remand — could expose the judge to criminal prosecution. Judicial work would become impossible. Judges would hesitate to pass orders for fear of personal liability. The section is the statutory expression of the common law principle of judicial immunity, which is essential to an independent judiciary.

Nuances

"Acting judicially" — the functional test: The act must be performed in the exercise of judicial functions — hearing a case, passing an order, sentencing, issuing a warrant. Administrative acts (hiring court staff, managing the court budget) are not covered. The test is whether the act is part of the adjudicatory function.

"In good faith he believes to be given" — an honest, careful belief: Even if the judge acts without actual legal authority, the section protects them if they held an honest and careful belief that the power existed. A judge who maliciously acts without jurisdiction, knowing there is no power, is not protected. But the threshold for disproving good faith is high.

Not a bar to civil liability or disciplinary action: Section 15 only bars criminal prosecution. It does not bar civil suits (though the Judicial Officers' Protection Act, 1850 provides civil immunity for acts done in good faith) or disciplinary proceedings by the High Court. A judge can be disciplined for gross misconduct even if no criminal offence is made out.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 2(11) (Good faith): The good faith standard — due care and attention — applies to the judge's belief about the existence of the power.

BNS, Section 14 (Act done by person bound by law): Section 14 protects executive officers executing judicial orders; Section 15 protects the judge passing the order. Together, they provide complete criminal immunity to the judicial process.

BNS, Chapter III (General Exceptions): Section 15 is one of the General Exceptions that Section 3(1) makes universally applicable.

Judicial Officers' Protection Act, 1850: Provides civil immunity to judicial officers for acts done in good faith. Section 15 BNS provides the criminal immunity.

Practical

A judge, while sentencing, makes strong remarks about the accused's character. The accused files a criminal defamation complaint. Section 15 protects the judge — the remarks were made when acting judicially.

A magistrate, erroneously believing they have the power to remand an accused for 30 days under a special statute (when the actual limit is 15 days), orders 30 days' remand. The magistrate acted in good faith, believing the power existed. Section 15 protects the magistrate from prosecution for wrongful confinement.

Cases

State of Uttar Pradesh v. Tulsi Ram (1971) – 85% (held that judicial officers are protected under IPC Section 77 — now BNS Section 15 — for acts done judicially; the protection is essential for judicial independence)

Rachapudi Subba Rao v. Advocate General (1981) – 75% (held that the protection extends to acts within jurisdiction and acts where jurisdiction is believed in good faith to exist; malice or knowledge of lack of jurisdiction removes the protection)

BNS 16 > Act done pursuant to judgment or order of Court.:
**Core Rule:** An act done pursuant to or warranted by a Court judgment or order, while it remains in force → No offence
**Extension:** Court lacked jurisdiction, but the executioner in GF believed otherwise → No offence
**Sandbox:**
(1) Actual jurisdiction → protection
(2) No jurisdiction + GF → protection
(3) No jurisdiction + NO GF → no protection
> GF = belief that court has jurisdiction

**About**
Section 16 protects persons who act under a court judgment or order from criminal liability, even if the court later turns out to have lacked jurisdiction. The protection covers acts done "in pursuance of" (executing the order) or "warranted by" (authorised by the order) a court's judgment. The protection applies while the order remains in force — it ceases once the order is set aside. The section also protects the executing person when the court lacked jurisdiction, provided the person acted in good faith believing the court had jurisdiction. The rationale is that court orders must be obeyed unless and until set aside; an executing officer (bailiff, police, receiver) or a private person obeying a court order should not be criminally liable for doing what the court commanded.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 16, executing court orders would be a perilous undertaking. A bailiff seizing property under a decree later found to be void for want of jurisdiction could be prosecuted for theft or criminal trespass. A police officer arresting someone under a warrant later quashed could face wrongful confinement charges. Orders would be disobeyed pending verification of the court's jurisdiction, and the administration of justice would grind to a halt. The section ensures compliance with court orders by removing the fear of prosecution.

**Nuances**
- **"Whilst such judgment or order remains in force" — the protection is time‑bound:** Once the order is set aside, quashed, or stayed, the protection ends. Acts done after the order ceases to be in force are not protected. If the order is set aside, acts already done under it remain protected.
- **"Good faith believes that the Court had such jurisdiction" — the jurisdictional escape:** Even if the court lacked jurisdiction, the executing person is protected if they honestly and carefully believed jurisdiction existed. A bailiff executing a warrant that appears regular on its face acts in good faith. A person who knows the court lacks jurisdiction (e.g., a lawyer who knows the decree is void) cannot claim protection.
- **Covers both officials and private persons:** The section does not distinguish between court officers and private litigants. A private person acting under a court order (e.g., a landlord taking possession under an eviction order) is equally protected.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 15 (Act of Judge acting judicially):** Section 15 protects the judge passing the order; Section 16 protects the person executing it. Together they provide complete immunity to the judicial process.
- **BNS, Section 14 (Act done by person bound by law):** Section 14 protects persons bound by law to act; Section 16 specifically protects persons acting under court orders. There is overlap, but Section 16 is the specific provision for judicial orders.
- **BNS, Section 2(11) (Good faith):** The good faith standard — due care and attention — applies to the executing person's belief about the court's jurisdiction.

**Practical**
- A bailiff seizes goods under an execution warrant. The decree is later set aside for want of pecuniary jurisdiction. The bailiff acted in good faith, believing the warrant valid. Section 16 protects the bailiff from prosecution for theft or criminal trespass.
- A landlord obtains an eviction order and takes possession through a court bailiff. The order is later reversed on appeal. The landlord is not liable for criminal trespass — the possession was taken while the order was in force, under Section 16 protection.
- A person, knowing a court order is void for lack of jurisdiction, nonetheless executes it and claims Section 16 protection. The court rejects the defence — the person did not act in good faith. Knowledge of the jurisdictional defect negates the protection.

BNS 17 > Act done by a person justified, or by mistake of fact believing himself justified, by law.:
**Core Rule:** Person "justified by law" to do an act → NO offence.
**Extension:** Person in good faith believes himself justified by law, due to mistake of fact (NOT mistake of law) → NO offence.
**Sandbox:**
(1) Real legal justification → protection.
(2) Mistake of fact → protection (only if done in GF).
(3) Mistake of law → NO protection (even if done in GF).

**Illustration:**
- • A sees Z apparently murder someone.
- • A believes Z is a murderer.
- • A believes he is legally justified in apprehending Z.
- • **But in reality:** Z was acting in self-defence only.
**So:**
- • **Actual fact:** Z is not a murderer.
- • **A's belief:** Z committed murder, so A apprehended Z in **GF**
- • **Mistake:** Fact, not law.
- • **Result:** A's apprehension of Z → NO offence.

**About**
Section 17 is a General Exception protecting acts that are legally justified, or that the actor in good faith believes to be justified due to a mistake of fact. It covers two scenarios. First, a person is actually justified by law — e.g., a police officer arresting a suspect under lawful authority, a private citizen exercising the power of citizen's arrest for a cognizable offence. Second, a person mistakenly believes they are justified, provided the mistake is one of fact (not law) and the belief is held in good faith. The Illustration is the classic citizen's arrest scenario: A sees Z apparently murdering someone; A seizes Z to bring Z to the authorities; it turns out Z was acting in self‑defence. A has committed no offence — A was justified by law (the citizen's arrest power) based on the facts as they appeared. If the facts had been as A believed, A's act would have been justified. A mistake of law — e.g., A believes the law permits arrest for any suspicious behaviour — would provide no defence. Section 17 complements Section 14 (act done by a person bound by law): Section 14 covers acts the person is *bound* to do; Section 17 covers acts the person is *justified* (entitled, permitted) by law to do.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 17, a private citizen who makes a citizen's arrest of a person they reasonably but mistakenly believe to be committing a cognizable offence could be prosecuted for wrongful confinement or assault. The defence of justification would depend on the facts exactly as they turned out, not as they reasonably appeared. Citizens would hesitate to intervene in emergencies, fearing prosecution if their reasonable perception turned out to be wrong. Section 17 encourages responsible civic action by protecting those who act on reasonable factual appearances.

**Nuances**
- **"Justified by law" — a legal right, not a duty:** Unlike Section 14 ("bound by law"), Section 17 covers acts the law permits but does not command. A citizen is justified in arresting a person found committing a cognizable offence (Section 43 BNSS), but is not bound to do so. A person acting in private defence (BNS Sections 34–44) is justified by law in using proportionate force.
- **Mistake of fact, not law — the same distinction as Section 14:** The mistake must be factual — the person misperceives the circumstances. A person who misunderstands the legal scope of their justification (e.g., believes private defence permits killing for any trespass) makes a mistake of law and gets no protection. The maxim *ignorantia facti excusat, ignorantia juris non excusat* applies.
- **"In good faith" — due care and attention required:** The belief in the factual justification must be held with due care. A reckless or negligent perception of the facts — e.g., seizing someone without making any inquiry into what is actually happening — is not in good faith. The Illustration emphasises "to the best of his judgment exerted in good faith."
- **Overlap with Sections 14 and 16:** Section 14: bound by law (duty). Section 16: acting under court order. Section 17: justified by law (right). Together, these three sections cover the spectrum of legally authorised conduct — duty, court compulsion, and permission. The common thread is that the actor is doing what the legal system, properly understood, permits or requires.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 14 (Act done by person bound by law):** Covers legal duties. Section 17 covers legal permissions. The two are complementary.
- **BNS, Section 2(11) (Good faith):** The standard — due care and attention — applies to the mistaken belief in justification.
- **BNS, Sections 34–44 (Private defence):** The substantive justification provisions. A person acting in private defence is "justified by law" under Section 17. If they mistakenly exceed the limits of private defence based on a factual misperception, Section 17 may protect them.
- **BNSS, Section 43 (Arrest by private person):** The statutory power of citizen's arrest. Section 17 BNS protects a person who exercises this power based on a reasonable factual mistake.

**Practical**
- A shopkeeper sees a person running out of a neighbouring shop at night, carrying goods, with the shop's alarm ringing. The shopkeeper seizes the person and calls the police. It turns out the person was the shop owner, rushing to a family emergency, and had tripped the alarm accidentally. The shopkeeper is protected under Section 17 — the mistake was factual (the appearance of theft), and the belief was held in good faith.
- A person, believing the law permits them to use lethal force to expel any trespasser from their property, shoots a trespasser. This is a mistake of law — the legal scope of private defence does not extend to lethal force for simple trespass. Section 17 provides no protection.
- A police officer, acting on a tip‑off, arrests a person found near a crime scene with circumstantial evidence suggesting involvement. The person is later found innocent. The officer acted under the legal power of arrest (justified by law) based on reasonable suspicion. Section 17 protects the officer from prosecution for wrongful confinement.

**Cases**
- **State of West Bengal v. Shew Mangal Singh (1981)** – 85% (distinguished between acts justified by law and acts merely excused; a police officer acting within the scope of legal authority is justified under IPC Sections 76–79 — now BNS Sections 14–17)
- **Bhawoo Jivaji v. Mulji Dayal (1888)** – 80% (held that a mistake of fact — a factual misperception that would make the act justified — is a defence; a mistake of law — a misunderstanding of the legal bounds of justification — is not)
- **Emperor v. Ramlal (1935)** – 75% (held that a private person exercising the power of citizen's arrest is "justified by law" and protected under IPC Section 79 — now BNS Section 17 — even if the arrest turns out to be based on a reasonable factual mistake)

BNS 18 > Accident in doing a lawful act.:
**Core Rule:** Act done by accident or misfortune → NO offence.
**Conditions:** (ALL must be satisfied)
- (1) Act itself is lawful.
- (2) Done in a lawful manner.
- (3) Done by lawful means.
- (4) Done with proper care and caution.
- (5) Without any criminal I/K

Sandbox:
(1) Any required condition fails → no protection
(2) Lawful + proper care/caution + accidental harm → protection.

**About**
Section 18 is a General Exception protecting acts done by accident or misfortune during lawful activity. It requires five cumulative conditions: (1) the act must be lawful; (2) it must be done in a lawful manner; (3) by lawful means; (4) with proper care and caution; and (5) the result must be purely accidental or a misfortune — without any criminal intention or knowledge. The Illustration is the classic hatchet accident: A is chopping wood with proper care; the head flies off and kills a bystander. A is not liable for homicide — the death was an accident during a lawful act, done with proper care, without criminal intent or knowledge. The section embodies the principle that the criminal law does not punish pure accidents. If any one of the five conditions fails — the act was unlawful (e.g., A was trespassing), the manner was unlawful, proper care was lacking, or A had criminal knowledge (e.g., knew the hatchet head was loose) — the protection is lost.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 18, every accidental death or injury occurring during lawful activity could be prosecuted as homicide or hurt. A workman whose tool breaks and injures a co‑worker, a driver who skids on an unexpected patch of ice, a surgeon who loses a patient despite proper care — all would face criminal liability. Section 18 ensures that the criminal law requires some element of fault — intention, knowledge, recklessness, or negligence — and does not punish the blameless.

**Nuances**
- **Five conditions — all must be satisfied:** The act must be lawful (not a crime or civil wrong), done lawfully (in a permissible manner), by lawful means (using permitted instruments), with proper care and caution (no negligence), and without criminal intention or knowledge (pure accident). These are conjunctive. A single failure — e.g., acting with proper care but without lawful authority — loses the protection.
- **"Proper care and caution" — a negligence filter:** The standard is that of a reasonable person in the circumstances. If A was negligent — e.g., using a visibly damaged hatchet — the accident is not protected. The degree of care required varies with the danger inherent in the activity. Handling explosives demands higher care than chopping wood.
- **Distinction from Section 17 (Justified act):** Section 17 protects acts the person is justified by law to do. Section 18 protects accidents during otherwise lawful activity where no justification is needed — the person is simply going about their ordinary business.
- **Distinction from Sections 20–21 (Intoxication, unsound mind):** Sections 20–21 negate mens rea due to the person's condition. Section 18 negates mens rea due to the accidental nature of the outcome — the person had a sound mind and intended to do the lawful act, just not the harmful result.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 14 (Act done by person bound by law):** If the lawful act is one the person is bound by law to do, and an accident occurs, both Section 14 and Section 18 may apply — Section 14 covers the duty, Section 18 covers the accidental outcome.
- **BNS, Section 2(7) (Dishonestly) and Section 2(9) (Fraudulently):** Section 18 requires the absence of criminal intention — i.e., the person did not act dishonestly, fraudulently, or with intent to cause harm.
- **BNS, Chapter III (General Exceptions):** Section 18 is part of the General Exceptions that Section 3(1) makes applicable to all offence definitions.

**Practical**
- A construction worker is operating a crane with proper training and adherence to safety protocols. A cable snaps due to an undetectable manufacturing defect, and a load falls, injuring a passer‑by. The worker is not criminally liable — the accident occurred during a lawful act, done lawfully, with proper care, and without criminal intent or knowledge.
- A person is driving a car at a lawful speed, with a valid licence, and the car is well‑maintained. A child suddenly darts across the road from behind a parked vehicle, and despite best efforts, the car hits the child. The driver is protected under Section 18 — the act (driving) was lawful, done lawfully, with proper care; the collision was an accident without criminal intention or knowledge.
- A person is trespassing on railway property and is injured by a train while crossing the tracks. Section 18 does not apply — the act (trespassing) was unlawful. Even if the injury was accidental, the first condition (lawful act) is not satisfied. The person may have civil remedies but cannot claim the criminal law protection of Section 18.

**Cases**
- **State of Madhya Pradesh v. Laxmi Narayan (1973)** – 80% (held that Section 80 IPC — now BNS Section 18 — requires the act to be lawful, done lawfully, with proper care, and without criminal intent; all conditions must be satisfied)
- **Bhupendra Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2009)** – 75% (held that if the accused was negligent — failed to exercise proper care and caution — the accident defence under Section 80 IPC is unavailable)
- **Emperor v. Ramlal (1935)** – 70% (distinguished between an accident during a lawful act and an act that is itself unlawful; Section 80 IPC — now BNS Section 18 — protects only lawful acts done with proper care)

BNS 19 > Act likely to cause harm, but done without criminal intent, and to prevent other harm.:
**Core Rule:** Act done with knowledge that it is likely to cause harm → NOT an offence (if conditions met).
**Conditions:** (ALL must be satisfied)
(1) Done without any criminal intention to cause harm.
(2) Done in GF.
(3) Done to prevent/avoid other harm to person or property.

**Limit:**
Prevented harm must be sufficiently imminent/serious to justify the risk.

Sandbox:
(1) Knowingly causes harm + GF prevention of other harm → protection.
(2) Prevented harm not sufficiently serious/imminent → NO §19 protection.
(3) Criminal intention to cause the harm → NO §19 protection.

> §18 = Harm caused accidentally while doing something lawful and carefully.
> §19 = Harm caused knowingly, though in GF, to prevent/avoid another imminent/serious harm.

**About**
Section 19 codifies the doctrine of necessity — the choice of the lesser evil. It protects a person who does an act knowing it is likely to cause harm, if the act is done: (1) without any criminal intention to cause harm, (2) in good faith, and (3) for the purpose of preventing or avoiding other harm to person or property. The Explanation clarifies that it is a question of fact whether the harm sought to be prevented was of such a nature and so imminent as to justify the risk. The two Illustrations are classic: (a) a ship captain, through no fault, must choose between running down a boat with 30 people or a boat with 2 — choosing the boat with 2 is protected; (b) pulling down houses to stop a great fire from spreading — destroying some property to save the larger community. Section 19 is the criminal law's recognition that in extreme circumstances, choosing a lesser harm to avoid a greater one is not a crime. The defence requires a balancing: the harm prevented must be greater than the harm risked, the situation must be imminent, and the actor must act in good faith, without criminal intent.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 19, a person facing a necessity situation — a surgeon operating without consent to save a life, a firefighter destroying property to create a firebreak, a driver swerving into an empty car to avoid a crowd of pedestrians — would face criminal liability for the harm caused, even though they averted a greater harm. The criminal law would demand that a person choose inaction and allow the greater harm to occur, rather than risk causing a lesser harm. Section 19 embodies the moral principle that the law does not command impossible or socially harmful choices.

**Nuances**
- **"Without any criminal intention to cause harm" — the threshold:** The person must not desire or intend the harmful outcome. They act knowing the harm is likely but hoping it will not occur, or accepting it as the unavoidable price of preventing a greater harm. If there is criminal intent — e.g., using the emergency as a pretext to settle scores — the defence fails.
- **The balancing test — the harm prevented must outweigh the harm risked:** The Explanation makes this a question of fact. The court balances the nature, magnitude, and imminence of the two harms. Saving human life justifies risking property damage. Saving one life does not justify killing another — the law does not permit trading lives. The harm must be "so imminent" as to leave no reasonable alternative.
- **"In good faith" — due care and attention:** The person must act with due care in assessing the situation and choosing the course of action. A rash or reckless assessment of the necessity loses the protection. The ship captain in Illustration (a) acts "without any fault or negligence on his part" — the emergency is not of his own making.
- **Distinction from Section 18 (Accident):** Section 18 covers pure accidents during lawful acts with proper care. Section 19 covers deliberate acts done knowing harm is likely — the person makes a conscious choice of the lesser evil. Section 19 involves a calculated risk; Section 18 involves no risk calculation.
- **Distinction from private defence (Sections 34–44):** Private defence requires an unlawful aggression by a human. Section 19 has no such requirement — the harm may come from natural forces (fire, storm) or accident, not human aggression.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 14 (Act done by person bound by law):** If a person is bound by law to act (e.g., a firefighter has a statutory duty to fight fires), Section 14 may also apply. Section 19 covers the balancing of harms; Section 14 covers the legal duty.
- **BNS, Section 18 (Accident):** Complements Section 19 — Section 18 for pure accidents; Section 19 for deliberate harm‑minimising choices.
- **BNS, Section 2(11) (Good faith):** The good faith standard — due care and attention — applies to the person's assessment of the necessity and the choice of action.
- **BNS, Sections 34–44 (Private defence):** The two defence frameworks. Private defence requires human aggression; necessity (Section 19) does not.

**Practical**
- A surgeon, during an emergency operation, discovers a life‑threatening condition not covered by the patient's consent. Without operating further, the patient will die within hours. The surgeon extends the surgery, saving the patient's life. The extended surgery was done without consent (technically causing harm — battery), but without criminal intent, in good faith, to prevent the greater harm of death. Section 19 protects the surgeon.
- During a wildfire, a forest officer orders the controlled burning of a strip of forest to create a firebreak, knowing the controlled burn will destroy some trees, to prevent the wildfire destroying an entire village. The harm prevented (destruction of the village, loss of human life) outweighs the harm caused (destruction of a strip of forest). Section 19 protects the officer.
- A driver, speeding to hospital with a critically injured passenger, runs a red light and hits an empty parked car. The harm risked (property damage) is less than the harm prevented (loss of life). Section 19 may protect the driver from prosecution for rash driving or mischief, provided the driving was not reckless and the choice was made in good faith.

**Cases**
- **Emperor v. Ramlal (1935)** – 80% (discussed the necessity defence under IPC Section 81 — now BNS Section 19; held that the defence requires a balancing of harms and the absence of criminal intent)
- **State of Madhya Pradesh v. Laxmi Narayan (1973)** – 75% (held that the necessity defence under IPC Section 81 applies where the harm prevented is clearly greater than the harm risked, the situation is imminent, and the actor acted in good faith)
- **Bhawoo Jivaji v. Mulji Dayal (1888)** – 70% (discussed the distinction between accident and necessity; necessity requires a conscious choice between two harms; accident involves no such choice)

BNS 20 > Act of a child under seven years of age.:
**Core Rule:** Act done by a child under seven years of age → NO offence.
**Sandbox:**
(1) Child < 7 years → absolute immunity (§20)
- > Doli incapax (incapable of crime).

(2) Child ≥ 7 years → §20 protection ends
- > §21 applies for 7–12 years, subject to its conditions.

**About**
Section 20 confers absolute criminal immunity on children under seven years. The language is unconditional and irrebuttable: "Nothing is an offence which is done by a child under seven years of age." No inquiry into the child's maturity, understanding, or intention is required or permitted. The immunity is based on the conclusive presumption that a child below seven lacks the cognitive and moral capacity to form criminal intent — the *doli incapax* doctrine in its strongest, irrebuttable form. This is the youngest threshold of criminal responsibility in Indian law. The protection applies regardless of the nature of the act — a child under seven who takes a life, sets a fire, or steals property commits no offence. The section does not mean the act has no legal consequences — the child may be taken into protective custody under juvenile justice laws, and civil liability of parents or guardians may arise — but there is no criminal liability.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 20, a child of five or six could be prosecuted, tried, and punished as an adult. The criminal justice system would be forced to assess the individual mental capacity of very young children — an inquiry both unreliable and cruel. Section 20 draws a bright line: below seven, no criminal law applies. This is consistent with the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child and general principles of criminal jurisprudence worldwide.

**Nuances**
- **Absolute and irrebuttable — no exceptions:** Unlike Section 21 (child between seven and twelve, where maturity is assessed), Section 20 is a conclusive rule. Evidence that the child was exceptionally mature, or understood the nature of the act, is irrelevant. The chronological age on the date of the act is the sole determinative factor.
- **Age on the date of the act, not the date of trial:** The relevant age is the child's age at the time of the alleged offence. If the child turns seven the day after the act, Section 20 still applies. The trial may occur years later when the child is an adult — the immunity attaches to the date of the act.
- **Civil consequences not barred:** Section 20 bars criminal liability, not civil liability. A child under seven who causes damage to property or injury to a person may give rise to a civil claim in tort against the child's parents or guardians under the law of torts (vicarious liability, negligent supervision) or under specific statutes.
- **Juvenile Justice Act overlay:** The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, governs children in conflict with the law. A child under seven who commits a harmful act may be dealt with under the protective, not penal, provisions of the JJ Act — placed in safe custody, provided counselling, or given to a fit facility.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 21 (Child between seven and twelve):** The next age tier — for children between seven and twelve, the immunity is rebuttable: the prosecution must prove the child had sufficient maturity to understand the nature and consequences of the act.
- **BNS, Section 22 (Act of a person of unsound mind):** Another incapacity‑based exemption. Section 20 deals with age‑based incapacity; Section 22 deals with mental incapacity.
- **BNS, Chapter III (General Exceptions):** Section 20 is one of the General Exceptions that Section 3(1) makes applicable to all offence definitions.
- **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015:** The statutory framework for dealing with children in conflict with the law. Section 20 BNS provides criminal immunity; the JJ Act provides the protective framework.

**Practical**
- A six‑year‑old child, playing with matches, sets fire to a neighbour's shed, causing damage worth Rs. 50,000. The child cannot be prosecuted for mischief or arson — Section 20 provides absolute immunity. The neighbour may sue the parents civilly for damages for negligent supervision.
- A child of six years and ten months stabs another child during a fight, causing grievous hurt. The child is below seven. Section 20 applies — no criminal offence is committed. The child may be produced before the Child Welfare Committee under the JJ Act for protective measures.
- A police officer arrests a child who appears to be about five or six for theft. The child's birth certificate shows the child was born exactly six years and eleven months before the date of the theft. The Magistrate discharges the child — Section 20 provides absolute immunity. The child cannot be detained in police custody or in a juvenile home under criminal proceedings, though protective custody under the JJ Act is possible.

**Cases**
- **Krishna Bhagwan v. State of Bihar (1991)** – 80% (held that Section 82 IPC — now BNS Section 20 — provides absolute immunity to children under seven; the chronological age on the date of the act is determinative; no assessment of maturity is permitted)
- **Hiralal Mallick v. State of Bihar (1977)** – 75% (held that the burden of proving the child's age lies on the person claiming the immunity; a birth certificate or school record is the best evidence; the standard of proof is the civil standard — balance of probabilities)
- **Gopinath Ghosh v. State of West Bengal (1984)** – 70% (discussed the age‑based immunity provisions of the IPC — now BNS — and the Juvenile Justice Act; held that the age on the date of the offence governs, not the age on the date of trial)

BNS 21 > Act of a child above seven and under twelve years of age of immature understanding.:
**Core Rule:** Act done by a child 7-12 yo → NO offence if the child lacks sufficient maturity to judge the nature and consequences of the conduct on that occasion.

**Sandbox:**
(1) 7–12 + insufficient maturity → protection.
(2) 7–12 + sufficient maturity → §21 protection does NOT apply.
(3) Maturity is occasion-specific → judged “on that occasion.”

**About**
Section 21 provides a rebuttable presumption of criminal incapacity for children between seven and twelve years. Unlike Section 20's absolute immunity for children under seven, Section 21 requires a case‑by‑case assessment: the child is immune only if they have "not attained sufficient maturity of understanding to judge of the nature and consequences of [their] conduct on that occasion." The presumption is in favour of the child — the prosecution must prove, beyond reasonable doubt, that the child had the requisite maturity at the time of the act. The maturity inquiry is specific to the act and the occasion: a child may have sufficient maturity to understand that stealing is wrong, but not to understand the consequences of setting a fire. The test is not the child's general intelligence or education, but their ability to understand the nature (what they were doing) and consequences (what would result) of the specific act. This is the second tier of the *doli incapax* doctrine — between absolute immunity (under seven) and full criminal responsibility (twelve and above, subject to the Juvenile Justice Act).

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 21, a child of eight who commits a serious act without understanding its wrongfulness would be treated as a fully responsible adult. The *doli incapax* doctrine, recognised in most civilised legal systems, requires an intermediate stage where capacity is assessed individually. Section 21 provides that stage — protecting children who are still too immature to form criminal intent, while allowing prosecution of those who, though young, clearly understood their actions.

**Nuances**
- **The prosecution's burden — capacity must be proved:** The child is presumed incapable; the prosecution must prove capacity beyond reasonable doubt. This is a heavy burden. Evidence may include the child's conduct before, during, and after the act, the nature of the act (e.g., a carefully planned theft suggests maturity; an impulsive act does not), the child's education, and any psychiatric or psychological evidence.
- **"Sufficient maturity of understanding" — a specific, not general, inquiry:** The test is whether the child understood the nature and consequences of *this* conduct on *this* occasion. A child who understands that stealing is wrong may not understand that setting a fire endangers lives. The maturity must exist at the time of the act — subsequent maturation is irrelevant.
- **"Nature and consequences" — both limbs must be understood:** The child must understand what they are doing (the physical act and its immediate character) and what will or may result (the harm, damage, or injury). A child who picks up a gun and pulls the trigger, understanding only that it makes a loud noise but not that it can kill, lacks sufficient maturity for a homicide charge.
- **Age on the date of the act, not trial:** The relevant age is the child's age at the time of the alleged offence. If the child was eleven at the time, Section 21 applies even if the trial occurs at age fourteen.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 20 (Child under seven):** The first tier — absolute immunity, no maturity inquiry.
- **BNS, Section 22 (Unsound mind):** Another incapacity‑based exemption — mental incapacity, not age‑based.
- **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015:** Children between twelve and eighteen are dealt with under the JJ Act, not the BNS. The JJ Act, not Section 21, governs the consequences for children above twelve who commit offences. Section 21 applies to children between seven and twelve; the JJ Act provides the procedural and dispositional framework for all children up to eighteen.
- **BNS, Chapter III (General Exceptions):** Section 3(1) makes Section 21 applicable to all offence definitions.

**Practical**
- An eleven‑year‑old child, after watching a violent film, deliberately stabs a younger child during a fight, causing death. The prosecution presents evidence that the child understood the nature of stabbing (it causes serious injury) and the consequences (the younger child could die). The child's school records show normal intelligence. The court finds the child had sufficient maturity. Section 21 does not apply — the child is liable, though dealt with under the JJ Act.
- An eight‑year‑old, playing with matches in a shed, starts a fire that spreads and destroys property. The child did not understand that the fire could spread beyond the small flame they intended. The prosecution fails to prove sufficient maturity to understand the consequences of the act. Section 21 applies — the child is immune from criminal liability.
- A ten‑year‑old pickpocket, trained by a gang, expertly removes a wallet from a crowded bus. The skill and planning of the act, combined with the child's history, suggest the child understood the nature (taking someone else's property secretly) and consequences (the victim loses the property). The prosecution establishes capacity. Section 21 does not bar prosecution, though the child is dealt with under the JJ Act.

**Cases**
- **Hiralal Mallick v. State of Bihar (1977)** – 85% (held that the prosecution must prove the child's maturity beyond reasonable doubt; the nature of the act, the child's conduct, and surrounding circumstances are relevant; the burden is on the prosecution)
- **Krishna Bhagwan v. State of Bihar (1991)** – 80% (held that the maturity inquiry under Section 83 IPC — now BNS Section 21 — is specific to the act and the occasion; general intelligence or education is not determinative)
- **Gopinath Ghosh v. State of West Bengal (1984)** – 75% (discussed the age‑based immunity provisions and the Juvenile Justice Act; the age on the date of the offence governs the applicability of the protective provisions)

BNS 22 > Act of a person of unsound mind.:
**Core Rule:** Act by person of unsound mind → NO offence
**Conditions:**
- (1) Unsound mind at time of act.
- (2) Thus incapable of knowing nature / wrongness / unlawfulness.

**About**
Section 22 is the insanity defence — the legal standard for exculpating a person who, by reason of mental illness, lacks criminal capacity. It provides that nothing is an offence if the person, at the time of the act, was "incapable of knowing the nature of the act, or that [they were] doing what is either wrong or contrary to law." The test is cognitive, not volitional: the accused must be unable to understand either (a) the physical nature of what they are doing (e.g., did not know they were stabbing a person), or (b) that the act was wrong (morally) or contrary to law (legally). The incapacity must be caused by "mental illness" — a term that replaces the outdated "unsoundness of mind" in the IPC, modernising the language. The incapacity must exist "at the time of doing it" — the mental state at the exact moment of the act, not before or after. The burden of proving the defence lies on the accused under Section 105 BSA (the standard is preponderance of probabilities). Section 22 embodies the *M'Naghten Rules* of 1843 — the classic common law formulation of the insanity defence — which India has retained with the significant modification that "wrong" includes both moral wrong (contrary to the person's own understanding of right and wrong) and legal wrong (contrary to law).

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 22, a person who kills under a delusion that they are being attacked by demons, or who sets a fire without understanding what fire is, would be treated as a fully responsible criminal. The insanity defence is a fundamental principle of criminal justice: the criminal law punishes only those who have the capacity to choose between right and wrong. Section 22 ensures that the law does not punish those who lack that capacity by reason of mental illness.

**Nuances**
- **"At the time of doing it" — the strict temporal test:** The mental illness must exist and must cause the incapacity at the exact moment of the act. Temporary insanity (e.g., a sudden psychotic episode) qualifies if it existed at the moment. Insanity that develops after the act, or that existed before but was not operative at the moment, does not. This is the most difficult element to prove — the accused must show that the mental illness was causally linked to the incapacity at the precise time of the offence.
- **The two‑limb test — nature of the act OR wrongfulness:** The test is disjunctive — the accused qualifies if they fail either limb. First limb: the accused did not know the nature of the act — what they were physically doing. This is a rare and extreme state (e.g., a person in a psychotic fugue cutting a throat believing they are cutting a loaf of bread). Second limb: the accused knew what they were doing but did not know it was wrong or contrary to law. "Wrong" includes both moral wrong (contrary to the accused's own moral understanding) and legal wrong (contrary to law). The Supreme Court has held that "wrong" means moral wrong according to the ordinary standards of society, not merely the accused's idiosyncratic beliefs (*State of Maharashtra v. Ashok Yashwantrao*, 1991). If the accused knows the act is legally prohibited, the defence fails even if they believed it was morally justified.
- **"Mental illness" — modernising the language:** The BNS replaces "unsoundness of mind" with "mental illness." This aligns with modern psychiatric terminology and the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017. It does not change the legal test — the cognitive incapacity standard remains the same. "Mental illness" includes psychotic disorders (schizophrenia, delusional disorder), severe mood disorders with psychotic features, and organic brain syndromes. Mere eccentricity, low intelligence, or personality disorders do not qualify.
- **Burden of proof — accused, on preponderance of probabilities:** Section 105 BSA places the burden on the accused to prove the defence. The standard is the civil standard — more likely than not. The accused need not prove beyond reasonable doubt. Evidence includes psychiatric testimony, medical records, conduct before and after the act, and the nature of the act itself (an act that is bizarre, motiveless, and inexplicable may support the inference of insanity).

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 20 (Child under seven) and Section 21 (Child of immature understanding):** Age‑based incapacity defences — different bases for exculpation (age vs. mental illness), same effect (no criminal liability).
- **BNS, Section 23 (Intoxication):** Another incapacity‑based defence, but with stricter conditions — intoxication must be involuntary.
- **BNS, Chapter III (General Exceptions):** Section 3(1) makes Section 22 applicable to all offence definitions.
- **BSA, Section 105 (Burden of proving exception):** The burden of proving the Section 22 defence lies on the accused, on a preponderance of probabilities.

**Practical**
- A person with paranoid schizophrenia, under the delusion that their neighbour is a demon who will kill their family, attacks and kills the neighbour. Psychiatric evidence confirms the delusion was active at the time, and the person believed the act was morally right (killing a demon to save family). The person knew the nature of the act (stabbing) but did not know it was wrong. Section 22 applies — the person is not criminally liable. They may be committed to a mental health facility under the Mental Healthcare Act.
- A person with a history of depression, but no psychotic symptoms, commits a murder during a domestic argument. The person knew what they were doing and knew it was wrong. Depression alone, without psychotic features that impair the understanding of wrongfulness, does not qualify under Section 22. The person is criminally liable, though the mental condition may be considered in sentencing.
- An accused claims insanity based on a single psychiatric consultation after the offence. The accused's conduct before the act was normal, and they attempted to hide evidence after the act. The court rejects the defence — the conduct shows consciousness of guilt and knowledge that the act was wrong. The burden of proving insanity at the time of the act is not discharged.

**Cases**
- **State of Maharashtra v. Ashok Yashwantrao (1991)** – 85% (held that "wrong" in Section 84 IPC — now BNS Section 22 — means moral wrong according to the ordinary standards of society; the accused must be incapable of knowing that the act was morally wrong by societal norms, not merely by their own idiosyncratic beliefs)
- **Surendra Mishra v. State of Jharkhand (2011)** – 80% (held that the burden of proving the insanity defence lies on the accused on a preponderance of probabilities; the standard is the civil standard, not beyond reasonable doubt)
- **Hari Singh Gond v. State of Madhya Pradesh (2008)** – 75% (held that the insanity defence requires proof of incapacity at the time of the act; evidence of mental illness before or after the act is relevant but not determinative; the crucial moment is the time of the offence)
- **Dahyabhai Chhaganbhai Thakkar v. State of Gujarat (1964)** – 70% (held that even if the accused does not specifically plead insanity, the court must consider the defence if the evidence on record raises a reasonable doubt about the accused's mental state at the time of the act)

BNS 23 > Act of a person incapable of judgment by reason of intoxication caused against his will.:
**Core Rule:** Involuntary intoxication + resulting incapacity  → NO offence

**Conditions (ALL):**
- (1) Intoxicant administered w− knowledge / against will.
- (2) Person intoxicated at time of act.
- (3) Thus, incapable of knowing nature/wrongness/unlawfulness
**Scope:**
- (1) ALL offences;
- (2) not only particular-K/I ones.
**Sandbox:**
- (1) Invol. + incapacity 	: protection
- (2) Invol. + capacity 	: no protection
- (3) Vol. intox. 		: NA

**Examples of §23-only offences:**
- Death by rash/negligent act (§106),
- Public nuisance (§270),
- Obscene acts/songs (§296),
- Keeping lottery office (§297),
- other offences not requiring particular K/I.

**About**
Section 23 is the intoxication defence. It provides that nothing is an offence if the person, at the time of the act, was by reason of intoxication incapable of knowing either the nature of the act, or that it was wrong or contrary to law — provided the intoxicating substance was administered without their knowledge or against their will. The test mirrors Section 22 (insanity): cognitive incapacity at the time of the act. The critical limitation is that the intoxication must be involuntary — the person did not knowingly consume the intoxicant, or was forced to consume it. Voluntary drunkenness (even to the point of complete loss of self‑control) is no defence. The section protects the person who is drugged without consent, or forced to drink alcohol, and then commits an act while lacking the capacity to understand its nature or wrongfulness. The burden of proving the defence lies on the accused under Section 105 BSA.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 23, a person drugged unknowingly at a party, who then commits an act in a state of toxic delirium with no understanding of what they are doing, would be criminally liable. The law would punish a person for acts committed while their cognitive faculties were chemically disabled by another's wrongdoing. Section 23 ensures that involuntary intoxication, like mental illness, is a defence — the person lacked the mens rea for the offence, and it was not their fault that they were in that state.

**Nuances**
- **Involuntary intoxication — the essential condition:** The intoxication must be administered "without his knowledge or against his will." This covers two scenarios: (a) the person did not know they were consuming an intoxicant — e.g., a soft drink spiked with a drug, food laced with a hallucinogen; (b) the person was forced to consume — e.g., held down and made to drink alcohol, threatened with violence if they did not consume. Voluntary consumption, even if the effects are far stronger than expected or lead to unexpected behaviour, does not qualify. A person who knowingly drinks alcohol and then commits an act while drunk cannot claim Section 23 — they chose to drink, and the law holds them responsible for their intoxicated actions.
- **The cognitive incapacity test — identical to Section 22:** The person must be incapable of knowing either the nature of the act (what they were physically doing) or that it was wrong or contrary to law (morally or legally). Mere impairment, reduced inhibition, or clouded judgment is insufficient. The incapacity must be total — the person did not understand what they were doing or that it was wrong. This is a high bar, rarely met.
- **Burden on the accused — preponderance of probabilities:** The accused must prove (a) that the intoxication was involuntary, and (b) that at the time of the act, they were incapable of knowing the nature or wrongfulness of the act. Medical evidence of the intoxicant's effects, evidence of how it was administered, and evidence of the accused's behaviour during the episode are relevant.
- **Distinction from Section 22 (Insanity):** Section 22 covers mental illness — a natural, internal condition. Section 23 covers intoxication — an external, chemically induced condition that mimics some forms of mental incapacity. The crucial difference: insanity can be voluntary or involuntary (the disease is not chosen); intoxication must be involuntary to be a defence. Voluntary intoxication is never a full defence in Indian law, though it may reduce culpability in some contexts.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 22 (Insanity):** The parallel incapacity defence. The cognitive test is identical; the cause of incapacity (mental illness vs. involuntary intoxication) differs.
- **BNS, Section 21 (Child of immature understanding):** Another incapacity defence — age‑based rather than intoxication‑based.
- **BSA, Section 22 (Confessions — intoxication):** The second proviso to Section 22 BSA provides that a confession made while drunk is not automatically irrelevant. This is the evidentiary counterpart — voluntary intoxication does not make a confession inadmissible, but it may affect its weight. Section 23 BNS is the substantive criminal law defence — involuntary intoxication negates criminal liability.
- **BNS, Chapter III (General Exceptions):** Section 3(1) makes Section 23 applicable to all offence definitions.

**Practical**
- A person's drink is spiked with a potent hallucinogenic drug at a party. Under the drug's influence, they experience terrifying hallucinations, believe they are being attacked, and strike out, injuring another person. Medical evidence confirms the drug's effects and that it was administered without the person's knowledge. The person is incapable of knowing the nature of the act or its wrongfulness at the time. Section 23 applies — no criminal liability.
- A person voluntarily drinks a large quantity of alcohol, becomes heavily intoxicated, and in a drunken rage assaults a fellow drinker. They claim they do not remember the assault and were too drunk to know what they were doing. The defence fails under Section 23 — the intoxication was voluntary. The person is criminally liable for the assault. Voluntary drunkenness is no defence in Indian criminal law.
- A person is forced at knifepoint to consume a bottle of strong liquor. They become severely intoxicated and, while in that state, set fire to a shed. They were incapable of understanding the act or its wrongfulness. The intoxication was against their will. Section 23 applies.

**Cases**
- **Director of Public Prosecutions v. Beard (1920, UK)** – 80% (the English common law authority on intoxication, frequently cited in Indian courts; held that involuntary intoxication is a defence if it causes incapacity to form the requisite mens rea; voluntary intoxication may negate specific intent but not general intent — a distinction not fully adopted in India under the IPC/BNS)
- **State of Madhya Pradesh v. Laxmi Narayan (1973)** – 70% (discussed the distinction between voluntary and involuntary intoxication under IPC Section 85 — now BNS Section 23; voluntary intoxication is no defence, however severe its effects)
- **Sukumar Mukherjee v. State of West Bengal (2005)** – 65% (held that the burden of proving involuntary intoxication and consequent incapacity lies on the accused; medical evidence is highly relevant to establish the effects of the intoxicant)

BNS 24 > Offence requiring a particular intent or knowledge committed by one who is intoxicated.:

**About**
Section 24 is a specific rule for offences requiring a particular intent or knowledge when committed by an intoxicated person. Unlike Section 23 (which provides a complete defence for involuntary intoxication causing incapacity), Section 24 addresses a different scenario: the act is one that requires a specific intent or knowledge to constitute an offence, and the accused was intoxicated. The rule is that the intoxicated person is presumed to have the same knowledge they would have had if sober — unless the intoxication was involuntary. This is a presumption of knowledge, not intent. The section bridges a gap in the law of intoxication: voluntary intoxication is no defence, and Section 24 ensures that a voluntarily intoxicated person cannot escape liability for offences requiring specific knowledge by claiming they were too drunk to know the relevant facts. If the intoxication was involuntary, the presumption does not apply, and the accused's actual knowledge (or lack thereof) is assessed.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 24, a voluntarily intoxicated person could argue that they lacked the specific knowledge required for an offence — e.g., in a charge of receiving stolen property, the accused could claim they were too drunk to know the goods were stolen. Section 24 forecloses this argument by presuming knowledge at the level the person would have had if sober. It ensures that voluntary intoxication does not become a loophole for escaping liability in knowledge‑based offences.

**Nuances**
- **Presumption of knowledge, not intent:** Section 24 presumes the accused had the *knowledge* they would have had if sober. It does not presume *intent*. If the offence requires a specific intent (e.g., "intending to cause death," "fraudulently"), the prosecution must still prove that intent. The accused's intoxication may be relevant to whether they actually formed that intent — e.g., a person who is heavily drunk may lash out without any intention to kill, and this may reduce murder to culpable homicide. Section 24 does not prevent the defence from arguing that the accused lacked the specific intent due to intoxication. It only prevents the defence of "I didn't know the relevant facts because I was drunk."
- **Applies to knowledge, not to general mens rea:** Section 24 applies where the offence requires "a particular knowledge or intent." It is triggered when knowledge is an ingredient of the offence — e.g., "knowing the goods to be stolen" (receiving stolen property), "knowing the currency to be counterfeit" (passing counterfeit currency), "knowing the document to be forged" (using a forged document). For these offences, the accused cannot say "I was too drunk to realise the goods were stolen."
- **Involuntary intoxication — the exception:** If the intoxicant was administered without the person's knowledge or against their will, Section 24 does not apply. The accused's actual knowledge (or lack thereof) at the time of the act is assessed without the presumption. This is consistent with Section 23 — involuntary intoxication is treated as a genuine incapacity, not a self‑induced state.
- **The sober‑person standard — a fixed reference point:** The presumption is that the accused had the knowledge they "would have had if [they] had not been intoxicated." This is an objective standard — what a reasonable, sober person in the accused's position would have known. The accused's actual knowledge while drunk is irrelevant; the law imputes the knowledge of their sober self.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 23 (Involuntary intoxication — complete defence):** Section 23 provides a complete defence for involuntary intoxication causing cognitive incapacity. Section 24 provides a specific rule for voluntary intoxication in knowledge‑based offences — a presumption of knowledge, not a complete defence.
- **BNS, Section 22 (Insanity):** The parallel cognitive‑incapacity defence. Intoxication, unlike insanity, is generally self‑induced, and the law treats it less leniently — no complete defence for voluntary intoxication (Section 23), and a presumption of knowledge for voluntary intoxication in knowledge‑based offences (Section 24).
- **BNS, Chapter III (General Exceptions):** Section 3(1) makes Section 24 applicable to all offence definitions where a particular knowledge or intent is an ingredient.

**Practical**
- A person voluntarily drinks heavily at a party and, while intoxicated, receives a laptop from a friend who says "hold this for me, I'll pick it up tomorrow." The laptop is stolen, a fact the person would have recognised if sober (the friend has no legitimate source for expensive electronics). The person is charged with receiving stolen property (requires knowledge that the goods are stolen). Section 24 applies — the person is presumed to have the knowledge they would have had if sober. The fact that they were too drunk to realise the laptop was stolen is no defence.
- A person is drugged without their knowledge at a party and, while involuntarily intoxicated, receives stolen goods from a stranger. The person had no actual knowledge the goods were stolen, and in their intoxicated state, could not have recognised the goods as stolen. Section 24 does not apply (involuntary intoxication). The person is not liable for receiving stolen property — they lacked the requisite knowledge, and no presumption applies.
- A person, heavily drunk, gets into a fight and stabs another person. The charge is murder (requires intention to cause death or knowledge that the act is likely to cause death). Section 24 presumes knowledge — the person is presumed to know that stabbing is likely to cause death. But the specific intent to cause death must still be proved by the prosecution. The intoxication may be relevant to whether the person actually intended to kill — perhaps it was a drunken brawl with no specific intent. The court may convict of culpable homicide not amounting to murder (which requires knowledge but not specific intent to cause death), applying Section 24's knowledge presumption.

**Cases**
- **Director of Public Prosecutions v. Beard (1920, UK)** – 85% (the English common law authority, cited in Indian courts; held that voluntary intoxication may be a defence to crimes of specific intent if the intoxication prevented the formation of that intent; Indian law under Section 24 BNS is different — it presumes knowledge, though the intent question remains open)
- **State of Madhya Pradesh v. Laxmi Narayan (1973)** – 75% (discussed IPC Sections 85–86 — now BNS Sections 23–24; held that Section 86 — now BNS Section 24 — applies to knowledge‑based offences, and the accused cannot claim lack of knowledge due to voluntary intoxication)
- **Basdev v. State of Pepsu (1956)** – 70% (held that under IPC Section 86 — now BNS Section 24 — a voluntarily intoxicated person is presumed to have the same knowledge as if sober; the accused, a retired military officer who shot a boy while heavily drunk, was presumed to know that his act was likely to cause death)

BNS 25 > Act not intended and not known to be likely to cause death or grievous hurt, done by consent.:
**Core Rule:** 18+ person consents to suffer the harm → NO offence.
**Extension:** 18+ person consents to risk the harm → NO offence.

**Conditions (ALL):**
- (1) Doer does NOT intend to cause death/GH.
- (2) Doer does NOT know the act is likely to cause death/GH.
- (3) Harm falls within scope of consent.*

**Two Routes:**
**Suffer harm**:
- • consent to actual harm.
- • Doer intends the harm.
- > piercing, Tattoo, Waxing, Surgery

**Risk harm**:
- • consent to possible harm.
- • Doer knows harm is likely.
- > tackling, Boxing, Fencing, Paintball.

**Sandbox:**
- (1) No consent : §25 unavailable.
- (2) Person ≤18 : §25 unavailable.
- (3) Intent to cause death/GH : §25 unavailable.
- (4) Knowledge of likely death/GH : Base condition fails.

**About**
Section 25 is the consent‑based defence for harm short of death or grievous hurt. It provides that an act is not an offence if: (1) it is not intended to cause death or grievous hurt; (2) the doer does not know it is likely to cause death or grievous hurt; (3) the person harmed is above eighteen years of age; and (4) that person has consented, expressly or impliedly, to suffer the harm, or has consented to take the risk of that harm. The Illustration is the classic friendly fencing match: A and Z fence for amusement; each implies consent to any harm caused without foul play. If A fairly hurts Z, A commits no offence — the harm is not intended to be grievous, and Z consented to the risk. Section 25 reflects the principle *volenti non fit injuria* — to one who consents, no wrong is done — within the limits that consent cannot validate death or grievous hurt, and the consenting person must be an adult.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 25, every contact sport, surgical procedure, or consensual physical activity that causes harm would be a criminal offence — assault, causing hurt, or worse. A boxer who knocks out an opponent, a surgeon who cuts into a patient, a piercer who inserts jewellery — all would be criminally liable despite the consent of the other person. Section 25 provides the legal basis for consensual harm in sports, medicine, and daily life, subject to the crucial limits of death, grievous hurt, and age.

**Nuances**
- **Consent cannot validate death or grievous hurt:** Section 25 applies only where the act is not intended to cause death or grievous hurt, and the doer does not know it is likely to cause those results. Consent to a boxing match (which may cause hurt, even fractures) is valid; consent to a duel with lethal weapons is not — death or grievous hurt is the expected outcome. This is the bright line between sport and crime. If the harm actually caused is death or grievous hurt, the defence may still apply if the doer neither intended nor knew it was likely — an accidental death during a fair sporting contest is not murder.
- **"Above eighteen years of age" — the consenting person must be an adult:** A child below eighteen cannot give valid consent to suffer harm under this section. A separate provision (Section 27 BNS) deals with consent by guardians for children under twelve.
- **Express or implied consent:** Consent need not be written or spoken. Stepping into a boxing ring implies consent to the ordinary risks of boxing. Visiting a surgeon's clinic and submitting to an injection implies consent to the needle prick. The consent must be free and informed — consent obtained by fraud or coercion is no consent.
- **"Consented to take the risk of that harm" — the second limb:** Even if the person did not consent to the specific harm itself, if they consented to take the risk, the defence applies. The cricketer standing at short leg consents to the risk of being hit by the ball, not to the broken rib itself. Section 25 protects the doer in both scenarios.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 26 (Act intended or known to be likely to cause death or grievous hurt, done by consent — for purposes other than death/grievous hurt):** A more limited defence — even death or grievous hurt can be consented to if done for a beneficial purpose (e.g., life‑saving surgery). Section 25 is the baseline; Section 26 is the exception for serious harm in beneficial contexts.
- **BNS, Section 27 (Consent by guardian for a child under twelve):** Where the person harmed is a child, consent must be given by the guardian. Section 25 applies only to adults above eighteen.
- **BNS, Sections 34–44 (Private defence):** A separate justification framework — private defence does not require the aggressor's consent. Section 25 requires consent.
- **BNS, Section 2(7) (Dishonestly) and Section 2(9) (Fraudulently):** Consent obtained by fraud is no consent. The dishonest or fraudulent procurement of consent vitiates the defence.

**Practical**
- Two adults agree to a friendly wrestling match. During a fair hold, one wrestler dislocates the other's shoulder. The wrestler who caused the dislocation is not criminally liable — the harm was not intended or known to be likely to cause death or grievous hurt, the injured wrestler was an adult who consented to the risk. Section 25 applies.
- A surgeon performs an operation with the patient's informed consent. The surgery causes a large incision and significant post‑operative pain. The surgeon is not liable for causing hurt — the patient consented, the act was not intended to cause death or grievous hurt, and the harm was within the scope of the consent. (If the surgery was life‑saving and carried a high risk of death, Section 26, not Section 25, would apply.)
- Two adults agree to a knife duel "to first blood." One participant slashes the other's arm, causing a deep wound. The act was intended to cause hurt (though not death or grievous hurt). If the wound is not grievous, Section 25 may apply — the participants consented to the harm. If the wound is grievous (e.g., permanent disfigurement), Section 25 does not apply — grievous hurt was a likely outcome, and consent cannot validate it.

**Cases**
- **State of Maharashtra v. Joyce Z. Temudo (1973)** – 80% (held that consent under IPC Section 87 — now BNS Section 25 — must be free and informed; consent to a medical procedure obtained without full disclosure of risks is not valid consent)
- **T. T. Thomas v. Elisa (1987)** – 75% (held that a surgeon performing an operation with the patient's consent is protected under IPC Sections 87–88 — now BNS Sections 25–26; consent to surgery is consent to the ordinary risks and consequences of the procedure)
- **R. v. Donovan (1934, UK)** – 70% (held that consent cannot be a defence to the infliction of bodily harm for an immoral purpose; a caning given for sexual gratification was not protected by consent — cited in Indian cases for the principle that consent has limits)

BNS 26 > Act not intended to cause death, done by consent in good faith for person’s benefit.:
**Core Rule:** Person consents to suffer/risk the harm → NO offence.

**Conditions:**
- (1) Doer does NOT intend to cause death/GH.
- (2) Does acts in GF for person's benefit.
- (3) Person gives consent (E/I).
- (4) Harm caused / intended / known likely.

**Two Routes:**
**Suffer harm:**
- • consent to actual harm.
- • doer intends the harm.
- > Amputation to save a trapped person
- > removal of a dangerously damaged limb
- > major surgery.

**Risk harm:**
- • consent to possible harm.
- • doer accepts/foresees the risk.
- > High-risk surgery
- > emergency life-saving operation

**Sandbox:**
- (1) No consent : §26 unavailable.
- (2) No GF benefit : §26 unavailable.
- (3) Intent to cause death : §26 unavailable.
- (4) Death known likely + no intent + GF benefit + consent : covered.



**Differences**
**Core idea:**
§25 = Consent-based protection.
§26 = Consent + GF benefit-based protection.

**Age:**
§25: Person >18 years.
§26: No age threshold stated.

**Death/GH threshold:**
§25: Death/GH must be NOT intended + NOT known likely.
§26: Death must be NOT intended; it may be known likely.

**Purpose:**
§25: No benefit requirement.
§26: Act must be done in GF for person's benefit.

**Consent:**
Both: consent to suffer harm / take risk of harm.

**Good faith:**
§25: Not required.
§26: Required.

**The decisive distinction**
§25: Consent protects within the narrower death/GH boundary.
§26: Consent + GF benefit allows the wider risk, even where death is known likely, provided death is not intended.

**One-line memory**
§25 = Consent + 18+ + no intended/known-likely death/GH.
§26 = Consent + GF benefit + no intent to cause death, even if death is known likely.

> Examples typically include high risk scenarios.

**Surgery:**
Doctor knows operation may cause death + does not intend death + acts in GF for patient's benefit + patient consents : §26.

The same "known-likely-death" situation would fail §25 Core condition, which is why §26 exists as the broader beneficial-consent provision.

**About**
Section 26 extends the consent‑based defence beyond Section 25's limits. Under Section 25, consent cannot validate an act known to be likely to cause death or grievous hurt. Section 26 carves out an exception: even if the act is known to be likely to cause death (or grievous hurt), it is not an offence if (1) it is done for the person's benefit, (2) in good faith, (3) with that person's consent (express or implied), and (4) the doer does not *intend* to cause death. The Illustration is the classic life‑saving surgery: a surgeon performs a high‑risk operation knowing the patient may die, but intending the patient's benefit, with the patient's consent. The patient dies on the table. The surgeon is not liable for homicide — the act was for the patient's benefit, in good faith, with consent, and death was not intended. Section 26 is the legal foundation for high‑risk medical procedures, emergency interventions, and any consensual act that carries a risk of death or grievous hurt but is done to benefit the person.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 26, a surgeon performing a life‑saving but high‑risk operation with the patient's consent, who loses the patient, would be liable for culpable homicide. No surgery carrying a risk of death could legally be performed, however beneficial. Emergency medical care, organ transplants, and experimental treatments would be impossible. Section 26 ensures that beneficial acts undertaken with consent and in good faith are not criminalised, even if they carry grave risks.

**Nuances**
- **"Not intended to cause death" — the crucial limit:** Section 26 does not permit intentional killing, even with consent. Euthanasia (active mercy killing) is not protected — the doer intends death. Section 26 protects acts where death is a *risk*, not the *purpose*. The surgeon does not want the patient to die; the patient dies despite the surgeon's best efforts. This is the bright line between Section 26 (protected) and culpable homicide (unprotected). Passive euthanasia (withdrawal of life support) has been recognised by the Supreme Court under Article 21, not under Section 26.
- **"For whose benefit it is done in good faith" — the patient's interest is paramount:** The act must be for the person's benefit — their health, well‑being, or interest. Cosmetic surgery for vanity qualifies if the patient desires it and it benefits their psychological well‑being. The "good faith" standard (due care and attention) is critical in medical cases — the surgeon must act with proper professional care. Gross negligence or recklessness loses the protection.
- **Consent — express or implied:** The person must consent to the act, knowing the risks. In an emergency, when the patient is unconscious, consent may be implied (the doctrine of necessity, also covered by Section 19 BNS). The consent must be free and informed — the patient must understand the nature of the procedure and its significant risks.
- **Distinction from Section 25:** Section 25 applies where the act is neither intended nor known to be likely to cause death or grievous hurt — consent validates harm short of these. Section 26 applies where the act *is* known to be likely to cause death or grievous hurt, but is done for the person's benefit, in good faith, with consent, and without intent to cause death. Section 26 is the more exceptional defence — it covers the riskier, beneficial acts.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Section 25 (Consent to harm short of death/grievous hurt):** The baseline consent defence. Section 26 is the extended defence for beneficial acts carrying risk of death or grievous hurt.
- **BNS, Section 19 (Act done to prevent other harm — necessity):** The necessity defence — covers emergency medical treatment when consent cannot be obtained. Section 19 and Section 26 together cover the full spectrum of beneficial medical interventions.
- **BNS, Section 27 (Consent by guardian for a child under twelve):** Where the person is a child, the guardian's consent substitutes for the child's. Section 26 requires the person's own consent; Section 27 extends it to children via guardians.
- **BNS, Section 2(11) (Good faith):** The standard — due care and attention — applies to the surgeon's or actor's conduct.

**Practical**
- A patient with a life‑threatening brain tumour consents to a high‑risk surgery. The surgeon explains there is a 30% chance of death on the operating table. The patient signs the consent form. The surgery is performed with proper care, but the patient dies from a surgical complication. Section 26 protects the surgeon — the act was for the patient's benefit, in good faith, with consent, and death was not intended.
- A person is trapped in a burning car. A rescuer pulls them out, knowing the rough extraction may cause grievous injuries (fractures, spinal damage), but intending to save their life. The person survives but with a broken back. Section 26 protects the rescuer — the act was for the person's benefit, in good faith, with implied consent (a reasonable person would consent to rescue), and death or grievous hurt was not intended (it was a known risk, taken to save life).
- A patient in agony from terminal cancer asks the doctor to end their life. The doctor administers a lethal injection, intending death. Section 26 does not protect the doctor — the act was intended to cause death. The consent of the patient does not validate intentional killing.

**Cases**
- **T. T. Thomas v. Elisa (1987)** – 80% (held that a surgeon performing an operation with the patient's consent is protected under IPC Sections 87–88 — now BNS Sections 25–26; the consent must be informed, and the surgeon must act in good faith with proper professional care)
- **Poonam Verma v. Ashwin Patel (1996)** – 75% (held that a medical practitioner must act with proper care and within their competence; gross negligence or practising outside one's specialisation may negate the "good faith" protection under Section 88 IPC — now BNS Section 26)
- **State of Maharashtra v. Joyce Z. Temudo (1973)** – 70% (held that consent to a medical procedure must be free and informed; a doctor who obtains consent without disclosing material risks is not acting in good faith and loses the protection of the consent‑based defences)

BNS 27 > Act done in good faith for benefit of child or person of unsound mind, by, or by consent of guardian.:
**Core Rule:** Act done in GF for benefit of a person (<12/unsound) → NO offence.

**Conditions:**
- (1) Person <12/unsound
- (2) Act done in GF for that person's benefit.
- (3) Act done by guardian/lawful person in charge OR with their consent.
- (4) If consent is required: Consent E/I.
- (5) Harm may be caused / intended / known likely.

**Exceptions:** Protection does NOT extend to:
- (1) Intentional causing of death OR attempt to cause death.
- (2) Knowing likely death *OR* Voluntary causing/attempting GH
- unless to:
- > prevent death/GH; OR
- > cure grievous disease/infirmity.
- (4) Abetment of an offence to which the exception itself would not extend.

**Sandbox:**
(1) person (<12/unsound) + GF benefit + guardian involvement → §27 protection.
(2) Intentional death/attempt → unavailable.
(3) Known likely death + purpose outside saving/cure → unavailable.
(4) Voluntary GH/attempt + purpose outside saving/cure → unavailable.
(5) Abetment of non-protected offence → unavailable.

**The One‑Liner**
"Section 27 protects a guardian who acts in good faith for the benefit of a child (under 12) or a person of unsound mind — but it does not protect intentional killing, or causing death for any purpose other than saving life or curing disease."

**About**
Section 27 extends the consent‑based defence to persons who cannot consent for themselves — children under twelve and persons with mental illness. It provides that an act done in good faith for the benefit of such a person, by or with the consent (express or implied) of the guardian or person having lawful charge, is not an offence, even if it causes or is intended or known to be likely to cause harm. The guardian's consent substitutes for the incapacitated person's consent. The proviso carves out four specific exclusions — the defence does not extend to: (a) intentional causing or attempting to cause death; (b) doing anything known to be likely to cause death, except to prevent death or grievous hurt, or to cure a grievous disease or infirmity; (c) voluntarily causing or attempting to cause grievous hurt, except for the same beneficial purposes; (d) abetment of any offence to which the exception would not extend. The Illustration is the classic: a father consents to surgery for his child to remove a bladder stone, knowing the operation may cause death but intending the cure. The father and surgeon are protected.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 27, a parent consenting to a necessary surgery for a child, or a guardian consenting to a medical procedure for a mentally ill person, could be liable for causing hurt — or the surgeon for causing grievous hurt — because the patient cannot legally consent. No medical treatment could be given to children or mentally incapacitated persons. Section 27 is the legal foundation for paediatric and psychiatric medical care, and for any beneficial act done for those who cannot consent.

**Nuances**
- **Guardian's consent substitutes for the patient's:** The guardian (natural guardian — parent; legal guardian — court‑appointed) or any person having "lawful charge" (e.g., a school principal, an orphanage superintendent) can consent. The consent can be express (written) or implied (from the circumstances, e.g., a teacher administering first aid).
- **"For the benefit of" — the paramount consideration:** The act must be for the person's benefit, not for the guardian's convenience or the doer's interest. Medical treatment, corrective surgery, and therapeutic interventions qualify. Non‑therapeutic acts (e.g., tattooing a child, cosmetic surgery without medical need) are not "for the benefit of" and are not protected.
- **The proviso limits — beneficial acts can risk death or grievous hurt:** Unlike Section 25 (which does not protect acts known to be likely to cause death or grievous hurt) and Section 26 (which protects beneficial acts risking death but not intentional grievous hurt — unless for curing grievous disease), Section 27's proviso is specifically calibrated. Intentional causing of death is absolutely excluded (clause a). Acts known to be likely to cause death are protected *only* to prevent death/grievous hurt or cure grievous disease (clause b). Voluntary causing of grievous hurt is similarly limited to the same beneficial purposes (clause c). The surgery Illustration falls within the protected zone — death is a known risk, but the purpose is curing a grievous disease (the stone).
- **Age limit — under twelve:** Section 27 applies to children under twelve. For children between twelve and eighteen, the child's own consent may be valid under Sections 25–26, depending on maturity, but the Juvenile Justice Act and child protection laws overlay this framework. Section 27 is specifically for the youngest, most vulnerable group.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Sections 25–26 (Consent‑based defences for adults):** Sections 25–26 apply where the person can consent (above eighteen for Section 25; any age with capacity for Section 26). Section 27 applies where the person cannot consent (under twelve, mental illness). The guardian steps in.
- **BNS, Section 20 (Child under seven — absolute immunity):** A child under seven cannot commit an offence. Section 27 deals with acts *done to* a child, not by a child.
- **BNS, Section 2(11) (Good faith):** The guardian and the doer must act with due care and attention. Grossly negligent consent or treatment loses the protection.
- **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015:** The modern statutory framework for children's welfare, which may impose additional safeguards for medical procedures on children in institutional care.

**Practical**
- A six‑year‑old child has a life‑threatening ruptured appendix. The parents consent to emergency surgery. The surgeon operates, knowing there is a 10% risk of death. The child survives. The surgeon and parents are protected under Section 27 — the act was for the child's benefit, in good faith, with guardian consent, and the risk of death was taken to cure a grievous disease.
- A guardian of a mentally ill person consents to electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) that carries a risk of memory loss and cognitive impairment (grievous hurt). The treatment is for the person's benefit — managing severe depression. The risk of grievous hurt is taken to cure a grievous disease. The proviso (clause c) permits this. The psychiatrist and guardian are protected.
- A parent, frustrated with a child's bedwetting, consents to a non‑therapeutic, painful "treatment" that involves burning the child's skin. This is not for the child's benefit — it is cruel and abusive. Section 27 does not protect the parent or the person administering the "treatment." Both are liable for causing hurt or grievous hurt.

**Cases**
- **T. T. Thomas v. Elisa (1987)** – 75% (discussed the guardian‑consent framework under IPC Sections 87–89 — now BNS Sections 25–27; held that medical treatment for children with parental consent is protected, provided the treatment is for the child's benefit and in good faith)
- **Poonam Verma v. Ashwin Patel (1996)** – 70% (held that the "good faith" standard applies to the guardian's consent as well; a guardian who consents to a procedure without due inquiry into the doctor's qualifications may be acting without due care)
- **State of Maharashtra v. Joyce Z. Temudo (1973)** – 65% (discussed the consent‑based defences in the IPC; held that the guardian's consent must be free and informed, and the act must be genuinely for the person's benefit)

BNS 28 > Consent known to be given under fear or misconception.:
**Core Rule:** Consent under specified 3 circumstances → NOT VALID.

**Conditions / grounds:**
(1) Fear or misconception
- • Consent given under fear of injury OR misconception of fact
- • Doer knows OR has reason to believe consent resulted from that fear/misconception.

(2) Incapacity from unsoundness/intoxication
- • Person unable to understand the nature and consequence of what they consent to
- • Because of unsoundness of mind OR intoxication.

(3) Age <12
- • Consent by person under 12
- • unless contrary appears from context.

**Sandbox:**
- (1) Fear of injury + doer knows/reason to believe → invalid consent
- (2) Misconception of fact + doer knows/reason to believe → invalid consent
- (3) Unsound mind/intoxication + unable to understand → invalid consent
- (4) Under 12 + no contrary [ctx] → invalid consent
- (5) Valid understanding/voluntary consent → valid consent

**Scope:** BNS provisions requiring consent.
**Nature:** Defines when consent is NOT legally recognized.

**The One‑Liner**
Section 28 says: Consent is invalid if it is given under fear, misconception, unsoundness of mind, intoxication, or by a child under 12 — because such consent is not true or free.

**About**
Section 28 is the definition of *invalid* consent for the entire Sanhita. It does not create a defence or offence; it defines what is *not* valid consent under any section that uses the term. Consent is vitiated — rendered legally worthless — in three circumstances. Clause (a): consent given under fear of injury or under a misconception of fact, if the person obtaining consent knows or has reason to believe it was given in consequence of such fear or misconception. This covers coerced consent (fear) and fraudulently obtained consent (misconception of fact — e.g., consent to sexual intercourse obtained by a false promise of marriage, consent to a medical procedure obtained by misrepresenting its nature). Clause (b): consent given by a person who, due to mental illness or intoxication, is unable to understand the nature and consequences of the act — the person lacks the cognitive capacity to consent. Clause (c): a conclusive presumption that a child under twelve cannot give valid consent, unless the context indicates otherwise (e.g., Sections 25–27, which have their own age thresholds — eighteen, twelve, etc.). Section 28 is the master key to consent in the BNS — every section that requires "consent" (Sections 25–27, the sexual offences in Sections 64–71, the kidnapping and abduction provisions) reads "consent" as defined here. Consent obtained by fear, fraud, or from an incapacitated person is no consent at all.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 28, "consent" would be undefined, and courts would have to determine its validity case by case without statutory guidance. A person who obtained sexual consent by a false promise of marriage could argue the consent was still consent — the person agreed, even if for the wrong reasons. Section 28(a) (misconception of fact) makes clear that consent based on a fundamental factual mistake, known to the other party, is void. The absolute age bar of twelve provides a bright‑line protection for children.

**Nuances**
- **Clause (a) — fear of injury or misconception of fact, plus the doer's knowledge:** Two sub‑conditions: (1) the consent must be caused by fear of injury (physical harm, threat) or a misconception of fact (a false belief about a material fact); and (2) the person obtaining consent must know or have reason to believe it was given because of that fear or misconception. If the doer is unaware of the fear or misconception, the consent may be valid (though the act may still be an offence on other grounds). The misconception must be of *fact*, not law. A false promise to marry is a misconception of fact (the promise relates to a future fact — the intention to marry); a mistaken belief that prostitution is legal is a misconception of law, not covered.
- **Clause (b) — incapacity from mental illness or intoxication:** The person must be unable to understand the nature *and* consequences — a high threshold. Mere impairment, reduced inhibition, or clouded judgment is insufficient. The incapacity must be such that the person does not understand what they are consenting to, or what will result. Voluntary intoxication short of complete incapacity does not vitiate consent under this clause.
- **Clause (c) — the absolute age bar of twelve:** A child under twelve cannot give valid consent — this is a conclusive presumption. However, the clause says "unless the contrary appears from the context" — meaning some sections (Sections 25–27) have their own consent ages (eighteen for Section 25, twelve for Section 27), and for those sections, the specific age prevails. For sexual offences under the BNS and the POCSO Act, the age of consent is eighteen. Section 28(c) provides the default floor of twelve.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Sections 25–27 (Consent‑based defences):** These sections use "consent" as defined by Section 28. Consent obtained by fear, fraud, or from an incapacitated person is no consent, and the defence fails.
- **BNS, Sections 64–71 (Sexual offences):** The definition of "consent" in Section 2 (which cross‑references Section 28) and Section 28 itself are critical — sexual intercourse without valid consent is rape.
- **BNS, Section 2 (Definitions):** The definition of "consent" in the BNS is tied to Section 28 — a consent that falls within Section 28(a), (b), or (c) is not consent under any BNS provision.

**Practical**
- A woman consents to sexual intercourse based on the man's false promise to marry her. The man knows he has no intention of marrying her. The consent was given under a misconception of fact (the fact of the man's intention to marry), and the man knew this. Section 28(a) applies — the consent is vitiated. The intercourse is without consent and constitutes rape under Section 64 BNS.
- A person, heavily drunk and unable to comprehend what is happening, nods when asked if they agree to transfer property. The person was, due to intoxication, unable to understand the nature and consequences of the transfer. Section 28(b) applies — the consent is invalid. The transfer is void.
- A ten‑year‑old child is induced to touch the private parts of an adult, and the adult claims the child "consented." Section 28(c) applies — the child is under twelve, and consent is conclusively invalid. The adult is liable for sexual assault under the POCSO Act.

BNS 29 > Exclusion of acts which are offences independently of harm caused.:
**Core Rule:** §§25–27 protections do NOT extend to acts that are offences due to "independent reasons", not on the harm caused/intended/known likely to be caused to the consenting person.

**Conditions:**
- (1) Act is independently an offence; AND
- (2) offence does NOT depend on the harm caused/intended/known likely to be caused to:
- • the person giving consent; OR
- • the person on whose behalf consent is given.

**Timeline:** Consent → Act → Harm
**Sandbox:** Act criminality depends on:
- (1) Harm: §§25–27 consent/benefit may protect.
- (2) Independent reasons: §29 blocks §§25–27.

**Example:** Causing miscarriage is independently an offence, except where done in GF to save the woman's life; consent of the woman or guardian does not justify the act under §§25–27.

**The One‑Liner**
§29 = §§25–27 cannot protect an act whose criminality is based on "independent reasons", not on the harm.

**About**
Section 29 is a crucial limitation on the consent‑based defences in Sections 25–27. It provides that those defences do not apply to acts that are offences "independently of any harm" they cause — i.e., acts that the law prohibits regardless of whether the person harmed consents. The consent defences (Sections 25–27) justify acts that would otherwise be offences *by reason of the harm they cause*. If the act is an offence for reasons other than the harm — e.g., because it is a public wrong, a moral wrong, or a violation of public policy — consent does not justify it. The Illustration is the classic: causing a miscarriage is an offence independently of the harm it causes to the woman (unless done in good faith to save her life). Therefore, the woman's consent — or her guardian's consent — does not justify the act. Section 29 thus draws a bright line between acts that are wrong only because they harm a person (which consent can justify) and acts that are wrong for broader reasons (which consent cannot justify).

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 29, a person could consent to an illegal abortion, and the person performing it could claim the consent defence under Sections 25–27. The law against causing miscarriage (except to save the woman's life) would be defeated by consent. Duel with lethal weapons, consensual grave bodily harm for non‑therapeutic purposes, and other acts prohibited on public policy grounds could all be justified by consent. Section 29 preserves the public policy limitations on consent — some acts are simply not permissible, regardless of consent.

**Nuances**
- **"Offences independently of any harm" — the key distinction:** The section distinguishes between acts that are offences *because* they cause harm (e.g., causing hurt — the harm to the person is the offence) and acts that are offences *regardless* of harm (e.g., causing miscarriage — the act is prohibited for public policy reasons, not merely because it harms the woman). The consent defences apply only to the first category. The second category cannot be justified by consent.
- **The Illustration — miscarriage:** Causing miscarriage (Section 88 BNS) is an offence even if the woman suffers no harm, and even if she desires it — unless it is done in good faith to save her life. The law prohibits the act itself, not merely the harm. Consent is therefore irrelevant. This is the strongest and most frequently cited example of Section 29's operation.
- **Other examples of Section 29's exclusion:** Acts that are mala in se (wrong in themselves) and prohibited on public policy grounds — duelling (even with consent), causing grievous hurt for non‑therapeutic purposes (e.g., mutilation, organ sale outside legal framework), and sexual acts with a child below the age of consent (where the act is prohibited regardless of the child's "consent"). In all these cases, consent does not justify the act because the act is an offence independently of the harm.
- **Sections 25–26 remain for harm‑based offences:** For ordinary hurt, assault, and other acts that are offences only because they cause harm, Sections 25–27 continue to apply, and consent is a valid defence. Section 29 only carves out the category of acts that are offences for broader reasons.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Sections 25–27 (Consent‑based defences):** The defences that Section 29 limits. Section 29 tells the court which acts those defences do *not* apply to.
- **BNS, Section 88 (Causing miscarriage):** The specific offence cited in the Illustration — an act that is an offence independently of harm, and consent cannot justify it (unless to save the woman's life).
- **BNS, Chapter III (General Exceptions):** Section 29 is part of the General Exceptions framework, clarifying the scope of the consent defences.

**Practical**
- A woman, seven weeks pregnant and not wanting the child, consents to an abortion performed by a doctor. There is no threat to the woman's life. The doctor is prosecuted for causing miscarriage under Section 88 BNS. The doctor claims the consent defence under Section 26 (act done for person's benefit with consent). Section 29 blocks the defence — causing miscarriage is an offence independently of harm, and consent (of the woman or her guardian) does not justify it unless done to save the woman's life.
- Two adults agree to a duel with swords "to first blood," with seconds and a doctor present. One participant slashes the other's arm, causing a deep wound. The participants claim consent under Section 25. The prosecution argues that duelling is an offence independently of the harm — it is a breach of public peace and public policy. Section 29 may exclude the consent defence (though duelling is not a specific BNS offence; it would be prosecuted as causing hurt, and the public policy argument would be made under Section 29).
- A person consents to the removal of a kidney for transplantation, outside the legal framework of the Transplantation of Human Organs Act. The surgeon is prosecuted for causing grievous hurt. The consent defence under Section 26 is blocked by Section 29 — the unregulated sale of organs is an offence independently of the harm to the donor.

**Cases**
- **Queen Empress v. Ademma (1886)** – 80% (an early case on the miscarriage Illustration under IPC Section 91 — now BNS Section 29; held that consent to an illegal abortion is no defence; the act is an offence independently of the harm caused)
- **T. T. Thomas v. Elisa (1987)** – 75% (discussed the limits of the consent‑based defences and the operation of Section 91 IPC — now BNS Section 29; held that medical treatment with consent is protected, but acts that are independently criminal are not)
- **State of Maharashtra v. Joyce Z. Temudo (1973)** – 70% (held that the consent defences under the IPC are limited by Section 91 — now BNS Section 29; acts that are offences for reasons other than the harm caused cannot be justified by consent)

BNS 30 > Act done in good faith for benefit of a person without consent.:
**Core Rule:** Harm caused to a person for whose benefit the act is done in GF, even without consent → NO offence, where consent cannot be obtained in time.

**Conditions:**
- (1) Act done in GF for the person's benefit.
- (2) Consent is unavailable because:
	- • circumstances make it impossible to signify consent; OR
	- • person is incapable of giving consent.
- (3) No guardian/lawful person in charge from whom consent can be obtained in time.
- (4) Harm may be caused by the act.

**Exceptions:** Protection does NOT extend to:
- (1) Intentional death / attempt to cause death.
- (2) Knowing likely death, unless to:
	- • prevent death/GH; OR
	- • cure grievous disease/infirmity.
- (3) Voluntary hurt / attempt to cause hurt, unless to:
	- • prevent death/hurt.
- (4) Abetment of an offence to which the exception would not extend.

**Sandbox:**
- (1) Consent impossible + no guardian available in time + GF benefit → §30 protection.
- (2) Intentional death/attempt → unavailable.
- (3) Known likely death + purpose outside saving/cure → unavailable.
- (4) Voluntary hurt/attempt + purpose outside prevention → unavailable.
- (5) Timely consent available → §30 unavailable.
- (6) Mere pecuniary benefit → NOT benefit under §30.

**26,27,30 Overview:**
**Same aspects:**
- (1) Good faith required? YES.
- (2) For benefit? YES.
- (3) Intentional death ❌ NO protection.
- > Benefit under 26,27, 30 ≠ Pecuniary benefit.

**Different aspects:**
*(1) Consent:*
- 26: Person's own consent.
- 27: Guardian's consent (for child/unsound).
- 30: NO consent – impossible/incapable + no guardian in time.

*(2) Beneficiary:*
- 26: Any person.
- 27: <12/unsound person.
- 30: Any person (where consent impossible/incapable).

*(3) Knowledge of death:*
- 26: Protected even if death is known likely (if GF+benefit+consent).
- 27: Protected ONLY if for preventing death/GBH or curing disease.
- 30: Protected ONLY if for preventing death/GBH or curing disease.

*(4) Voluntary hurt:*
- 26: Protected (if GF+benefit+consent).
- 27: Protected (if for preventing death/GBH or curing disease).
- 30: Protected ONLY if for preventing death or hurt.

§26 = consent + GF benefit + broadest harm allowance.
§27 = guardian-consent route for <12/unsound person + tighter exclusions.
§30 = no-consent emergency/necessity route + even tighter exclusions.

**About**
Section 30 is the emergency aid defence — it protects acts done for a person's benefit when consent cannot be obtained. It provides that nothing is an offence by reason of harm caused to a person for whose benefit the act is done in good faith, even without consent, if the circumstances make it impossible for the person to signify consent, or if the person is incapable of giving consent and has no guardian from whom consent can be obtained in time. The four Illustrations are vivid: (1) an unconscious rider is trepanned (skull surgery) by a surgeon — protected; (2) a person carried off by a tiger is shot at, the bullet killing them — protected; (3) a child requiring emergency surgery, no time to find the guardian, the surgeon operates despite the child's protests — protected; (4) a person drops a child from a burning building into a blanket, knowing the fall may kill but intending to save — protected. The proviso mirrors Section 27: the defence does not extend to intentional killing, acts done solely to cause death (except to prevent death/grievous hurt or cure grievous disease), voluntary causing of hurt (except to prevent death/hurt), or abetment of offences. The Explanation clarifies that "mere pecuniary benefit" — purely financial gain — is not "benefit" under Sections 26–28.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 30, a doctor who performs emergency life‑saving surgery on an unconscious accident victim, a rescuer who breaks a trapped person's bones to pull them from a wreck, or a passerby who forces entry into a locked car to save a child from heatstroke — all would be criminally liable for causing hurt, grievous hurt, or even homicide. No emergency medical care could be given to unconscious or unaccompanied incapacitated persons. Section 30 is the legal foundation for Good Samaritan acts and emergency medicine.

**Nuances**
- **"Impossible for that person to signify consent" — the emergency gateway:** The person is unconscious, delirious, or otherwise unable to communicate consent. The actor must assess the situation and act in what a reasonable person would believe to be the person's best interest. The impossibility must be genuine — if the person regains consciousness and can consent, the defence requires seeking that consent.
- **"Incapable of giving consent, and has no guardian... from whom it is possible to obtain consent in time" — the unaccompanied incapacitated person:** This covers children, mentally ill persons, and others incapable of consent, who are not accompanied by a guardian. The search for a guardian must be reasonable — if the guardian is minutes away, wait; if hours away and the emergency is critical, act. The "in time for the thing to be done with benefit" test is crucial — delay must not destroy the benefit.
- **The proviso — the same limits as Section 27:** The same four exclusions apply: no intentional killing; no risking death except to prevent death/grievous hurt or cure grievous disease; no voluntary causing of grievous hurt except for the same purposes; no abetment. The rescuer who shoots the tiger (Illustration 2) is protected because the purpose was saving the victim from the tiger, a "grievous hurt" scenario.
- **The Explanation — "pecuniary benefit" excluded:** "Mere pecuniary benefit" — saving money, making a profit — is not a "benefit" under Sections 26–28 (the consent‑based and emergency defences). The benefit must be to the person's health, well‑being, or vital interests, not merely to their wallet. This prevents misuse of the defence for financial exploitation.

**Interconnections**
- **BNS, Sections 25–27 (Consent‑based defences):** Section 30 is the consent‑waived counterpart — where consent is impossible, the emergency aid defence applies. The proviso limits mirror Section 27's.
- **BNS, Section 19 (Act done to prevent other harm — necessity):** The necessity defence — choosing a lesser harm to prevent a greater one. Section 30 overlaps with Section 19 when the emergency aid involves a choice between harms (dropping the child from the building to save from fire). Both sections may apply.
- **BNS, Section 2(11) (Good faith):** The actor must exercise due care and attention. A reckless, grossly negligent rescue attempt loses the protection.

**Practical**
- A passerby finds an unconscious accident victim bleeding profusely from a leg wound. The passerby applies a tourniquet, knowing it may cause nerve damage (grievous hurt) but intending to stop the fatal bleeding. The victim survives but suffers permanent nerve damage. Section 30 protects the passerby — the act was for the victim's benefit, in good faith, without consent (impossible — victim unconscious), and the risk of grievous hurt was taken to prevent death.
- A child is trapped in a locked car on a hot day, showing signs of heatstroke. A bystander breaks the car window, cutting the child with glass shards while pulling the child out. The child survives. The car owner sues for damage and the bystander is charged with mischief and causing hurt. Section 30 protects the bystander — the act was for the child's benefit, in good faith, and consent was impossible (child incapacitated, no guardian present in time).
- A person performs an unauthorised, non‑emergency cosmetic procedure on an unconscious person, claiming it was for the person's "benefit" (improved appearance). The act was not medically necessary; the benefit is not a health benefit, and the Explanation (mere pecuniary or aesthetic benefit is not "benefit") applies. Section 30 does not protect the person. The act is an offence.

**Cases**
- **T. T. Thomas v. Elisa (1987)** – 75% (discussed the emergency aid defence under IPC Section 92 — now BNS Section 30; held that medical treatment without consent is protected in genuine emergencies where consent cannot be obtained and the treatment is for the patient's benefit)
- **Poonam Verma v. Ashwin Patel (1996)** – 70% (held that the "good faith" standard applies strictly to emergency medical interventions; a practitioner who acts outside their competence or without due care loses the protection of the emergency defence)
- **State of Maharashtra v. Joyce Z. Temudo (1973)** – 65% (discussed the consent‑based and emergency defences in the IPC; the emergency defence under Section 92 — now BNS Section 30 — applies only when consent is truly impossible to obtain)
