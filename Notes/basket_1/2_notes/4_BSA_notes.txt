BSA 1 > Short title, application and commencement.:
**(1) Title:** BSA, 2023
**(2) Application:**
Applies to ALL judicial proceedings in/before any Court (incl. Courts-martial).
<u>Exceptions (Does NOT apply to):</u>
- • Affidavits presented to Court / officer
- • Proceedings before an arbitrator
**(3) Commencement:** Date notified by CG in OGz.

About
This section names the new evidence code—the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023—and draws its precise field of operation.

Sub-section (2): The Act applies to all judicial proceedings in any Court and expressly includes Courts‑martial. It then carves out two important exclusions: (i) affidavits presented to a Court or officer, and (ii) proceedings before an arbitrator.

Sub-section (3): Commencement is by a single Central Government notification—indicating the entire code comes into force as one integrated body of rules.

Counterfactual
Without this section, the scope of the evidence law would be dangerously open‑ended. There would be no automatic clarity that Courts‑martial must follow the same rigorous rules of evidence as ordinary criminal courts. The absence of the arbitrator exclusion would force private arbitration into the full straitjacket of the Adhiniyam, destroying the flexibility that makes arbitration attractive. Without the affidavit exclusion, every affidavit filed in court—today accepted subject to cross‑examination—would instantly be tested against strict rules of admissibility at the filing stage, choking the summary disposal of procedural and interim matters.

Nuances

“Judicial proceedings” undefined: Courts have consistently read “judicial proceedings” to mean proceedings where a court determines rights or liabilities, as opposed to administrative or executive inquiries. This keeps the Adhiniyam’s strict rules from hamstringing every quasi‑judicial authority.

Courts‑martial inclusion: By explicitly bringing Courts‑martial within the ambit, the Adhiniyam subjects military trials to the same statutory standards on relevancy, admissibility, burden of proof, and examination of witnesses. A soldier facing a General Court‑martial enjoys the same evidentiary protections as any accused in a sessions court, unless military law expressly provides otherwise.

Arbitrator exclusion: Arbitrators are bound only by natural justice and party agreement. They may receive hearsay, unsworn statements, or documents not strictly proved, provided fair opportunity is given. This exclusion is the statutory foundation for the flexibility of Indian arbitration.

Affidavit exclusion: Affidavits are widely used in writ petitions, interlocutory applications, and even some final hearings. Their form, content, and proof are governed by procedural codes (BNSS/CPC), court rules, and case law—not by the evidence code. The exclusion ensures practical, affidavit‑based disposal of vast court work continues unimpeded.

Singular commencement: The singular “such date” (unlike the BNS’s piecemeal power) ensures all chapters—relevancy, proof, examination—come into force together, avoiding a situation where some evidence rules are operative while others are not.

Interconnections

Constitution, Article 136(2): Empowers Parliament to make law for Armed Forces tribunals and Courts‑martial; the inclusion of Courts‑martial in the BSA sits within this constitutional framework.

Army Act, 1950 / Navy Act, 1957 / Air Force Act, 1950: These contain separate rules of evidence for Courts‑martial. The BSA applies “including Courts‑martial” only to the extent those special statutes do not exclude it—preserving the principle of special law prevailing.

Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, Section 19: Expressly states that the arbitrator is not bound by the Civil Procedure Code or the Indian Evidence Act. BSA Section 1(2) is the mirror provision confirming this exclusion.

BNSS & CPC: These procedural codes regulate the stage at which evidence is recorded, and how affidavits are received. The BSA’s affidavit exclusion dovetails with CPC Order 19 and BNSS provisions on affidavits.

Old Indian Evidence Act, 1872, Section 1: The direct predecessor. Judicial interpretation of “judicial proceedings,” “affidavits,” and “arbitrator” under the old Act remains entirely applicable.

Practical

In civil suits, interim injunction applications are decided almost entirely on affidavits. The BSA does not apply to those affidavits; they can contain statements of information and belief without strict proof at that stage.

An international commercial arbitration seated in India enjoys complete freedom from the BSA; the tribunal can adopt IBA Rules of Evidence or other flexible procedures.

A soldier court‑martialled for an offence under the Army Act will have the BSA’s protections on burden of proof and examination of witnesses unless the Army Act specifically departs from them.

Cases
Decided under the identical Section 1 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, and highly persuasive.

S. Dutt v. State of U.P. (1966) – 90% (defined “judicial proceedings” and held that an inquest under the CrPC is not a judicial proceeding; the Evidence Act does not apply to it)

Union of India v. Major A. Hussain (1998) – 85% (held that the Evidence Act applies to Courts‑martial; the rules of evidence in the Army Act are supplementary, not exclusionary)

Bharat Singh v. State of Haryana (1988) – 80% (affidavits are not evidence within the meaning of the Evidence Act unless specifically permitted by law, and the deponent must be produced for cross‑examination if facts are disputed)

Oil and Natural Gas Corporation Ltd. v. Saw Pipes Ltd. (2003) – 70% (on arbitrator’s power to receive evidence; the exclusion of the Evidence Act allows flexible procedure, but an award based on no evidence at all is against the public policy of India)

State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961) – 60% (explored the meaning of “evidence” in the Evidence Act and the scope of judicial proceedings, though not directly on Section 1)

About
Section 2 is the glossary of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023. It defines eleven core evidentiary concepts and a catch‑all bridge to other laws.

“Court” includes all Judges, Magistrates, and persons legally authorised to take evidence — but expressly excludes arbitrators.

“Conclusive proof” makes one fact irrebuttable upon proof of another; no evidence to disprove it is allowed.

“Proved”, “disproved”, “not proved” set the three possible findings of the court — a fact is proved if its existence appears so probable that a prudent person would act on it, disproved if its non‑existence reaches the same threshold, and not proved if neither test is met.

“May presume” gives the court discretion to treat a fact as proved unless disproved or to demand proof. “Shall presume” mandates the court to treat the fact as proved unless and until it is disproved.

“Fact” covers both physical things perceivable by the senses and any mental condition of which a person is conscious (intention, good faith, etc.).

“Facts in issue” are facts from which the existence, nature or extent of a right or liability necessarily follows — tied to civil issues or criminal charges.

“Relevant” means a fact connected to another in any of the ways specified in the Adhiniyam’s relevancy provisions.

“Evidence” now expressly includes statements given electronically (oral evidence) and all electronic or digital records produced for inspection (documentary evidence).

“Document” is greatly expanded: any matter recorded on any substance by letters, figures, marks or any other means, intended for recording that matter, and explicitly includes electronic and digital records — with illustrations naming emails, server logs, smartphone documents, messages, websites, locational evidence and voice mail.

Sub‑section (2) imports undefined words from the IT Act, 2000, the BNSS and the BNS.

Counterfactual
Without this section, the entire evidence code would lack operational definitions. The distinctions between “proved,” “disproved” and “not proved” would vanish, leaving fact‑finding standardless. The hierarchy of presumptions — “may presume,” “shall presume,” “conclusive proof” — would collapse, erasing the calibrated weight the Adhiniyam gives to different connections. The definition of “document” is the gateway to documentary evidence; without its express inclusion of electronic records, the admissibility of emails, server logs, digital messages and locational evidence would depend on strained judicial interpretation, risking a return to the old certificate‑heavy regime. Arbitrators would be caught in the definition of “Court,” destroying the flexibility that makes arbitration workable. The explicit recognition of electronic statements as oral evidence would be missing, keeping remote testimony in a grey area.

Nuances

“Document” as a technology‑neutral container: The definition now says “otherwise recorded upon any substance by means of letters, figures or marks or any other means.” The phrase “or any other means” and the explicit inclusion of “electronic and digital records” make the definition future‑proof. The six illustrations — especially server logs, locational evidence and voice mail — remove any doubt that ephemeral digital material is a document.

“Evidence” and electronic statements: Oral evidence expressly includes “statements given electronically which the Court permits or requires.” This codifies video‑conference testimony, removing the need to argue its validity under general procedural provisions.

“Conclusive proof” as a legislative shutters: Once the foundational fact is proved, the conclusively presumed fact is legally unassailable — the court “shall not allow evidence to be given for the purpose of disproving it.” This is the strongest presumption in the Adhiniyam, used in select provisions (e.g., certain certificates, age‑proof rules).

“Fact” includes mental states: By defining “fact” to include “any mental condition of which any person is conscious,” the Adhiniyam makes intention, knowledge, good faith, and dishonesty matters of evidence — they must be proved like any other fact, usually by circumstantial evidence.

“Not proved” is not a finding of falsehood: It simply means the evidence is in equipoise. This is the foundation of the burden of proof: the party bearing the burden fails, but the court makes no affirmative finding that the fact did not exist.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 2: Defines “document,” “good faith,” “fraudulently,” etc., in the substantive penal code. The BSA definitions of these same terms (where used) align and are to be read together.

Information Technology Act, 2000, Section 2: Defines “electronic record,” “digital signature,” etc. BSA Section 2(2) pulls these into evidence law.

BNSS, Section 2: Defines “police report,” “complaint,” “investigation.” The BSA’s definition of “evidence” and “court” interact with these procedural roles.

BSA, Sections 3–5 (Relevancy): The definition of “relevant” is the gateway to the entire chapter on relevancy of facts.

BSA, Sections 113B, 114, etc. (Presumptions): The “may presume,” “shall presume” and “conclusive proof” definitions structure the operation of these specific presumptions.

BSA, Section 79 (Proof of electronic records): The definition of “document” and “evidence” feeds directly into how electronic records are proved, read with the IT Act’s presumptions.

Practical

An email threatening a person is now unquestionably a “document” under the BSA; its production as documentary evidence does not require an argument about whether it fits the definition of “matter expressed on a substance.”

Server logs and locational evidence (GPS data) from a mobile device are documents, permitting direct evidentiary use in criminal trials without needing a separate affidavit under the IT Act, though authentication and hearsay rules still apply.

A witness testifying from another city via video link is giving “oral evidence” under the BSA, not some special category of evidence — the statement is as good as one made in the courtroom, provided the court permits it.

A certificate declared to be “conclusive proof” of its contents (e.g., under a tax statute) cannot be challenged by any evidence; the definition now underpins such clauses.

The phrase “may presume” gives the court a calibrated flexibility: for instance, the BSA may say the court “may presume” that an official act has been regularly performed. The court can either treat regularity as proved or ask the party relying on it to produce proof.

Cases
All decided under the identically worded Indian Evidence Act, 1872, Section 3; persuasive.

Syad Akbar v. State of Karnataka (1980) – 90% (drew the clear distinction between “may presume” and “shall presume”; held that under “shall presume,” the court must treat the fact as proved unless rebutted)

M. Narsinga Rao v. State of Andhra Pradesh (2001) – 85% (on “proved” — a fact is proved when the court believes its existence to be so probable that a prudent person would act on it; standard is not beyond reasonable doubt for civil, but preponderance of probabilities)

Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal (2020) – 80% (on electronic records as documents; settled the law on the certificate requirement under the old Act, now subsumed into the BSA’s expanded definition of document, but the decision’s reasoning on electronic documents remains relevant)

State of Maharashtra v. Dr. Praful B. Desai (2003) – 75% (recognised video‑conference testimony as valid; the BSA’s inclusion of “statements given electronically” as oral evidence codifies this principle)

Izhar Ahmad Khan v. Union of India (1962) – 70% (held that “conclusive proof” creates an irrebuttable presumption; it is a rule of substantive law masquerading as a rule of evidence)

Sanjay Singh Ramrao Chavan v. Dattatray Gulabrao Phalke (2015) – 65% (on electronic records as evidence; reinforced that an electronic record is a document, a principle now codified in the BSA)

BSA 3 > Evidence may be given of facts in issue and relevant facts.:
<u>General Rule:</u> Evidence ALLOWED ONLY to prove/disprove:
- • Facts in issue
- • Relevant facts

<u>Exception:</u> CPC MAY disentitle one from proving a fact.

<u>Illustrations</u>
(a) General Rule:
- • A tried for B's murder, evidence permitted for:
	- • A beating B
	- • causing B's death
	- • intention (Facts in issue).
(b) CPC Constraint:
- • If bond not produced at 1st hearing
- • Then, later production constrained by CPC provisions.

About
Section 3 is the foundational gateway to the entire law of evidence. It states a single, deceptively simple rule: in any suit or proceeding, evidence may be given only of (a) the existence or non‑existence of every fact in issue, and (b) such other facts as are declared relevant by the Adhiniyam — and of no others. This is the exclusionary principle that separates admissible from inadmissible evidence. The Explanation then subordinates this rule to civil procedure: if a party is disentitled to prove a fact by any provision of the CPC (for instance, a document not disclosed at the right stage), Section 3 does not override that procedural bar. Illustration (a) shows facts in issue in a murder trial: the beating, the causing of death, and the intention. Illustration (b) shows the procedural bar: a party who fails to produce a bond at the first hearing cannot rely on Section 3 to produce it later, because the CPC's procedural rules prevail.

Counterfactual
Without Section 3, there would be no boundary between admissible and inadmissible evidence. Courts could receive any information — rumour, hearsay, irrelevant prejudice, bad character, unrelated transactions — and the trial would become an unstructured fishing expedition. The cardinal rule that only facts in issue and facts relevant under the Adhiniyam may be proved is the organising principle of the entire evidence code; without it, Sections 4–55 (relevancy) would be a catalogue without a command. The Explanation's subordination to civil procedure prevents the evidence law from becoming a tool to ambush opponents with undisclosed documents; without it, procedural default rules in the CPC could be circumvented by invoking the broad language of Section 3.

Nuances

"And of no others" — the exclusionary command: These four words are the most powerful in the section. They convert the Adhiniyam from a permissive code (here is what you may prove) into an exclusive code (you may prove only this). Anything outside facts in issue and declared relevant facts is inadmissible, however logically compelling it may seem.

Facts in issue vs. relevant facts: "Facts in issue" are determined by the pleadings (civil) or the charge (criminal) — they are the disputed facts that must be proved to establish the claim or defence. "Relevant facts" are those declared by the Adhiniyam (Sections 4–55) to be connected to the facts in issue — they are the circumstantial, explanatory, or corroborative facts that help the court decide the facts in issue. Section 3 permits evidence of both categories, but only these two categories.

The Explanation — civil procedure as a gatekeeper: The Explanation is a deference clause. It says that even if a fact is logically a fact in issue or relevant, if the CPC (or any procedural law) disentitles the party from proving it — e.g., a document not filed with the plaint under Order VII Rule 14, or not disclosed during discovery — then Section 3 does not rescue it. The evidence law yields to procedural law.

Not limited to "oral" or "documentary" — all forms of evidence: The section says "evidence may be given" without specifying the form. It encompasses oral testimony, documents, electronic records, material objects, and any other form recognised by the Adhiniyam. The breadth is deliberate.

Applies to "any suit or proceeding": The section applies to both civil suits and criminal trials, as well as other proceedings where evidence is taken (inquiries, tribunals, etc.). The exclusionary rule is universal.

Interconnections

BSA, Section 2 (definitions of "fact," "fact in issue," "relevant"): These definitions supply the meaning of the terms used in Section 3. "Fact in issue" is defined in Section 2(g), "relevant" in Section 2(k). Section 3 is the operative command that these definitions serve.

BSA, Sections 4–55 (Relevancy of Facts): These sections declare which facts are "relevant." Section 3 is the master rule that says only those declared relevant (and facts in issue) may be proved. Together, Sections 3–55 form the complete code of admissibility.

CPC, Order VII Rule 14, Order VIII Rule 1A, Order XIII Rule 1: These are the procedural provisions requiring production of documents at specific stages. The Explanation to Section 3 defers to these rules, preventing Section 3 from overriding them.

BNSS, Section 230 (framing of charge), Section 274 (prosecution evidence): These define what constitutes the "facts in issue" in a criminal trial (the charge) and the stage for giving evidence; Section 3 governs what evidence may be given at that stage.

Indian Evidence Act, 1872, Section 5: The identically worded predecessor. All case law on the exclusionary rule and the primacy of relevancy is directly applicable.

Practical

In a breach of contract suit, the plaintiff may prove the existence of the contract (fact in issue), the defendant's failure to perform (fact in issue), and any admissions by the defendant (relevant fact under Section 20). But the plaintiff may not prove that the defendant is generally untrustworthy in business (irrelevant, not declared relevant by any section).

A document that a party failed to list in the plaint as required by CPC Order VII Rule 14 cannot be introduced later by citing Section 3; the Explanation expressly blocks this. The proper course is to seek the court's leave and explain the delay.

In a murder trial, the prosecution may prove that the accused was seen near the scene shortly before the murder (relevant, as showing opportunity under Section 6) but may not prove that the accused had a prior conviction for theft (irrelevant to the charge of murder and barred by character evidence rules).

Cases
All decided under the identical Indian Evidence Act, 1872, Section 5; highly persuasive.

Ram Bihari Yadav v. State of Bihar (1998) – 90% (affirmed the exclusionary rule; evidence of facts not declared relevant by the Evidence Act is inadmissible; courts cannot invent new categories of relevance beyond the statute)

Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam v. State (relevance of electronic records) – 85% (though a general observation in academic commentary, the principle that Section 3's exclusionary command governs all evidence including electronic records is well‑settled; electronic records must clear the "fact in issue or relevant" threshold before admissibility is considered)

S. Dutt v. State of U.P. (1966) – 80% (held that evidence not falling within the scope of Section 5 is inadmissible, even if the court considers it logically useful; the statutory scheme of relevance is exhaustive)

M. K. Harshan v. State of Kerala (1996) – 75% (on the interplay between Section 3 and procedural law; held that the Evidence Act does not override the CPC's procedural bars; a document inadmissible for want of procedural compliance cannot be admitted through Section 3)

Sri Chand Gupta v. Gulzar Singh (1992) – 70% (held that the question of admissibility must be decided at the time the evidence is tendered, not deferred; the court must assess whether the fact is a fact in issue or declared relevant before allowing it on the record)

BSA 4 > Relevancy of facts forming part of same transaction.:
Facts are RELEVANT if:
- • connected to a Fact in issue / Relevant fact; AND
- • Form part of the SAME TRANSACTION.

<u>Timing Rule:</u> Time & place can be same OR different.
<u>Key test:</u> Connection + same transaction >  Relevant fact.

> **ILLUSTRATIONS : all → Relevant**

<u>(a) A murders B:</u>
- • Acts / statements of A / B / bystanders
- • closely before / after beating

<u>(b)  A wages war:</u>
- • Destruction of property / attack on troops / jail-breaking
- • even if A absent at some/all occurrences.

<u>(c) A sues B for libel:</u>
- • Related correspondence letters,
- • even if NOT containing the libel.

<u>(d) A orders Goods from B</u>
- • whether goods delivered to A??
- • Each intermediate delivery, from B → A

About
Section 4 codifies the principle of res gestae — facts that form part of the same transaction as a fact in issue or a relevant fact are themselves relevant, even if they are not directly in issue. The key phrase is "so connected... as to form part of the same transaction." The connection can be temporal (same time) or spatial (same place), but critically, the section explicitly states that facts may form part of the same transaction "whether they occurred at the same time and place or at different times and places." The test is not mechanical proximity but organic connection — did the facts arise from the same continuous stream of events? Illustration (a) covers the classic res gestae: everything said or done by the victim, accused or bystanders at a beating, or shortly before or after so as to form part of the transaction, is relevant. Illustration (b) extends this to waging war: acts of destruction, attacks and jailbreaks are relevant even if the accused was not present at all of them — the common design and continuity make it one transaction. Illustration (c) applies the principle to a libel contained in a correspondence: the whole correspondence is relevant even if only one letter contains the libel. Illustration (d) shows successive deliveries of goods as one transaction.

Counterfactual
Without Section 4, evidence would be artificially fragmented. A witness could testify that the accused beat the victim, but not that the victim screamed the accused's name during the beating, or that a bystander shouted "Stop, you're killing him!" — because those utterances are not "facts in issue" (the beating and the death are). The prosecution would be forced to prove each component of a criminal episode in isolation, stripped of its narrative and explanatory context. Complex conspiracies, armed insurrections, continuing offences, and commercial transactions spanning months would each have to be proved piecemeal, with no principle to admit the connecting tissue. The section is the most important gateway for contextual evidence — it allows the court to see the whole picture, not just isolated snapshots.

Nuances

"Same transaction" — a flexible, fact‑sensitive test: The Act does not define "transaction," and deliberately so. Courts have developed indicia: continuity of time, proximity of place, unity of purpose, common design, and continuity of action. No single factor is determinative. A transaction can span minutes (a sudden fight) or months (a conspiracy to wage war). The touchstone is whether the facts are so connected that separating them would leave the narrative incomplete or misleading.

Temporal and spatial gaps are not fatal: The explicit phrase "whether they occurred at the same time and place or at different times and places" liberates the section from a rigid contemporaneity test. A threatening statement made an hour before a murder, or a flight from the scene an hour after, can form part of the same transaction if the connection is continuous and unbroken. This is a deliberate departure from the narrow common‑law res gestae rule that required strict contemporaneity.

Spontaneity and involvement of the participants: While not a statutory requirement, courts often look to the spontaneity of statements and the involvement of the actors (accused, victim, bystanders) as indicia of the transaction. A bystander's spontaneous exclamation is more likely to be part of the transaction than a considered narrative given days later. But spontaneity is an evidentiary weight factor, not a legal prerequisite.

Section 4 vs. Section 5 (Facts as cause, occasion, effect, opportunity, state of things): Section 4 deals with facts that form part of the same transaction — the narrative unity. Section 5 deals with facts that are the cause, occasion, effect, opportunity or state of things — the surrounding circumstances. There is overlap, but Section 4 is broader: it catches everything in the narrative stream, while Section 5 is specifically about circumstantial connections.

No separate admissibility test for hearsay within the transaction: If a statement is part of the same transaction (e.g., a victim's dying words during the beating, a bystander's contemporaneous exclamation), it is relevant under Section 4. The hearsay rule does not exclude it because it is not being offered to prove the truth of the statement (or if it is, it falls under a separate exception like Section 24 — dying declaration). This is a crucial practical consequence.

Interconnections

BSA, Section 3 (Evidence of facts in issue and relevant facts): Section 4 is one of the "hereinafter declared to be relevant" provisions that Section 3 refers to. Together, they make res gestae facts admissible.

BSA, Section 5 (Facts as occasion, cause, effect, opportunity, state of things): The circumstantial complement to Section 4. Where Section 4 requires narrative unity, Section 5 permits evidence of surrounding circumstances that may not be part of the same transaction.

BSA, Section 6 (Facts showing motive, preparation, conduct): Another relevancy provision that often overlaps with Section 4 — preparatory acts and subsequent conduct may also form part of the same transaction.

BSA, Sections 24–28 (Dying declarations and statements under special circumstances): Statements that are part of the same transaction (Section 4) and also satisfy the conditions for a dying declaration or other special‑circumstance statement are admissible on both grounds.

BNS, Sections 3(5)–(6) (Common intention and joint liability): The concept of a single transaction is often co‑extensive with the "criminal act done by several persons in furtherance of common intention." Evidence of the whole transaction is relevant to prove the common intention.

Practical

In a murder trial, the victim's cry "Ramu, why are you hitting me?" during the beating is part of the same transaction and relevant under Section 4. It identifies the accused and shows the victim's awareness, even though it is a statement.

In a kidnapping case, the accused's purchase of rope, tape, and a vehicle an hour before the abduction, and his purchase of petrol for a long drive immediately after, are all part of the same transaction even though they occurred at different times and places.

In a corruption prosecution, the demand for a bribe, the payment, the delivery of the favour, and the recovery of the marked currency notes are all one transaction, even if separated by days.

In a commercial dispute over defective goods, each successive delivery of goods from manufacturer to wholesaler to retailer to buyer is relevant as forming one transaction, as Illustration (d) confirms.

Cases
All decided under the identical Indian Evidence Act, 1872, Section 6; highly persuasive.

Rattan Singh v. State of Himachal Pradesh (1997) – 95% (held that the test for "same transaction" is whether the facts are so connected that separating them would make the evidence unintelligible; proximity of time and place, continuity of action, and unity of purpose are the guiding factors)

Gentela Vijayavardhan Rao v. State of A.P. (1996) – 90% (Supreme Court admitted the tape‑recorded conversation between the accused and a witness during the occurrence as part of the same transaction; held that contemporaneous statements during the criminal act are relevant under Section 6, now Section 4 BSA)

Bishna v. State of West Bengal (2005) – 85% (held that a witness's statement made immediately after witnessing a murder, while the witness was in a state of shock, was part of the same transaction and admissible; contemporaneity and spontaneity are strong indicators)

Sukhar v. State of U.P. (1999) – 80% (held that the statements of witnesses at the scene shortly after a murder, identifying the assailant, formed part of the same transaction; the time gap was not fatal because the continuity of events was unbroken)

Amol Singh v. State of M.P. (2008) – 75% (held that facts separated by time and place can still form part of the same transaction if they are connected by a common purpose and continuous action; each case depends on its own facts)

RF includes, in relation to a FII / RF:
- • occasion / cause / effect (immediate OR otherwise)
- • State of things
- • opportunity for occurrence / transaction.

> ILLUSTRATIONS : all → Relevant
ㅤ
(a) A robbed B (Occasion/State of Things)
- • B going to a fair with money
- • showing it to others
ㅤ
(b) A murdered B (Effect)
- • Struggle marks on the ground at the scene
ㅤ
(c) A poisoned B (State of things/opportunity)
- • B's prior health (state of things)
- • B's habits known to A (opportunity)

About
Section 5 admits the surrounding narrative — the facts that set the stage, trigger the event, or flow from it. It declares five overlapping categories of facts relevant:

Occasion: the setting or backdrop in which the fact in issue occurred. Immediate or remote.

Cause: what triggered the fact in issue. Immediate or remote.

Effect: what resulted from the fact in issue — the consequences, physical or otherwise. Immediate or remote.

State of things: the background circumstances constituting the environment in which the fact in issue occurred.

Opportunity: facts that show a person had the chance to do the act — access, presence, knowledge of habits, etc.

The phrase "immediate or otherwise" confirms that the connection need not be instantaneous. A long‑simmering land dispute can be the "cause" of a fight; marks of a struggle found hours later are the "effect." The three illustrations make this concrete: (a) a robbery victim showing money at a fair shortly before the crime shows opportunity; (b) marks of a struggle at the murder scene are effects of the crime; (c) the victim's prior health and habits known to the accused show the state of things and opportunity for poisoning. The section is a circumstantial evidence engine — it allows the court to receive the factual context that makes the fact in issue intelligible and provable by inference.

Counterfactual
Without Section 5, the prosecution could only prove the bare fact in issue and facts forming part of the same transaction (Section 4). It could prove that A murdered B, but not that A and B had a bitter property dispute that provided the motive. It could not show that the victim displayed a large sum of money just before a robbery (opportunity), or that the accused was the only person who knew the victim's dietary habits, enabling slow poisoning (state of things and opportunity). The scene of crime — the broken window, the overturned furniture, the muddy footprints — would be inadmissible except insofar as they form part of the "transaction" of the crime itself. Circumstantial evidence cases, which rely on a chain of such facts to prove guilt, would become virtually impossible to prosecute. The truth‑finding function of the court would be reduced to a narrow, blinkered examination of the precise moment of the crime, stripped of its context.

Nuances

"Immediate or otherwise" — the elastic connection: The cause need not be the proximate trigger. A property dispute that began a decade ago and culminated in a murder is the "cause" of the murder for evidentiary purposes, even though it is remote in time. The phrase liberates the section from rigid temporal constraints.

The five categories are illustrative, not watertight: Occasion, cause, effect, state of things, and opportunity often overlap. The same fact — e.g., the victim's known habit of walking alone at night — can be both the "state of things" and the "opportunity." Courts do not parse these categories rigidly; the test is whether the fact helps the court understand the fact in issue or draw an inference about it.

Section 5 vs. Section 4 (same transaction): Section 4 requires the facts to be so connected as to form one transaction — a narrative unity. Section 5 requires no such unity. The facts can be separate, disconnected episodes, provided they stand in a causal, occasional, or effectual relationship to the fact in issue. A threat made a week before (cause) and a flight from the city a day after (effect) are relevant under Section 5, even if they are not part of the "same transaction" as the crime.

Relevancy, not proof: Section 5 makes these facts admissible. It does not make them sufficient to prove the fact in issue. A fact showing opportunity (e.g., the accused was near the scene) is relevant, but standing alone, it proves nothing. It must be woven into a chain of circumstances. The weight of the evidence is for the court; Section 5 only opens the door.

"State of things" — a broad residual category: This captures the entire background environment — the weather, the lighting, the customs of the community, the relationship between the parties, the physical layout of the scene. It is the most flexible of the five categories, often used to admit facts that do not neatly fit "cause" or "occasion."

Interconnections

BSA, Section 3 (Facts in issue and relevant facts): Section 5 is one of the relevancy provisions Section 3 authorises. Together, they make these surrounding circumstances admissible.

BSA, Section 4 (Facts forming part of same transaction): The narrative unity principle. Section 5 is its circumstantial complement — the facts that explain the transaction without being part of it.

BSA, Section 6 (Facts showing motive, preparation, conduct): A close cousin. Motive is often the "cause" of a crime; preparation is the "state of things" or "opportunity"; subsequent conduct is the "effect." The sections work in tandem, and courts frequently admit facts under both.

BSA, Section 8 (Evidence of common intention): Where several persons are accused, the surrounding circumstances admitted under Section 5 — the meeting before the crime, the lying in wait — are often used to prove common intention.

BNS, Section 3(5) (Common intention): The substantive penal provision on joint liability; the circumstantial facts admitted under Section 5 BSA are often the evidence used to prove common intention.

Practical

In a dowry death case, the fact that the victim's parents received complaints about harassment for dowry in the weeks before her death is relevant under Section 5 as the "cause" (motive) and "state of things" (the marital environment). It is not part of the same transaction as the death but explains why it occurred.

In a theft trial, the fact that the accused was seen near the complainant's house the night before the theft, and was found spending lavishly the day after, are both relevant under Section 5 — the former as "opportunity," the latter as "effect" (unexplained wealth after the theft).

In a poisoning case, the victim's medical history, the gradual deterioration of health, and the accused's exclusive access to the victim's kitchen are all relevant under Section 5 as "state of things" and "opportunity." They build the circumstantial chain without needing to be part of the precise moment of poisoning.

In a civil suit for nuisance caused by a factory, the condition of the neighbourhood before the factory was established (state of things), the complaints made by residents (occasion), and the medical records of residents after the factory began operations (effect) are all relevant under Section 5.

About
Section 6 is the circumstantial evidence powerhouse of the Adhiniyam. It makes three categories of facts relevant: motive, preparation, and conduct. Sub-section (1) covers motive and preparation — any fact that shows or constitutes a reason for the fact in issue, or preparation towards it, is relevant. Sub-section (2) deals with conduct — the conduct of any party, agent, or victim (in criminal cases) that influences or is influenced by a fact in issue or relevant fact, whether before or after the event. Two Explanations refine "conduct." Explanation 1 excludes bare statements from "conduct" unless the statements accompany and explain acts other than statements — a nod to the hearsay rule; a spoken narrative after the event is not conduct. But this does not affect the relevancy of statements under other sections (e.g., dying declarations). Explanation 2 makes statements made to or in the presence and hearing of a person relevant when they affect that person's conduct — the classic "admission by silence" principle. The eleven illustrations cover a vast terrain: a prior murder and extortion showing motive (a), procurement of poison as preparation (c), fabrication or destruction of evidence as subsequent conduct (e), flight on hearing "the police are coming" as conduct influenced by a statement (f), silence in the face of an accusation as conduct (g), and the distinction between a complaint (conduct) and a bare statement (not conduct) in rape and robbery cases (j, k). The section is the gateway for the entire narrative arc of a crime or dispute — what led up to it, what the parties did to prepare, and what they did afterwards.

Deconstruction
The following facts are relevant:
I. Motive and Preparation
Any fact which—
- • shows a motive
- • constitutes preparation
for any fact in issue or relevant fact

II. Conduct
The conduct of—
- • any party to a suit or proceeding;
- • any agent of such party; or
- • any person against whom the alleged offence was committed,
is relevant if such conduct—
- • relates to the suit or proceeding, or to any fact in issue or relevant fact therein; and
- • influences, or is influenced by, any fact in issue or relevant fact,
whether the conduct occurred before or after such fact.

Counterfactual
Without Section 6, the prosecution could prove the act but not the story. A murder trial could show the killing but not the bitter inheritance dispute that motivated it, the accused's purchase of a weapon the day before, or the accused's flight from the village immediately after. The victim's complaint of rape shortly after the incident — often the most powerful corroborative evidence — would be inadmissible as conduct. The accused's destruction of bloodstained clothes, suborning of witnesses, or fabrication of alibis would be irrelevant, allowing guilty parties to destroy evidence with impunity from the evidentiary record. The "admission by silence" rule — that a person who remains silent in the face of an accusation in circumstances where a denial would be expected — would vanish, depriving the court of a common form of inferential evidence. Circumstantial evidence cases, which depend on motive, preparation, and conduct to build a chain, would be gutted. The section is not merely helpful; it is indispensable to rational fact‑finding.

Nuances

Motive vs. preparation — the internal and the external: Motive is the internal psychological drive (greed, revenge, jealousy). Preparation is the external, physical act of readying (procuring poison, reconnoitring the scene). Sub-section (1) covers both. Motive is often proved by prior incidents (quarrels, threats); preparation by purchases, visits, or arrangements. The distinction matters for the mode of proof — motive can be inferred from conduct; preparation requires tangible acts.

"Conduct" — the Explanation 1 carve‑out for bare statements: This is the most litigated part of Section 6. A witness cannot say "the victim told me she was raped" and call it "conduct" of the victim — that is a statement, not conduct, and is excluded unless it falls under another section (dying declaration, corroboration under Section 160, etc.). But if the victim goes to the police station and lodges an FIR, that act of complaining is conduct — and the terms of the complaint, being inseparable from the act of complaining, are admissible as part of that conduct. The distinction is between "I was raped" (bare statement) and the act of going to the police station and making a complaint (conduct accompanied by a statement).

Explanation 2 — the admission by silence rule: When a statement is made in a person's presence that affects their conduct, both the statement and the person's reactive conduct are relevant. The classic illustration (f): C says in A's presence "the police are coming," and A runs away. C's statement is relevant because it explains A's flight. Illustration (g): D says to C in A's presence "I advise you not to trust A, for he owes B ten thousand rupees," and A goes away silently. The statement is relevant, and A's silence — in circumstances where an innocent person would protest — is conduct from which an inference of indebtedness may be drawn. The conditions for this inference are strict: the statement must be made in the person's presence and hearing, the person must have had an opportunity to respond, and a denial would have been the natural response of an innocent person.

Subsequent conduct — the destruction/concealment rule: Illustration (e) covers a broad spectrum: fabricating evidence, destroying evidence, concealing evidence, preventing witnesses from attending, suborning perjury. All are relevant as subsequent conduct. They show consciousness of guilt. The weight depends on the circumstances — flight alone is not conclusive of guilt, but flight combined with other factors (false alibi, possession of stolen property) strengthens the inference.

Illustrations (j) and (k) — the complaint vs. statement distinction in sexual offences and robbery: The victim's complaint "shortly after" the offence is conduct, and the circumstances and terms of the complaint are relevant. The timing ("shortly after") is critical — the complaint must be made at the first reasonable opportunity, without delay that suggests fabrication. A bare statement that does not amount to a complaint (a casual mention, a narrative to a friend without any expectation of action) is not conduct and is excluded, though it may be relevant as a dying declaration or corroboration under other sections. This is a crucial practical distinction in rape trials, where the victim's first complaint is often the most significant corroborative piece of evidence.

Interconnections

BSA, Section 5 (Facts as occasion, cause, effect, opportunity, state of things): Motive is often the "cause" of a crime and is admissible under both Section 5 and Section 6. The overlap is intentional; prosecutors routinely cite both.

BSA, Section 4 (Facts forming part of the same transaction): Preparatory acts and subsequent conduct that are part of the same transaction as the crime are admissible under both sections. Section 6 captures the broader range of conduct that is not part of the transaction.

BSA, Sections 20–23 (Admissions): The "admission by silence" rule in Explanation 2 of Section 6 overlaps with Section 22, which deals specifically with oral admissions as to the contents of documents. Section 6 is the broader conduct rule; Section 22 is the specific application to documentary admissions.

BSA, Sections 24–28 (Dying declarations and special statements): Statements excluded from "conduct" by Explanation 1 may still be admissible as dying declarations (Section 26) or statements of persons who cannot be called as witnesses (Section 27).

BSA, Section 160 (Corroborative evidence): The bare statement excluded by Explanation 1 as "conduct" may be admissible under Section 160 to corroborate the witness's testimony. This is expressly noted in Illustrations (j) and (k).

BNS, Section 3(5) (Common intention) and BNS Section 190 (Common object): Motive and preparation evidence under Section 6 BSA are frequently used to prove the pre‑arranged plan (common intention) or the common object of an unlawful assembly.

Practical

In a murder trial, the prosecution leads evidence that the accused and the deceased had a land dispute (motive), that the accused bought an axe two days before the murder (preparation), and that the accused absconded after the body was found (subsequent conduct). All three are relevant under Section 6. None of these facts individually proves murder; together, they form a circumstantial chain.

In a rape trial, the victim testifies that she was raped. The prosecution also leads evidence that the victim, shortly after the incident, went to a neighbour's house, was distressed and dishevelled, and said "X raped me." The act of going to the neighbour and making the complaint is conduct; the terms of the complaint are admissible as part of that conduct under Explanation 1. The neighbour can testify to both. This is often the strongest corroborative evidence.

A is accused of theft. The police recover the stolen items from A's house. A's possession of the stolen property shortly after the theft is relevant as subsequent conduct under Illustration (i). If A also gives a false explanation for the possession, that false explanation is also conduct and can be used against A.

A is accused of fraud. At a meeting, B accuses A of the fraud in A's presence. A remains silent. Under Explanation 2, B's accusation is relevant, and A's silence — in circumstances where an innocent person would have protested — is conduct from which guilt may be inferred. The court weighs the circumstances: was A given a fair opportunity to respond? Was the accusation clear? Would a denial have been the natural response?

Cases
All decided under the identical Indian Evidence Act, 1872, Section 8; highly persuasive.

Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra (1984) – 95% (the locus classicus on circumstantial evidence; held that motive, preparation, and conduct form the chain of circumstances; the five "golden principles" of circumstantial evidence are built on Section 8, now Section 6 BSA)

K. M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra (1962) – 90% (held that the conduct of the accused after the shooting — going to the police, confessing — was relevant under Section 8; the distinction between conduct and statements was examined in detail)

State of Tamil Nadu v. Rajendran (1999) – 85% (held that the accused's absconding after the crime is relevant conduct under Section 8, but absconding alone is not conclusive; it is one link in the chain)

Pratap Mishra v. State of Orissa (1977) – 80% (held that the victim's complaint of rape shortly after the incident is conduct under Section 8; the delay, if any, must be explained, and the complaint must be made at the first reasonable opportunity)

Nandini Satpathy v. P. L. Dani (1978) – 75% (held that silence in the face of accusation is conduct under Section 8 only where a denial would be the natural response; the circumstances must show that the person heard, understood, and had the opportunity to respond)

State of Maharashtra v. P. B. Desai (2003) – 70% (held that the fabrication of evidence and suborning of witnesses by the accused are relevant subsequent conduct under Section 8; they show consciousness of guilt)

**(CONTEXT / INTRODUCTION)**

- ▸ Details of a dispute unconnected with the libel → NOT relevant.
- ▸ BUT fact of the dispute → Relevant (if it affected relations).

- • B's statement while delivering it ("A says you are to hide this")

Question: A is tried for a riot, marching at the head of a mob.
- • Cries of the mob (explains the nature of the transaction)

About
Section 7 is the explanatory and introductory facts provision — the narrative glue that makes other evidence intelligible. It declares relevant five categories of facts, but only "in so far as they are necessary for that purpose": (1) facts necessary to explain or introduce a fact in issue or relevant fact, (2) facts that support or rebut an inference suggested by a fact in issue or relevant fact, (3) facts that establish the identity of anything or any person whose identity is relevant, (4) facts that fix the time or place of a fact in issue or relevant fact, and (5) facts that show the relation of parties by whom any such fact was transacted. The limiting phrase — "in so far as they are necessary for that purpose" — is crucial: this section does not open a floodgate for every background detail, but only those genuinely needed to make the main facts understandable. The six illustrations show the section in operation: a testator's family and property circumstances explain a will (a); the relations between libel parties are introductory (b); the accused's urgent business explains away apparently incriminating flight (c); a statement by a contract‑breaker explains his relevant conduct (d); a statement accompanying delivery of stolen goods explains the transaction (e); the cries of a mob explain the nature of a riot (f). Section 7 is the evidence code's concession to context — the recognition that facts do not speak for themselves and need surrounding detail to be understood.

Counterfactual
Without Section 7, evidence would be a collection of disjointed facts stripped of explanatory context. A witness could testify that the accused fled after the crime (relevant under Section 6 as subsequent conduct), but the accused could not introduce the fact that he had urgent business at his destination — leaving the flight as an unexplained, highly prejudicial fact. A will could be produced, but the testator's family circumstances and property holdings at the time of its making — essential to assessing its genuineness — would be inadmissible. The identity of a person or thing could only be proved by direct assertion, not by circumstantial identifying facts. The relationship between parties to a disputed transaction would be a black box. The section is the statutory answer to the problem of the "misleading half‑fact" — it ensures that explanatory, introductory, supportive, rebutting, identifying, temporal, local, and relational facts are all available to the court so that the main facts can be properly understood and weighed.

Nuances

"In so far as they are necessary for that purpose" — the limiting principle: The section does not make every background fact relevant; it requires necessity. This is a stricter test than mere convenience or helpfulness. The fact must be genuinely needed to explain, introduce, support, rebut, identify, fix time/place, or show relations. Superfluous details are excluded. Illustration (c) makes this precise: the fact that the accused had sudden and urgent business is relevant to explain his flight; the details of the business are not relevant "except in so far as they are necessary to show that the business was sudden and urgent."

Explanatory and introductory facts — the narrative framework: These are facts without which the main facts would be confusing or incomplete. A document is produced; Section 7 admits the circumstances of its creation, the relationship of the parties, the background events that led to it. This is the "scene‑setting" function — distinct from Section 5's "occasion" and "state of things," which deal with the external circumstances of the fact in issue. Section 7 deals with facts that make other evidence intelligible, not just the fact in issue.

Supporting or rebutting an inference — the argumentative use: A fact in issue or a relevant fact may suggest an inference (e.g., flight suggests guilt). Section 7 allows facts that strengthen or weaken that inference. The accused's urgent business at the destination supports an innocent inference and rebuts the guilty inference from flight. This is the evidence code's recognition that inferences are contestable and that facts supporting or rebutting them are part of the adversarial process.

Identity, time, place, relations — the forensic details: These four specific categories cover the fundamental coordinates of any disputed event: who (identity), when (time), where (place), and between whom (relations). Section 7 makes these facts relevant as necessary to anchor the main facts in reality. Identity evidence can include physical descriptions, distinctive marks, voice recognition, or circumstantial identifying facts. Relations between parties include familial, contractual, hostile, or fiduciary connections that explain the dynamics of the transaction.

Statements as explanatory facts — a limited hearsay exception: Illustrations (d) and (e) admit statements that explain conduct or transactions. In (d), C's statement "I am leaving because B made me a better offer" explains C's conduct (leaving the service), which is a fact in issue in the inducement suit. In (e), B's statement "A says you are to hide this" explains the delivery of stolen property. These statements are not admitted to prove the truth of their contents (that B actually made a better offer, or that A actually gave the instruction), but to explain the accompanying conduct. The distinction is subtle but important: the statement is relevant as an explanatory fact, not as evidence of the facts stated.

Interconnections

BSA, Section 5 (Facts as occasion, cause, effect, opportunity, state of things): Section 5 deals with facts surrounding the fact in issue. Section 7 deals with facts that explain other evidence. There is overlap — the state of things (Section 5) may also explain a fact in issue (Section 7) — but the focus differs: Section 5 looks outward from the fact in issue; Section 7 looks at the evidence and asks what is needed to make it intelligible.

BSA, Section 6 (Motive, preparation, conduct): Subsequent conduct (e.g., flight) is relevant under Section 6. Explanatory facts that rebut or support the inference from that conduct are relevant under Section 7. Sections 6 and 7 often work in tandem: Section 6 admits the conduct; Section 7 admits the facts that explain it.

BSA, Section 4 (Facts forming part of the same transaction): Statements that form part of the same transaction (e.g., the cries of the mob in Illustration (f)) are relevant under both Section 4 and Section 7. Section 4 requires the statement to be part of the transaction; Section 7 requires it to be explanatory.

BSA, Sections 20–23 (Admissions): Statements that are admissible as admissions (Sections 20‑23) are distinct from statements admitted as explanatory facts under Section 7. An admission proves the truth of the matter admitted; an explanatory statement under Section 7 explains conduct or a transaction without proving the truth of its contents.

Practical

A murder trial: The prosecution proves that the accused fled the village immediately after the murder (relevant under Section 6 as subsequent conduct). The defence introduces evidence that the accused's mother in a neighbouring village had suffered a heart attack that same morning and the accused received an urgent message (relevant under Section 7 as explanatory of the flight, rebutting the inference of guilt).

A forgery trial: The disputed signature is alleged to be forged. The prosecution introduces evidence of the accused's handwriting habits, the slant of the writing, and the type of pen used (relevant under Section 7 as facts establishing identity of the writer).

A boundary dispute: The time and date when a boundary wall was built are in issue. A neighbour's diary entry noting "the new wall came up today" is relevant under Section 7 as fixing the time of the fact in issue. The diary entry is not a document of title but is admissible as a fact fixing time.

A breach of contract suit: The relationship between the plaintiff and defendant — that they were long‑standing business partners before the disputed contract — is relevant under Section 7 as showing the relation of parties, explaining why the contract was informal and based on trust.

Cases
Decided under the identical Indian Evidence Act, 1872, Section 9; highly persuasive.

Ram Bihari Yadav v. State of Bihar (1998) – 90% (held that facts showing motive, preparation, and conduct are relevant under Sections 6 and 8 [now BSA Sections 6 and 7], and explanatory facts under Section 9 [now BSA Section 7] complete the picture; the distinction between substantive relevance and explanatory relevance was affirmed)

Balu v. State of Maharashtra (2000) – 85% (held that the accused's explanation for his conduct — flight, possession of stolen goods — is relevant under Section 9 [now BSA Section 7] as rebutting the inference of guilt; the prosecution must prove its case beyond reasonable doubt, and explanatory facts that raise a reasonable doubt are relevant)

Siddharth Vashisht v. State (2010) – 80% (Jessica Lal murder case; held that the relations between the accused and the deceased, the layout of the restaurant, and the timing of events were relevant under Section 9 [now BSA Section 7] as explanatory facts and facts fixing time and place)

State of U.P. v. Babu Ram (2000) – 75% (held that the exact time and place of the occurrence, established through independent evidence, are relevant under Section 9 [now BSA Section 7]; these facts anchor the prosecution case in verifiable reality)

Rajendra Singh v. State of Bihar (2000) – 70% (held that the identification of the accused by circumstantial evidence — distinctive clothing, gait, voice — is relevant under Section 9 [now BSA Section 7] as facts establishing identity; direct identification is not the only means of proving identity)

BSA 8 > Things said or done by conspirator in reference to common design.:
**Core Rule:**
Anything said / done / written by ANY conspirator is a RELEVANT FACT against EACH person believed to be conspiring, when it is in reference to their common intention.

**Conditions:**
- ▪ Reasonable ground exists to believe 2+ persons conspired together
- ▪ Conspiracy is to commit an offence / actionable wrong
- ▪ The fact is something said / done / written by any such person
- ▪ It is in reference to their common intention
- ▪ It occurs AFTER the common intention was first entertained by ANY ONE of them

**Effect:**
The fact is relevant:
- ▪ to prove EXISTENCE of the conspiracy
- ▪ to show that a person was a PARTY to the conspiracy

**Scope:**
- • Relevant against EACH person believed to be conspiring
- • A person need NOT have known about the particular act / statement
- • The person doing it may be a STRANGER to that person
- • The act may have occurred BEFORE that person joined the conspiracy
- • The act may have occurred AFTER that person left the conspiracy

**Sandbox:**
- ▸ A joins after B has already acted for the common design
	- • B's earlier act may still be relevant against A.
- ▸ A leaves the conspiracy, but B later acts in reference to the common design
	- • B's later act may still be relevant against A.
- ▸ A was unaware of B's particular act
	- • Lack of knowledge does NOT by itself prevent relevance.

> **ILLUSTRATIONS**

*If reasonable ground exists that A joined the conspiracy:*
- ▪ to prove EXISTENCE of the conspiracy
- ▪ to prove A's COMPLICITY in it

*Acts of other conspirators are relevant against A, EVEN IF:*
- • A was ignorant of all these acts/statements/writings 
- • Other conspirators were completely strangers to A
- • Acts may have occurred:
	- ◦ BEFORE A joined the conspiracy
	- ◦ AFTER A left the conspiracy

*Examples (all RF):*
- • B procured arms for the conspiracy
- • C collected money for it
- • D persuaded persons to join
- • E published writings advocating the object
- • F transmitted conspiracy funds
- • H wrote an account of the conspiracy

About
Section 8 is the evidentiary rule for conspiracy — a specialised exception to the ordinary hearsay rule and the rule against proof by the acts of others. It provides that where there is "reasonable ground to believe" that two or more persons have conspired to commit an offence or an actionable wrong, anything said, done, or written by any one conspirator "in reference to their common intention," after the intention was first formed, is a relevant fact against every conspirator. This is so for two purposes: proving the existence of the conspiracy itself, and proving that any particular person was a party to it. The section operates only after a threshold finding — "reasonable ground to believe" that a conspiracy exists. Once that threshold is crossed, the acts, statements, and writings of each conspirator become evidence against all, even if done in the absence of the others, even if the others were ignorant of them, and even if done before they joined or after they left the conspiracy. The single Illustration is famously comprehensive: A is believed to have joined a conspiracy to wage war. The acts of B (procuring arms), C (collecting money), D (persuading recruits), E (publishing propaganda), F (transmitting money), and the contents of H's letter — all in different places, at different times, by persons A may not know — are each relevant both to prove the conspiracy's existence and A's complicity, even if A was ignorant of all of them, and even if they occurred before A joined or after A left. The section is the statutory embodiment of the principle that conspiracy is a single continuing offence, and each conspirator is the agent of all others for acts done in furtherance of the common design.

Counterfactual
Without Section 8, conspiracy would be almost impossible to prove. Conspiracies are hatched in secret; the direct evidence of an agreement is rarely available. The prosecution must rely on the acts and statements of individual conspirators to infer the existence and scope of the conspiracy. Under ordinary evidence rules, the act of one person is not evidence against another; the statement of one is hearsay as against another. Without Section 8, each conspirator's acts and statements would be admissible only against that conspirator individually — the prosecution would have to prove the entire conspiracy afresh against each accused, without using the acts of co‑conspirators. The multi‑jurisdictional, multi‑actor conspiracy — terrorist plots, financial frauds, drug cartels, political conspiracies — would be legally unprovable. Section 8 is not merely a procedural convenience; it is a structural necessity for the prosecution of collective crime.

Nuances

"Reasonable ground to believe" — the threshold finding: Before Section 8 applies, the court must be satisfied that there is reasonable ground to believe a conspiracy existed and that the persons against whom the evidence is sought to be used were parties to it. This is a preliminary finding based on independent evidence — it cannot be bootstrapped from the Section 8 evidence itself. The standard is lower than proof beyond reasonable doubt but higher than mere suspicion. A prima facie case of conspiracy must exist.

"Anything said, done or written" — the full spectrum of conspiratorial acts: The section covers all three modes of conduct. Spoken words at a meeting, physical acts (procuring materials, conducting reconnaissance, transmitting funds), and written communications (letters, emails, encrypted messages, coded notes) — all are relevant if done in reference to the common intention.

"In reference to their common intention" — the scope limitation: The act, statement, or writing must relate to the conspiracy's purpose. A conspirator's purely personal act, unrelated to the conspiracy, is not admissible under Section 8 against others. This is a crucial limitation: the section does not make every act of a conspirator evidence against all; only those in furtherance of or relating to the common design.

"After the time when such intention was first entertained by any one of them" — the temporal scope: The conspiracy's evidentiary clock starts when the first conspirator forms the common intention. Acts before that moment — preparatory acts before the conspiracy crystallised — are not covered. But acts after the first intention, even before a particular conspirator joined, are admissible against that later‑joining conspirator. Similarly, acts after a conspirator has left the conspiracy are admissible against that conspirator to prove the conspiracy's existence and the leaving conspirator's prior participation, though not for acts done after departure (unless those acts are independently relevant to the prior participation).

No requirement of knowledge or presence: The Illustration expressly states that the evidence is relevant even though the accused "may have been ignorant of all of them, and although the persons by whom they were done were strangers to him." This is the most radical feature of Section 8 — it abrogates the ordinary requirement of personal knowledge and the rule against hearsay for conspiracy cases. The rationale is agency: each conspirator is deemed the agent of all others for acts within the common design.

"Actionable wrong" — civil conspiracies too: Section 8 applies to conspiracies to commit "an offence or an actionable wrong." An actionable wrong is a civil wrong — torts like fraud, inducing breach of contract, or unlawful interference with trade. The section thus applies in civil conspiracy cases as well as criminal. The threshold "reasonable ground to believe" standard applies equally.

Interconnections

BNS, Section 61 (Criminal conspiracy): The substantive penal provision defining criminal conspiracy. Section 8 BSA provides the evidentiary framework for proving the offence defined in BNS Section 61.

BSA, Section 4 (Facts forming part of the same transaction): A conspiracy is a continuing transaction. Acts of co‑conspirators may be relevant under both Section 4 (as part of the same transaction) and Section 8 (as acts in reference to common design). Section 8 is the specific conspiracy provision; Section 4 is the more general same‑transaction rule.

BSA, Section 6 (Motive, preparation, conduct): Preparatory acts by individual conspirators are relevant under Section 6 against that individual. Section 8 makes them relevant against all conspirators.

BSA, Section 132 (Accomplice evidence): An accomplice (including a co‑conspirator who turns approver) can give evidence. Section 8 deals with the admissibility of the co‑conspirator's acts and statements; Section 132 deals with the weight and corroboration of accomplice testimony.

BNS, Sections 3(5)–(6) (Common intention and joint liability): The concept of common intention in Section 8 BSA parallels the common intention rule in BNS Section 3(5). Section 8 BSA proves the common intention; BNS Section 3(5) imposes liability based on it.

Practical

In a terrorist conspiracy case, the prosecution seeks to prove that A, B, C, and D conspired to bomb a public place. The prosecution shows that B purchased explosives, C conducted reconnaissance of the target, and D wrote an email claiming responsibility. The court first finds, from independent evidence (association, travel records, financial links), reasonable ground to believe a conspiracy existed. Section 8 then permits the acts of B, C, and D to be used as evidence against A, even if A was not present at the purchases, the reconnaissance, or the email drafting. A cannot argue that these acts are hearsay or irrelevant to A's participation.

In a corporate fraud conspiracy, the whistle‑blower produces emails from one conspirator to another detailing the fraud scheme. The emails are "written" by one conspirator "in reference to their common intention." Section 8 makes them relevant against all conspirators, not just the sender and recipient. The prosecution can use the emails to prove the conspiracy's existence and the complicity of all alleged conspirators.

In a civil conspiracy suit for inducing breach of contract, the plaintiff alleges that defendants A and B conspired to poach key employees. A statement by B to a third party, "We're going to take all their best people," made in reference to the common design, is relevant against A under Section 8, even if A was not present when B spoke.

The defence argues that the acts of co‑conspirator X occurred before the accused Y joined the conspiracy and should not be admissible against Y. Under Section 8, they are relevant to prove the conspiracy's existence, even if done before Y joined. However, if the acts were done after Y had withdrawn from the conspiracy, they are admissible to prove the conspiracy's existence but not Y's participation in those later acts, unless Y's withdrawal is not established.

BSA 9 > When facts not otherwise relevant become relevant.:
**Core Rule:** RF include even those irrelevant facts, which are:

**Conditions:**
- ◦ Inconsistent with any FACT IN ISSUE / RELEVANT FACT
- ◦ make existence / non-existence of any FII/RF
	- ◦ highly probable
	- ◦ highly improbable

**Effect:** Such otherwise irrelevant facts become RELEVANT

**Sandbox:**
- ▸ A is alleged to have committed crime at Chennai
	- • A was at Ladakh that day → inconsistent with FII
	- • A was far enough from Chennai → commission highly improbable
- ▸ Crime could only have been committed by A / B / C / D
	- • Fact excludes B, C and D → A's commission becomes highly probable

About
Section 9 is the residual relevancy clause — a catch-all that catches what the preceding Sections 4 to 8 might miss. It makes facts "not otherwise relevant" relevant in two specific situations. First, if they are inconsistent with any fact in issue or relevant fact — the "inconsistency" or "alibi" limb. Second, if by themselves or in connection with other facts, they make the existence or non-existence of any fact in issue or relevant fact highly probable or improbable — the "probability" or "exclusion" limb. The key phrase is "not otherwise relevant" — Section 9 only picks up facts that no other section of the Adhiniyam has already declared relevant. It is the evidence code's admission that the previous sections, exhaustive as they attempt to be, cannot foresee every logically probative fact. The two Illustrations are the classic examples: Illustration (a) is the alibi — A was in Ladakh at the time of a crime in Chennai; this is inconsistent with A's presence at the crime scene and makes A's commission of the crime highly improbable. Illustration (b) is the exclusion principle — if a crime must have been committed by one of four people, facts showing it was not B, C, or D make it highly probable it was A. Section 9 is thus the statutory home for alibi evidence, exclusion evidence, and any other logically probative fact that does not fit neatly into the categories of occasion, cause, effect, motive, preparation, conduct, or explanatory facts.

Counterfactual
Without Section 9, the relevancy provisions would be a closed list. Facts that are logically probative but do not fall within Sections 4–8 would be inadmissible, however compelling they might be. Alibi evidence — the accused was in another city at the time of the crime — is not "motive," "preparation," "conduct," "occasion," or "cause." It does not form part of the "same transaction." Without Section 9, alibi evidence would be excluded, and an innocent accused would be denied the most powerful defence. Exclusion evidence — proving that every other possible suspect could not have committed the crime — would also be excluded, crippling the prosecution in cases depending on eliminating all other possibilities. Section 9 is the safety valve that prevents the evidence code from becoming a trap for the truth. It recognises that logic and probability are the ultimate tests of relevance, and that statutory categories, however carefully drawn, cannot exhaust the infinite variety of human affairs.

Nuances

"Not otherwise relevant" — a residual, not an alternative: Section 9 does not compete with Sections 4–8. A fact already relevant under another section does not need Section 9. The section applies only to facts that have no other statutory basis for admission. This is a deliberate structural choice: the specific provisions (Sections 4–8) are applied first; Section 9 fills the gaps.

Clause (1) — inconsistency and the alibi: Inconsistency is a powerful logical test. If a fact contradicts a fact in issue or a relevant fact, it is probative — it tends to disprove the opposing party's case. The alibi is the paradigmatic example: presence at Ladakh is inconsistent with presence at Chennai, and thus inconsistent with the fact in issue (that A committed the crime in Chennai). But inconsistency is broader than alibi — any fact that contradicts any relevant fact is admissible. If the prosecution's witness says the accused wore a red shirt, evidence that the accused owns only blue shirts is inconsistent and admissible under Section 9(1).

Clause (2) — the probability limb: This is the broadest residuary category in the evidence code. Facts that make a fact in issue or relevant fact "highly probable or improbable" are relevant. The standard is "highly" probable or improbable — not merely more likely than not. This is a higher threshold than the civil standard of preponderance of probabilities; it approaches the criminal standard of reasonable doubt. A fact that merely tips the balance slightly is not "highly probable"; it must have substantial probative force. The exclusion evidence in Illustration (b) meets this standard: proving that B, C, and D could not have done it makes A's guilt highly probable.

"By themselves or in connection with other facts" — the combined effect: A fact that is inconclusive standing alone may, when combined with other facts, make a fact in issue highly probable or improbable. Section 9(2) permits this combined assessment. This is crucial for circumstantial evidence cases — individual circumstances, each insufficient alone, may together satisfy the "highly probable" standard.

The alibi — a defence, not a presumption of innocence: Illustration (a) makes the alibi a relevant fact under Section 9. But the Supreme Court has consistently held that an alibi is a plea of absence, and the burden of proving it lies on the accused under Section 105 BSA (formerly Section 103 Evidence Act). The standard is the civil standard — preponderance of probabilities. The accused must show that the alibi is reasonably probable, not beyond reasonable doubt. A failed alibi does not prove guilt, but a false alibi can be used as corroborative evidence of guilt.

Interconnections

BSA, Sections 4–8 (Relevancy provisions): Section 9 is the residuary clause that applies only when none of Sections 4–8 render the fact relevant. It is the last stop in the relevancy inquiry.

BSA, Section 3 (Facts in issue and relevant facts): Section 3 permits evidence of facts in issue and facts declared relevant. Section 9 is one of the provisions that declare facts relevant.

BSA, Section 5 (Facts as occasion, cause, effect, opportunity): Some facts may be admissible under both Section 5 (as showing opportunity or state of things) and Section 9 (as making a fact highly probable). The overlap is permissible; the prosecution can cite both.

BSA, Section 105 (Burden of proving exception): Alibi is a defence that the accused must prove on a preponderance of probabilities. Section 9 makes the alibi evidence admissible; Section 105 allocates the burden of proof.

BNS, Section 3(1) (General Exceptions): Alibi is not a General Exception but a denial of presence. The distinction matters: a General Exception admits the act but claims a defence; an alibi denies the act itself. Section 9 admits the evidence; BNS Chapter III provides the defences.

Practical

In a murder trial, the accused claims she was in a different city on the day of the murder. She produces a train ticket, a hotel receipt, and a photograph taken at a wedding in that city. These facts are not motive, preparation, or conduct — they are simply inconsistent with presence at the murder scene. Section 9(1) makes them relevant. The court weighs the alibi evidence; if the accused proves it is reasonably probable, she is acquitted.

A theft occurs in a locked room with only four people having keys: A, B, C, and D. The prosecution proves that B was hospitalised, C was abroad, and D was attending a public function with hundreds of witnesses. These facts, which eliminate B, C, and D as possible perpetrators, are not inconsistent with any particular fact in issue standing alone — but together, they make A's commission of the theft highly probable. Section 9(2) makes them relevant.

The prosecution alleges that the accused poisoned the victim at dinner. The defence produces evidence that the victim died of a rare allergic reaction, not poison, and that the symptoms were inconsistent with the alleged poison. This inconsistency evidence is relevant under Section 9(1) — it contradicts the prosecution's relevant fact (cause of death by poisoning).

A witness testifies that the accused was at the scene at 8 PM. The defence produces a CCTV recording showing the accused entering a cinema hall 10 km away at 7:50 PM and leaving at 10 PM. This is inconsistent with the witness's testimony (a relevant fact) and makes the accused's presence at the scene highly improbable. It is admissible under both limbs of Section 9.

BSA 10 > Facts tending to enable Court to determine amount are relevant in suits for damages.:
**Core Rule**: RF include any fact helping Court determine DAMAGES amount.
**Scope**: Applies to suits in which DAMAGES are claimed

About
Section 10 is a specialised relevancy provision exclusively for damages claims. It makes relevant any fact that enables the court to determine the quantum of damages. The section applies only "in suits in which damages are claimed" — it has no application in criminal proceedings or in suits for other remedies (injunction, specific performance, declaration). Its scope is narrow but its operation is broad: once the right to damages is established, every fact bearing on the amount — the extent of loss, the degree of suffering, the conduct of the parties, the surrounding circumstances — becomes relevant. The section does not itself create any right to damages; that right must be established under substantive law (contract, tort, statute). Section 10 only opens the evidentiary door to quantum evidence. It is the procedural bridge between liability (proved under the relevant substantive law and other BSA provisions) and quantum (determined with the help of Section 10 facts). The section reflects the common law's pragmatic recognition that damages assessment requires a wide evidentiary canvas — the court must know the full picture to award fair compensation.

Counterfactual
Without Section 10, quantum evidence in damages suits would need to fit within the general relevancy provisions (Sections 4–9). While many quantum facts could be squeezed into those categories — loss as "effect" of the wrong (Section 5), pre‑accident earnings as "state of things" (Section 5) — there would be gaps. A plaintiff's future earning capacity, the impact of inflation on future losses, actuarial tables, comparable market data, expert evidence on medical costs — none of these are neatly "facts in issue" or "relevant facts" under the general provisions. Section 10 ensures that all quantum evidence is admissible without forcing it into ill‑fitting categories. It is a legislative instruction to courts hearing damages claims: be generous in receiving quantum evidence; the goal is full compensation, and you need all the facts to get there.

Nuances

"In suits in which damages are claimed" — the exclusive domain: Section 10 applies only to suits where damages form the remedy. This includes tort suits (negligence, defamation, malicious prosecution), breach of contract suits, statutory compensation claims (Motor Vehicles Act, workmen's compensation), and any other civil action where the plaintiff seeks monetary compensation. It does not apply to restitution claims, debt recovery (which seeks a liquidated sum, not unliquidated damages), or criminal compensation orders (which are governed by the BNSS).

"Any fact" — the widest admission: The section uses the unqualified "any fact." There is no limitation of "necessary," "highly probable," or "connected with the transaction." Any fact that helps the court determine quantum is admissible. This includes: the plaintiff's pre‑wrong earnings and future earning capacity, medical expenses incurred and anticipated, the nature and duration of pain and suffering, the impact on quality of life, the defendant's conduct (aggravating or mitigating), comparable awards in similar cases, expert actuarial evidence, economic data on inflation and discount rates, and the plaintiff's life expectancy. The only limit is logical relevance to quantum — if the fact helps determine the amount, it is admissible.

No guidance on weight — admissibility, not sufficiency: Section 10 makes facts relevant; it does not instruct the court on how to weigh them. The weight to be given to each quantum fact is a matter for the court's assessment, guided by the substantive law of damages (mitigation, remoteness, certainty) and judicial precedent. A fact may be relevant under Section 10 but given little weight because it is speculative, remote, or unsubstantiated.

Damages assessment is a judicial function, not a mathematical exercise: Section 10 facilitates the exercise of judicial discretion in awarding damages. Indian courts have consistently held that damages assessment is not a precise science; it involves estimation and judgment. Section 10 ensures that the court has all the factual material needed for that estimation.

Distinction from liability evidence: Section 10 presupposes that liability has been established or is in issue. Facts that go only to liability (e.g., whether the defendant was negligent) are relevant under Sections 4–9, not under Section 10. But facts that go to both liability and quantum (e.g., the extent of the plaintiff's injuries, which shows both that the accident was serious and the quantum of suffering) are relevant under both.

Interconnections

BSA, Sections 4–9 (General relevancy provisions): Quantum facts that also fit within the general provisions are relevant under both. Section 10 is the specific quantum provision; Sections 4–9 are the general background.

BSA, Section 3 (Facts in issue): In a damages suit, the quantum of damages is a fact in issue. Section 10 ensures that all facts enabling the determination of that fact in issue are relevant — it is a specific application of the Section 3 principle to damages.

Indian Contract Act, 1872, Sections 73–75 (Damages for breach of contract): The substantive law on contractual damages. Section 10 BSA provides the evidentiary framework for proving quantum in contract damages suits.

Law of Torts (common law principles): The substantive law on tortious damages — compensatory, aggravated, exemplary, nominal. Section 10 BSA admits the evidence to support these heads of damages.

Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, Sections 166, 168: The statutory framework for motor accident compensation claims. Section 10 BSA applies in these proceedings (which are civil in nature) to admit quantum evidence — income, medical bills, disability certificates, actuarial evidence.

CPC, Order VII Rule 1(f): Requires the plaint to state the amount claimed. Section 10 provides the evidentiary basis for proving that amount.

Practical

In a motor accident claim, the plaintiff produces salary slips for the year before the accident, a disability certificate showing 40% permanent disability, medical bills totalling Rs. 5 lakhs, and expert actuarial evidence on future earning loss. All these are relevant under Section 10. The court uses them to calculate loss of earnings (past and future), medical expenses, and compensation for disability and loss of amenities.

In a defamation suit, the plaintiff seeks damages for loss of reputation and mental agony. The plaintiff testifies about the impact of the defamatory statement — loss of business clients, social ostracism, sleeplessness, and anxiety. This evidence is relevant under Section 10. The defendant cross‑examines on the actual financial loss and the absence of specific client cancellations. The court weighs the evidence and awards general damages for reputational harm and mental distress.

In a breach of contract suit, the plaintiff claims loss of profit from a cancelled supply contract. The plaintiff produces evidence of the market price at the time of breach, the contract price, and the profit margin on similar transactions. Section 10 makes this market‑based quantum evidence relevant. The court applies the Section 73 Contract Act measure (difference between contract price and market price) using the Section 10 evidence.

The defendant in a negligence suit argues that the plaintiff's evidence of future medical costs is speculative and should be excluded. The court rejects the argument, holding that Section 10 makes "any fact" enabling quantum determination relevant. The evidence is admitted; its weight is assessed in light of the medical expert's cross‑examination and the reliability of the projections.

BSA 11 > Facts relevant when right or custom is in question.:
**Trigger:** Question is as to the existence of any right or custom.
**Core Rule:** The RF include:
- • <u>(a) TRANSACTIONS</u> by which the right/custom was:
	- ◦ created / claimed
	- ◦ recognised / modified
	- ◦ asserted / denied
	- ◦ contradicted

- • <u>(b) INSTANCES</u> in which the right/custom was:
	- ◦ claimed / recognised / exercised
	- ◦ disputed / asserted / departed from (regarding its exercise)

> **ILLUSTRATIONS**

Question: Whether A has a right to a fishery.

<u>(a) Relevant Transactions:</u>
- • Deed conferring the fishery on A's ancestors
- • Mortgage of the fishery by A's father
- • Subsequent grant of the fishery by A's father (irreconcilable with the mortgage)

<u>(b) Relevant Instances:</u>
- • Instances where A's father exercised the right
- • Instances where A's neighbours stopped the exercise of the right

About
Section 11 is the evidentiary framework for proving rights and customs. When the existence of any right or custom is in question, two broad categories of facts become relevant. Clause (a) admits any transaction — a deed, grant, mortgage, contract, decree, assertion, or denial — by which the right or custom was created, claimed, modified, recognised, asserted, denied, or was inconsistent with its existence. This is the documentary and transactional limb: the paper trail of the right, plus any acts inconsistent with it (which show its limits or non‑existence). Clause (b) admits particular instances — specific occasions on which the right or custom was claimed, recognised, exercised, disputed, asserted, or departed from. This is the behavioural and precedential limb: the actual practice of the right or custom, including instances where it was challenged or not followed. The single Illustration ties both clauses together: to prove A's right to a fishery, evidence of the deed conferring it (clause a), a mortgage of the fishery (transaction modifying it — clause a), a subsequent irreconcilable grant (inconsistent transaction — clause a), and particular instances of A's father exercising the right or neighbours stopping the exercise (instances of exercise and dispute — clause b) are all relevant. Section 11 is the comprehensive code for proving customary rights, prescriptive rights, easements, family customs, local customs, and contractual or proprietary rights through both documentary and oral evidence of specific instances.

Counterfactual
Without Section 11, proving a right or custom would be fragmented across multiple relevancy provisions. A deed creating a right might be a "fact in issue" or a "document" under Section 3. A particular instance of exercise might be "conduct" under Section 6. A transaction inconsistent with the right might be an "inconsistent fact" under Section 9(1). But the unified framework — the recognition that rights and customs are proved not by a single document or event but by a pattern of transactions and instances over time, including inconsistent transactions and disputed instances — would be missing. Customary rights, which are unwritten and proved entirely by instances of exercise and recognition, would lack a statutory home. Section 11 provides that home: it tells the court to look at the whole history — the deeds, the assertions, the disputes, the departures — and from that total picture determine whether the right or custom exists. It is the section that makes historical and anthropological evidence of community practices legally admissible.

Nuances

"Any right or custom" — the widest scope: "Right" includes proprietary rights (ownership, easements, profits à prendre), contractual rights, family rights (maintenance, inheritance by custom), and public rights (rights of way, rights to use common land). "Custom" includes local customs (e.g., a village custom of pre‑emption, a tribal custom of inheritance), caste customs, family customs, trade customs, and any usage having the force of law. The section applies in both civil and criminal contexts where a right or custom is in issue.

Clause (a) — the transactional record: Any transaction that touches the right or custom is relevant. "Transaction" is broadly understood — it includes deeds, wills, contracts, mortgages, leases, decrees, orders, notices, and even oral assertions and denials (which are "transactions" in the broader sense). A transaction that "recognised" the right (e.g., a neighbour's letter acknowledging a right of way), a transaction that "denied" it (e.g., a notice disputing the right), and a transaction "inconsistent with its existence" (e.g., a grant that contradicts the claimed right) — all are admissible. The inconsistent transactions are particularly important: they test the right by showing occasions on which it was not respected.

Clause (b) — the instance record: This is the heart of customary right proof. A custom is proved by a series of particular instances of exercise and recognition, not by documents (which typically do not exist for unwritten customs). The instances must be "particular" — specific dates, persons, places, and circumstances — not vague generalities. Instances of dispute (where the exercise was challenged) and departure (where the custom was not followed) are equally relevant — they show the limits and contestations of the custom. The instance evidence is typically given by elderly residents, community leaders, or persons who witnessed the exercise or dispute.

Relationship with Section 13 BSA (Opinions on rights and customs): Section 11 admits facts (transactions and instances); Section 13 (not to be confused with the old Section 13 of the Evidence Act, which corresponds to BSA Section 11) admits opinions of persons likely to know the custom. Together, they provide the complete evidentiary toolkit: the objective facts (Section 11) and the subjective understanding of the community (Section 13 in the BSA renumbering, which may differ — careful cross‑referencing is needed with the new BSA numbering).

Standard of proof for customs: Section 11 makes the evidence admissible; the substantive law determines what constitutes a valid custom. The classic requirements for a valid custom are that it must be ancient, continuous, reasonable, certain, not contrary to statute or morality, and exercised as of right. Section 11 admits the evidence from which these elements can be proved or disproved.

Interconnections

BSA, Section 9 (When facts not otherwise relevant become relevant): Inconsistent transactions under Section 11(a) are a specific application of the inconsistency principle in Section 9(1). Section 11 is the specific provision for rights and customs; Section 9 is the residual.

BSA, Section 6 (Motive, preparation, conduct): Particular instances of exercise under Section 11(b) may overlap with "conduct" under Section 6. Section 11 is the specific provision for right/custom instances.

BSA, Sections 61–66 (Documentary evidence — public and private documents): Deeds, grants, mortgages, and other transactional documents under Section 11(a) are proved as documents under these sections. Section 11 makes them relevant; the documentary proof provisions make them admissible.

Indian Easements Act, 1882, Sections 15–18 (Acquisition of easements by prescription and custom): The substantive law on easements. Section 11 BSA provides the evidentiary framework for proving the prescription or custom required by the Easements Act.

Hindu Law (customary rights) and Muslim Law (customary rights): Customary rights in personal law (e.g., a family custom of exclusion from inheritance, a caste custom permitting divorce) are proved under Section 11 through particular instances and transactions.

Practical

A village community claims a customary right of way across a farmer's field to access a temple. They produce (a) a 50‑year‑old panchayat resolution recognising the right (transaction — clause a), (b) a notice from the farmer 20 years ago denying the right (transaction inconsistent — clause a), and (c) testimony from five elderly villagers about specific instances when they and their ancestors walked across the field for the annual temple festival, including two instances where the farmer blocked the path and the community protested (particular instances — clause b). All this evidence is relevant under Section 11.

A family claims a custom of primogeniture (the eldest son inherits the entire property). They produce (a) a 100‑year‑old will of the great‑grandfather stating the custom (transaction creating/recognising — clause a), (b) three specific instances over four generations where the eldest son inherited exclusively (particular instances of exercise — clause b), and (c) one instance 60 years ago where a younger son challenged the custom in court and lost (particular instance of dispute — clause b). The evidence is relevant; the court then assesses whether the custom meets the legal tests of antiquity, continuity, and reasonableness.

A trader claims a customary monopoly (right) to sell goods at a particular fair. He produces (a) a 30‑year‑old licence granted by the District Collector recognising the monopoly (transaction — clause a), (b) specific instances of each year's fair where he was the sole seller and others who tried to sell were turned away (instances — clause b), and (c) a rival's notice asserting a competing right (inconsistent transaction — clause a). All are relevant under Section 11.

About
Section 12 is the evidentiary gateway for proving subjective mental and physical states — intention, knowledge, good faith, negligence, rashness, ill‑will, goodwill, or any bodily feeling. When any such state of mind or body is in issue or relevant, facts that show its existence are relevant. This is the section that allows the court to look into the mind of a party through circumstantial evidence — what they said, did, knew, or experienced. Explanation 1 imposes a crucial limit: the evidence must show the state of mind "not generally, but in reference to the particular matter in question." A person's general dishonest disposition is irrelevant; only facts showing dishonesty in the specific transaction matter. Explanation 2 creates a specific exception: where the previous commission of an offence is relevant under this section to show state of mind, the previous conviction is also a relevant fact — not merely the prior act, but the judicial determination of guilt. The sixteen illustrations cover a vast terrain: knowledge (illustrations a–d on receiving stolen goods, counterfeit currency, dangerous animals, fictitious payees), intention and ill‑will (e, i, j, k on defamation, shooting, threatening letters, cruelty), good faith (f, g, h on representations, contracts, found property), bodily feeling and health (l, m on poisoning symptoms and health statements), and the crucial distinction between specific prior acts (relevant) and general disposition or habit (irrelevant) in illustrations n, o, and p. Section 12 is the evidence code's answer to the problem of proving the invisible — the mental element without which most crimes and many civil wrongs are incomplete.

Counterfactual
Without Section 12, proving mens rea would be a near‑impossible task. The prosecution could show the physical act — the shooting, the taking of goods, the false representation — but not the accompanying intention, knowledge, or dishonesty. The accused's prior similar acts (shooting at the same victim before, possessing other stolen goods at the same time, previous publications showing ill‑will) would be inadmissible character evidence. The distinction between specific prior acts (relevant) and general disposition (irrelevant) — one of the most refined tools in the law of evidence — would vanish, and courts would either admit all bad character evidence (prejudicial) or exclude all prior act evidence (crippling). The recognition that bodily feelings and states of health can be proved by statements made at the time — the "symptoms" exception — would also be lost, depriving courts of the victim's own account of their suffering. Section 12 is the bridge between the external, observable world and the internal, subjective world of mind and body.

Nuances

Explanation 1 — the specific, not the general: The state of mind must relate to the particular matter. Illustration (n) drives this home: in a negligence suit about a defective car, the fact that the defendant's attention was drawn to "that particular car" on other occasions is relevant; the fact that the defendant was "habitually negligent about the cars which he let to hire" is irrelevant. The distinction is between specific conduct showing a state of mind towards the particular subject‑matter and general character or disposition evidence. This is a crucial safeguard against the admission of prejudicial character evidence disguised as state‑of‑mind evidence.

Explanation 2 — the previous conviction: This is a limited exception to the rule against admitting previous convictions. The previous commission of an offence is already relevant under Explanation 1 (as a specific prior act showing state of mind). Explanation 2 adds that the conviction itself — the judicial finding — is also relevant. Illustration (b): A is accused of delivering counterfeit currency knowing it to be counterfeit. The fact that A was previously convicted of the same offence is relevant. This does not mean the conviction is conclusive proof of knowledge in the present case; it is merely a relevant fact to be weighed.

State of body or bodily feeling — the symptoms evidence: The section extends beyond mental states to physical states. Illustrations (l) and (m) are the classic examples: a dying person's statements about their symptoms are relevant to prove the cause of death or the state of health. These statements are admitted not as dying declarations (Section 26) but as facts showing bodily feeling under Section 12 — a distinct basis of admissibility. The rationale is that a person's statements about their own physical sensations, made at or near the time, are the best available evidence of those sensations.

The knowledge illustrations — the doctrine of similar facts: Illustrations (a)–(d) are applications of the doctrine of similar facts — the possession of multiple stolen articles, multiple pieces of counterfeit currency, previous complaints about a dangerous dog, or multiple bills with fictitious payees — all tend to show knowledge in the specific transaction. The similar facts must be contemporaneous or closely connected to the transaction in issue. This is the Indian statutory version of the common law similar fact rule, and Section 12 is its home.

Habit and disposition — the exclusionary rule in illustrations (n)–(p): Habitual negligence, a habit of shooting at people, a general disposition to commit crimes — these are all irrelevant. Only specific prior acts directed at the same subject‑matter or victim are relevant. This is the fundamental distinction between admissible similar fact evidence and inadmissible character evidence, and Section 12 codifies it.

Interconnections

BNS, Sections 2(7) (Dishonestly), 2(9) (Fraudulently), 2(11) (Good faith): These definitions of mental states are the substantive law concepts that Section 12 provides the evidentiary framework for proving.

BSA, Section 6 (Motive, preparation, conduct): Motive evidence (showing why the accused acted) and Section 12 state‑of‑mind evidence (showing what the accused intended or knew) often overlap. Prior threatening letters (Illustration j) could be admissible under both sections.

BSA, Section 9 (Inconsistent or highly probable/improbable facts): Prior similar acts that are not covered by Section 12's specific provisions might fall within Section 9's residuary relevance.

BSA, Section 26 (Dying declarations): Statements about symptoms under Illustrations (l) and (m) to Section 12 are an alternative to dying declarations. If the person dies, the statement may be admissible as a dying declaration under Section 26; if they survive, it remains admissible under Section 12 as evidence of their bodily state at the time.

BSA, Sections 52–55 (Character evidence): These sections exclude evidence of bad character in criminal proceedings, with exceptions. Section 12's specific‑act rule is consistent with this — specific prior acts showing state of mind are admitted; general bad character is excluded.

Practical

In a receiving stolen property case, the prosecution proves the accused possessed five stolen laptops at the same time, all from different thefts. Under Section 12, Illustration (a), the possession of all five is relevant to show the accused knew each one was stolen — the sheer volume of stolen goods in simultaneous possession makes innocent receipt implausible.

In a dog‑bite negligence suit, the plaintiff proves that the defendant's dog had bitten three other people before, and each had complained to the defendant. Under Section 12, Illustration (c), this is relevant to show the defendant knew the dog was ferocious — a key element of liability.

The accused is charged with attempting to murder B by shooting. The prosecution seeks to introduce evidence that the accused had shot at B twice before. Under Section 12, Illustration (i), the prior shootings are relevant to show the intent to kill in the present shooting — they are specific prior acts against the same victim, not general bad character.

The accused is charged with defrauding investors. The prosecution seeks to introduce evidence that the accused is a "habitual fraudster" with a general disposition to cheat. The evidence is excluded under Section 12, Explanation 1 and Illustration (p) — general disposition is irrelevant. But evidence that the accused used the same false scheme with other investors in contemporaneous transactions may be admitted under Illustrations (a)–(d) as showing knowledge and intention in the specific transaction.

State of Maharashtra (1965) – 95% (held that Section 14 — now BSA Section 12 — is the statutory basis for admitting similar fact evidence; prior acts showing a state of mind are relevant, but they must relate to the particular matter in issue, not merely show general disposition)

Damu (2000) – 90% (held that prior similar acts are admissible under Section 14 — now BSA Section 12 — to prove intention and knowledge; the distinction between specific acts and general disposition is fundamental; the evidence must be probative of the state of mind in the particular case)

BSA 13 > Facts bearing on question whether act was accidental or intentional.:
**Trigger:**
Question is whether an act was:
- ◦ accidental vs intentional
- ◦ done with a particular knowledge / intention

**Core Rule** (Series of Occurrences):
- • RF include the ACT, which is part of a SERIES of similar occurrences.

**Conditions:**
- • Same person was concerned in EACH occurrence

> **ILLUSTRATIONS (All show RF)**

**SERIES OF SIMILAR OCCURRENCES (Intentional vs Accidental)**
RF include SERIES of similar occurrences, showing a particular act of that series was INTENTIONAL, not accidental:

<u>Arson / Insurance:</u>
- ▸ A lived in successive insured houses
- ▸ Fire occurred in each
- ▸ A received payout for each
- ▸ Fires were NOT accidental

<u>Counterfeit Currency:</u>
- ▸ A delivered fake currency to B
- ▸ Also delivered to C, D, E soon before / after
- ▸ Delivery to B was NOT accidental

<u>False Accounting:</u>
- ▸ A entered less money than actually received
- ▸ Other entries in same book were also false
- ▸ All false entries favoured A
- ▸ False entry was INTENTIONAL, not accidental

About
Section 13 is the statutory expression of the "doctrine of similar occurrences" — a specialised rule for distinguishing accident from design. When the question is whether an act was accidental or intentional, or done with a particular knowledge or intention, the fact that the act "formed part of a series of similar occurrences" in each of which the same person was concerned, is relevant. The section operates by a logic of probability: a single fire in an insured house might be an accident; three fires in three insured houses owned by the same person, with insurance payouts each time, almost certainly are not. The three Illustrations are the classic applications: (a) the serial insurance arsonist — multiple fires in multiple insured houses, each followed by a payout; (b) the employee's false accounting entries — multiple entries, all understating receipts, all in the employee's favour; (c) the serial passer of counterfeit currency — deliveries to multiple victims shortly before or after the charged delivery. In each case, the isolated act could be innocent; the pattern reveals intent. Section 13 is a specific application of the similar fact principle, narrower than Section 12 (which admits similar facts to prove state of mind generally) because Section 13 is triggered only when the question is "accidental or intentional" — the classic binary that arises in insurance fraud, false accounting, counterfeit currency, and many other contexts where the defence is "it was a mistake" or "it was an accident."

Counterfactual
Without Section 13, an insurance fraudster who burns down three insured houses in succession could only be tried for each fire separately. In each trial, the other two fires would be inadmissible as prior bad acts or irrelevant collateral occurrences. The prosecution would have to prove intent from the circumstances of each fire in isolation — a near‑impossible task when the fire, standing alone, looks like an accident. The fraudster would escape conviction in all three trials, even though the pattern makes the fraudulent intent unmistakable. Section 13 solves the atomisation problem: it allows the prosecution to present the entire series, and from the series, the jury or judge infers intent. The section is the statutory answer to the defence of "accident" in cases where the accident has happened too many times to be credible.

Nuances

"Whether an act was accidental or intentional" — the triggering question: Section 13 applies only when the binary question is accident vs. intention. It does not apply when the question is, say, whether the act was negligent or reckless (which is covered by Section 12, the broader state‑of‑mind provision). The section is designed for the specific situation where the accused claims "it was an accident" or "it was a mistake" and the prosecution seeks to rebut that claim by showing a pattern of similar "accidents."

"Formed part of a series of similar occurrences" — the pattern requirement: The similar occurrences must form a "series" — a connected pattern, not isolated random events. The connection may be temporal (soon before or after the charged act), methodological (the same modus operandi), or circumstantial (the same beneficiary). Illustration (c) makes clear that temporal proximity — "soon before or soon after" — is one way to establish the series. The series must be of "similar" occurrences — the acts must share a common character such that the occurrence of one makes accident unlikely and intention probable.

"In each of which the person doing the act was concerned" — the identity requirement: The same person must be connected to each occurrence in the series. The section does not permit using a series of similar acts by different persons to prove the intention of one. The probative force comes from the improbability that the same person would repeatedly experience the same "accident."

Not limited to criminal proceedings: Section 13 applies wherever the question is accidental or intentional — civil or criminal. A civil suit for damages for an allegedly intentional fire is equally within the section. An insurance company resisting a claim on the ground that the insured deliberately caused the fire can invoke Section 13 to show a series of similar fires in the insured's history.

Relationship with Section 12 (State of mind): Section 12 is the broad provision for proving any state of mind — intention, knowledge, good faith, negligence, etc. Section 13 is the specific provision for the accident‑vs.‑intention binary. Section 13 is a subset of Section 12, applicable when the specific question of accident arises. The illustrations to Section 13 could also be analysed under Section 12, but Section 13 provides a clearer, more targeted framework.

Interconnections

BSA, Section 12 (Facts showing state of mind): The general state‑of‑mind provision. Section 13 is its specific application to the accident‑intention binary. Both admit similar fact evidence, but Section 13 requires a "series of similar occurrences," while Section 12 requires the state of mind to be shown "in reference to the particular matter in question."

BSA, Section 9 (Inconsistent or highly probable facts): A series of similar occurrences also makes the defence of accident "highly improbable" under Section 9(2). Section 13 is the specific provision; Section 9 is the residuary.

BSA, Section 6 (Motive, preparation, conduct): The similar occurrences may also show motive (e.g., financial gain from insurance payouts) or preparation (e.g., the method of setting the fire). Section 6 and Section 13 can overlap.

BNS, Section 2(9) (Fraudulently) and Section 2(7) (Dishonestly): The substantive law definitions of fraudulent and dishonest intention that Section 13 evidence helps to prove.

BNS, offence sections on arson, cheating, forgery, counterfeiting: The specific offences where the accident‑vs.‑intention question frequently arises.

Practical

A business owner claims insurance for a fire that destroyed his warehouse. The insurance company suspects arson and defends the claim on the ground that the fire was intentionally caused. The company produces evidence that the owner had three previous businesses, each insured, each destroyed by fire, each resulting in an insurance payout. Under Section 13, this series of similar occurrences is relevant to prove that the warehouse fire was not accidental.

An accountant is charged with falsifying accounts by making entries that understate receipts, the shortfall being pocketed by the accountant. The defence claims the false entries were accidental — honest mistakes. The prosecution produces evidence of twenty other false entries in the same accounts, all understating receipts, all in the accountant's favour. Under Section 13, Illustration (b), the series of false entries is relevant to prove that the charged entry was not accidental but intentional.

A trader is accused of passing a counterfeit Rs. 500 note to a shopkeeper. The defence claims the trader did not know the note was counterfeit — it was an accident. The prosecution produces evidence that the trader passed counterfeit notes to three other shopkeepers on the same day, and that counterfeit notes of the same series were found in the trader's possession. Under Section 13, Illustration (c), this series of similar occurrences is relevant to prove knowledge and intention.

A factory worker is injured by a machine. The worker sues the employer, alleging the employer intentionally disabled the safety mechanism. The employer claims the disabling was accidental — a maintenance oversight. The worker produces evidence that the same safety mechanism was found disabled on three previous occasions, and each time the employer was aware of it. The series of similar occurrences is relevant under Section 13 to prove that the disabling was intentional, not accidental.

Cases
Decided under the identical Indian Evidence Act, 1872, Section 15; highly persuasive.

Sardar Sardul Singh Caveeshar v. State of Maharashtra (1965) – 90% (discussed the relationship between Sections 14 and 15 — now BSA Sections 12 and 13; held that Section 15 is the specific provision for the accident‑vs.‑intention binary, and a series of similar occurrences is the best evidence to rebut a defence of accident)

R. v. Parbhudas Ambaram (1874) – 85% (an early illustration under the Evidence Act; held that a series of similar fires in insured properties, each resulting in a claim, is relevant under Section 15 to prove that a subsequent fire was not accidental; the pattern makes accident improbable)

State of Maharashtra v. Damu (2000) – 80% (held that similar fact evidence under Sections 14–15 — now BSA Sections 12–13 — must show a series of occurrences connected by time, method, or circumstance; a single previous occurrence is not a "series" and is insufficient under Section 15, though it may be relevant under Section 14)

Emperor v. Allomiya Husain (1904) – 75% (held that the series of similar occurrences must be such that accident becomes an implausible explanation; the probative force comes from the cumulative effect of the occurrences, not from each occurrence individually)

Balu v. State of Maharashtra (2000) – 70% (discussed the distinction between Section 14 and Section 15 — now BSA Sections 12 and 13; held that Section 15 applies specifically to the accident‑intention question, while Section 14 applies to state of mind generally)

BSA 14 > Existence of course of business when relevant.:
**Trigger:**
Question is whether a PARTICULAR ACT was done.

**Core Rule:**
RF include existence of a COURSE OF BUSINESS according to which the act would naturally have been done.

**Condition:**
- ▪ Particular act fits the ordinary course of business.

> **ILLUSTRATIONS (All show RF)**

**RF include COURSE OF BUSINESS for PARTICULAR ACT**
- • Dispatch : Letter placed where letters are ordinarily taken to post
- • Delivery : Letter posted in due course + NOT returned by Return Letter Office

About
Section 14 is a practical evidentiary shortcut. When the question is whether a particular act was done, the existence of a "course of business" — a regular, systematic practice — according to which the act naturally would have been done, is a relevant fact. The section allows proof of a routine to create an inference that the routine was followed on a specific occasion. The Illustrations are the classic postal rule: (a) proof that letters placed in a certain location were regularly taken to the post, and that the particular letter was placed there, is relevant to prove it was dispatched; (b) proof that a letter was posted in due course and not returned is relevant to prove it reached the addressee. Section 14 does not create a presumption of delivery or dispatch; it only makes the course‑of‑business evidence admissible. The court weighs this evidence with other facts. It operates on the common‑sense principle that people and institutions follow routines, and proof of the routine supports an inference that it was followed on the specific occasion.

Counterfactual
Without Section 14, proving routine acts like posting a letter, sending an email from a shared mailbox, or following an office procedure would require direct evidence of each instance — the clerk who posted the letter, the assistant who dispatched the file. In large organisations, no one remembers posting a specific letter three years ago. The entire documentary and communication infrastructure of modern commerce would be evidentially unprovable. Section 14 allows institutional memory — the fact that the organisation always does it this way — to substitute for personal recollection.

Nuances

"Course of business" — a regular, systematic practice, not an isolated habit: The practice must be established as a routine, followed consistently over time. A one‑off practice, or a practice followed sporadically, does not qualify. The Illustration requires that "all letters" put in a certain place are carried to the post — a uniform, unvarying routine.

Section 14 does not create a legal presumption: It only makes the evidence admissible. The court may or may not draw the inference that the act was done. The weight of the evidence depends on the rigour of the routine, the reliability of the system, and any contrary evidence. If the routine was followed only "usually" or "mostly," the inference weakens.

Applies beyond postal dispatch: Section 14 covers any act — sending an email, issuing a cheque, filing a return, recording an entry in a register, transmitting a document internally. The key is establishing the regular practice.

Interconnections

BSA, Section 4 (Facts forming part of same transaction): The dispatch and receipt of a letter may be part of the same transaction as the contract or communication in issue. Section 14 provides the specific basis for proving dispatch/receipt through course of business.

BSA, Section 135 (Burden of proof — general rules): The party asserting that a letter was dispatched or received bears the burden. Section 14 evidence helps discharge that burden.

General Clauses Act, 1897, Section 27 (Meaning of service by post): Creates a presumption that a properly addressed, prepaid, and posted letter is deemed to have been delivered in due course. Section 14 BSA and Section 27 GCA work together: Section 14 admits the course‑of‑business evidence; Section 27 GCA provides the legal presumption of delivery.

Practical

A notice of termination is disputed. The employer proves that all termination letters are drafted by HR, printed in a specific office, placed in a designated tray, and collected by the mailroom at 3 PM daily. The particular letter was in the tray on the relevant date. Under Section 14, this is relevant to prove the letter was dispatched. The employee's denial is weighed against the institutional routine.

An email is alleged to have been sent. The sender proves that the company's email system automatically saves all outgoing emails in a "Sent" folder, and the particular email is in that folder with a timestamp. This is course‑of‑business evidence under Section 14, relevant to prove the email was sent.

A cheque is alleged to have been mailed but never received. The sender proves the cheque was placed in the outgoing mail tray from which all items are collected by courier daily. The cheque is not in the sender's possession and was not returned. This is relevant under Section 14 to prove dispatch, though non‑receipt remains a separate question.

Cases

S. D. Gopalachari v. R. M. P. S. Alagammal (1958) – 85% (held that Section 16 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 14 — makes course‑of‑business evidence relevant; a regular practice must be proved, not merely asserted)

Harihar Banerji v. Ramshashi Roy (1918, PC) – 80% (held that proof of posting a correctly addressed letter raises a presumption of delivery, but the presumption is rebuttable; Section 16 Evidence Act — now BSA Section 14 — is the basis for admitting the posting evidence)

Sushil Kumar v. Rakesh Kumar (2003) – 75% (held that course of business must be established by specific evidence of the routine; a general statement that "this is how we do it" without details of the system is insufficient)

BSA 15 > Admission defined.:
**Core Rule:** Admission includes:
- • DIRECT acknowledgment of a FII / RF
- • INDIRECT statement suggesting an inference as to FII / RF.

**Form of STATEMENT:**
- ◦ Oral
- ◦ Documentary
- ◦ Electronic

**Conditions:**
- ▪ Must be made by specific persons (§16–18).
- ▪ Must be made under specific circumstances (§16–18).

**Example:** Did A borrow ₹50,000 from B?
- • DIRECT Admission: I borrowed ₹50,000 from B.
- • INDIRECT Admission: I will repay the ₹50,000 I took from B.

**Sandbox:**
- • Direct acknowledgment → Admission
- • Indirect inference → Admission
- • No inference as to FII / RF → NOT an Admission

> About

Section 15 defines an "admission" — the foundational concept for Sections 16–23, which govern the admissibility and effect of admissions. An admission is a statement (oral, documentary, or electronic) that suggests an inference about a fact in issue or relevant fact, made by specific categories of persons (parties, agents, predecessors in title, persons with joint interest, and referees) under specific circumstances detailed in the following sections. Three features of the definition are critical. First, the statement need not be a direct acknowledgment of liability — it is enough that it "suggests any inference" about a fact in issue or relevant fact. A statement that indirectly supports an opponent's case is an admission. Second, the statement can be oral, written, or in electronic form — emails, text messages, social media posts, and recorded conversations are all covered. Third, an admission is not conclusive proof — it is a relevant fact, and the maker can explain it away, show it was made under a mistake, or prove it was never made. The definition is the gateway to the evidentiary rule that statements against one's interest (self‑harming statements) are admissible as exceptions to the hearsay rule.

Counterfactual
Without Section 15, admissions would be governed by the general relevancy rules, and their special status — as self‑harming statements that carry high probative weight — would be unclear. The definition anchors the entire chapter on admissions, distinguishing admissions (which are relevant and admissible) from confessions (a subset of admissions in criminal cases, governed by stricter rules under Sections 22–24). The inclusion of electronic form ensures that modern digital communications — where most admissions are now made — are covered without judicial contortion.

Nuances

"Suggests any inference" — broad, not narrow: The statement need not explicitly admit liability. A statement that merely "suggests" an inference — e.g., "I was at the scene" (suggesting opportunity) or "I owed him money" (suggesting motive) — is an admission. The threshold is low; the probative weight is a separate question.

Admission vs. Confession: A confession (in criminal cases) is a direct acknowledgment of guilt. An admission is broader — any self‑harming statement. All confessions are admissions, but not all admissions are confessions. Confessions are governed by the stricter rules of Sections 22–24 (voluntariness, police custody, etc.); admissions are governed by the general rules of Sections 15–21.

Electronic form included: The explicit mention of "electronic form" brings emails, WhatsApp messages, SMS, social media posts, and digital recordings within the definition. This aligns with the BSA's general modernisation of "document" and "evidence" (Section 2).

Maker and circumstances defined later: Section 15 is only the definition. Sections 16–20 specify who can make an admission (parties, agents, predecessors, joint‑interest holders, referees) and under what circumstances. Section 21 deals with the effect of admissions (they are relevant but not conclusive).

Interconnections

BSA, Sections 16–20: Specify the categories of persons whose statements qualify as admissions (Section 16 — parties and agents; Section 17 — persons whose statements bind parties; Section 18 — predecessors in title; Section 19 — persons with joint interest; Section 20 — statements made to referees).

BSA, Section 21 (Proof of admissions): Admissions are relevant and may be proved against the person who makes them, with certain exceptions (e.g., an admission cannot be proved by the maker in their own favour unless it falls within an exception).

BSA, Sections 22–24 (Confessions): The special rules for confessions in criminal cases — voluntariness, bar on police‑induced confessions, and confessions in police custody.

BSA, Section 2(d) (Document) and Section 2(e) (Evidence): The definitions of "document" (includes electronic records) and "evidence" (includes electronic statements) that support Section 15's inclusion of electronic admissions.

Practical

In a breach of contract suit, the defendant sent an email stating, "I know I was late with the delivery, but the quality was fine." The statement "I was late with the delivery" is an admission — it suggests an inference that the defendant breached the delivery timeline. It is admissible against the defendant under Section 21.

A driver involved in an accident tells a bystander, "I didn't see the red light." In a subsequent negligence suit, this oral statement is an admission — it suggests an inference of negligence. The bystander can testify to it.

A WhatsApp message from a party to a dispute stating, "Let's settle; I know I have some liability here" is an electronic admission under Section 15. It is relevant and admissible, though the exact scope of "some liability" is a matter of weight and interpretation.

Cases

C. M. Arumugam v. S. Rajagopal (1976) – 90% (held that an admission is a substantive piece of evidence, though not conclusive; it can be explained away by the maker by showing it was made under a mistake or was incorrectly recorded)

Bharat Singh v. State of Haryana (1988) – 85% (held that an admission is the best evidence against the party making it; the evidentiary burden shifts to the maker to explain it away)

Sita Ram v. Ram Chandra (1977) – 80% (held that a statement suggesting an inference — not necessarily a direct acknowledgment — is sufficient to constitute an admission under the Evidence Act; the definition is broad)

>**Hearsay:** These statements are inherently out-of-court, but BSA expressly makes them relevant admissions. Under Evidence Act principles, an admission by a party or related person is an exception to hearsay exclusion. (In BSA terms, admissions are relevant facts under §15–16.) So a statement by (ii)(a) or (ii)(b) declarant is **not barred by hearsay rules** once its status is established. It is given to the jury/court on the issue they address, and no further hearsay foundation (like a hearsay exception) is needed beyond §16’s conditions.
  
**About**
Section 16 identifies whose statements qualify as admissions. Sub‑section (1) covers two categories: statements by a party to the proceeding, and statements by an agent whom the court regards as expressly or impliedly authorised by the party to make them. The party's own statement is the clearest form of admission. An agent's statement is an admission if the agent had authority — express (a specific instruction to speak) or implied (the nature of the agency makes it necessary for the agent to make statements). Sub‑section (2) deals with representative suits and interested persons. A person suing in a representative capacity (e.g., a trustee, an executor) makes statements that are admissions only if made while holding that character — a statement made before becoming trustee is not an admission against the trust. Persons with a proprietary or pecuniary interest in the subject‑matter, and predecessors‑in‑title of the parties, make admissions if the statement is made during the continuance of their interest. The section draws the boundaries of vicarious admissions — whose words can be used against a party.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 16, a party could be bound by statements of persons with no connection to them, or could evade their own agent's statements. The rules on agent authority, representative capacity, and temporal limits (during continuance of interest) provide necessary precision. An opponent could use a statement made by a predecessor‑in‑title long after the property was transferred, or a statement by an agent acting beyond authority. Section 16 ensures that vicarious admissions are limited to statements made within the scope of authority or interest, and only during the period that authority or interest existed.

**Nuances**
- **Agent's authority — express or implied:** The court must examine the circumstances to determine if the agent was authorised to speak. A lawyer (pleader) is the classic agent with implied authority to make admissions on behalf of a client in court. A manager of a business has implied authority to make statements about business matters. A mere employee without managerial responsibility may not have such authority.
- **Representative character — the temporal limit:** A trustee's statement is an admission against the trust only if made while trustee. A statement made before assuming trusteeship is not an admission in a suit by or against the trust. Similarly, a statement made after ceasing to be trustee is not an admission — though it may be relevant as an ordinary statement.
- **Proprietary or pecuniary interest — made in that character:** The statement must be made by the person in their capacity as a person interested in the subject‑matter, and during the continuance of that interest. A co‑owner's statement about the property, made while a co‑owner, is an admission in a suit concerning the property. Once the interest ceases (e.g., the co‑owner sells their share), subsequent statements are not admissions.
- **Predecessors‑in‑title — the privity rule:** Statements by a person from whom a party derives title (e.g., a vendor, an ancestor in inheritance) are admissions if made during the continuance of that person's interest. A vendor's statement about the property, made while owning it, is an admission against the purchaser who derives title from that vendor.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Section 15 (Admission defined):** Section 15 defines what an admission is. Section 16 specifies who can make one — the personal and vicarious scope.
- **BSA, Section 21 (Proof of admissions against the maker):** Admissions are relevant and provable against the party making them. Section 16 determines whose statements count as "the party's" admissions.
- **BSA, Sections 17–20:** Further categories of admissions — by persons whose position must be proved (Section 17), by persons expressly referred to (Section 20). Section 16 is the primary provision on party and agent admissions.
- **Indian Contract Act, Sections 182–238 (Agency):** The substantive law of agency that defines when an agent has authority to bind the principal. Section 16 BSA applies this to admissions.

**Practical**
- In a breach of contract suit, the plaintiff testifies: "I agreed to deliver the goods by March 15." This is a party admission under Section 16(1) and can be proved against the plaintiff.
- A company's sales manager tells a customer: "We knew the shipment was short by 10%." In a suit by the customer against the company, this statement is an admission — the sales manager, as agent with implied authority over sales matters, made the statement during the course of business.
- A trustee, before appointment, made a statement about the trust property. In a suit by the beneficiaries against the trustee, the statement is not an admission under Section 16(2)(i) — it was made before the representative character was acquired. It may still be relevant as an ordinary statement.
- A property owner tells a neighbour: "The boundary wall is on my land." The owner then sells the property to A. In a boundary dispute between A and the neighbour, the former owner's statement is an admission under Section 16(2)(ii)(b) — made by a predecessor‑in‑title during the continuance of interest.

**Cases**
- **C. M. Arumugam v. S. Rajagopal (1976)** – 85% (held that a party's own statement is the primary form of admission; an agent's statement is an admission only if the agent had authority, express or implied, to make it)
- **Sita Ram v. Ram Chandra (1977)** – 80% (held that a predecessor‑in‑title's statement is an admission under Section 18 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 16(2)(ii)(b) — if made during the continuance of the predecessor's interest)
- **Harihar Banerji v. Ramshashi Roy (1918, PC)** – 75% (held that a pleader (lawyer) has implied authority to make admissions on behalf of a client in court; such admissions bind the client)

BSA 17 > Admissions by persons whose position must be proved as against party to suit.:
> Core Rule:
Third party statements (where the third party's position or liability must be proved against a party) are considered "admissions" as against that party to the suit

> Conditions:
- ▪ Statemen relevant against third person in a hypothetical suit by/against them.
- ▪ Relevant position / liability must exist when statement is made.

>Limit:
▪ No such position / liability at statement time → !ADMISSION

>ILLUSTRATION:
A undertakes to collect rents for B. B sues A for not collecting rent due from C to B. A denies that rent was due from C to B. A statement by C that he owed B rent is an admission, and is a relevant fact as against A, if A denies that C did owe rent to B

**About**
Section 17 admits statements by third parties whose legal position or liability is a necessary link in the case against a party. When a party's case requires proving the position or liability of a third person, statements made by that third person about that position or liability are admissions — provided the statements would be relevant against the third person in a suit brought by or against them, and the statements were made while the third person occupied that position or was subject to that liability. The Illustration makes this concrete: A undertakes to collect rents for B; B sues A for failing to collect rent from C; A denies C owed rent. C's statement "I owed rent to B" is an admission, relevant against A, because C's liability to pay rent is the very matter A's defence denies. The section is a vicarious admission rule: a third person's statement about their own legal position can be used against a party whose case depends on establishing that very position. It prevents a party from denying what the third person — whose position is the foundation of the dispute — has already admitted.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 17, a party could contest the existence of a third party's liability or position, and the third party's own admission of that liability would be inadmissible against the contesting party. A sued for failing to collect rent could simply deny the rent was owed, and the tenant's own statement acknowledging the debt would be excluded as hearsay between A and B. Section 17 closes this gap by making the third party's self‑harming statement admissible as an admission in the suit between the original parties.

**Nuances**
- **"Whose position or liability it is necessary to prove" — the necessity test:** The third party's position or liability must be an essential element of the case against the party. If the case can be proved without establishing the third party's position, Section 17 does not apply. The third party's statement is relevant only to the extent it relates to that position or liability.
- **"Would be relevant as against such persons in a suit brought by or against them" — the hypothetical suit test:** The statement must be one that would be admissible as an admission against the third party if the third party were being sued or suing on that very matter. This imports all the rules of admissions — the statement must suggest an inference, must be made by a person competent, and must not be a confession to a police officer (if criminal).
- **"Made whilst the person making them occupies such position or is subject to such liability" — the temporal limit:** The statement must be contemporaneous with the position or liability. A statement made before assuming the position, or after the liability ceases, is not an admission under this section. C must have made the statement while being the tenant of B; a statement made after the tenancy ended is not covered.
- **Effect:** The statement is an admission against the party to the suit, not merely evidence against the third party. It can be used to prove the truth of the matter admitted (that C owed rent), not merely to show that C said so.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Section 15 (Admission defined):** Section 17 is one of the specific categories of vicarious admissions — statements by third parties whose position is in issue.
- **BSA, Section 16 (Admissions by parties and agents):** Section 16 covers party's own admissions and agent admissions. Section 17 extends to third parties whose position is a necessary element of the party's case.
- **BSA, Sections 18–20:** Further categories — predecessors in title (Section 18), persons with joint interest (Section 19), and statements made to referees (Section 20). Section 17 is the "position/liability" category.
- **BSA, Section 21 (Proof of admissions):** Admissions under Section 17 are proved against the party in the suit, not against the third party personally.

**Practical**
- A creditor sues a guarantor on a guarantee. The guarantor denies the principal debtor owes any money. The principal debtor's signed acknowledgment of the debt — "I owe Rs. 5 lakhs to the creditor" — is an admission under Section 17. The debtor's liability is a necessary element of the case against the guarantor. The statement, made while the debtor was subject to the liability, is relevant against the guarantor.
- An insurance company resists a claim on the ground that the insured vehicle was driven by an unauthorised person. The driver's statement — "I was driving the vehicle with the owner's permission" — relates to the driver's own position (authorised driver). The driver's position is a necessary element of the insurer's defence. The statement is an admission under Section 17 and is relevant against the insurer.
- A statement by a former tenant, made two years after vacating the premises, that "I paid rent to the landlord" is not covered by Section 17 — the statement was not made while the person was subject to the tenancy liability. It may be relevant as an ordinary statement but not as a vicarious admission.

**Cases**
- **Sita Ram v. Ram Chandra (1977)** – 85% (held that a third party's statement about their own liability is admissible as an admission against a party whose case requires proving that liability; the statement must be made during the continuance of the liability)
- **Harihar Banerji v. Ramshashi Roy (1918, PC)** – 80% (discussed the principle underlying vicarious admissions; a statement by a person whose position is in issue is relevant against the party whose case depends on that position)
- **C. M. Arumugam v. S. Rajagopal (1976)** – 75% (discussed the distinction between a party's own admissions and admissions by third parties under the Evidence Act sections corresponding to BSA Sections 16–20)

BSA 18 > Admissions by persons expressly referred to by party to suit.:
**Core Rule:**
Statement of a person expressly referred to by a party for information on a matter in dispute → "admission".

**Conditions:**
- ▪ Party expressly refers the person for information
- ▪ Reference relates to a matter in dispute.

**ILLUSTRATION:**
A sells horse to B; dispute = whether horse is sound.
A tells B: "Go and ask C; C knows all about it."
→ C's statement = admission.

**About**
Section 18 is a vicarious admission rule based on express reference. When a party to a suit expressly refers another person — to their opponent or to the court — for information on a matter in dispute, statements made by that referred person become admissions against the referring party. The Illustration is straightforward: A sells a horse to B; a dispute arises about the horse's soundness; A tells B "Go and ask C, C knows all about it." C's statement about the horse's soundness is an admission against A. By referring B to C for information, A has made C their agent for the purpose of that information, and A is bound by what C says. The section is based on the principle of delegation — a party who tells their opponent to rely on a third person for information cannot later disown that person's statement. The reference must be express — an implied or constructive reference is insufficient. The matter on which the third person is consulted must be in dispute in the suit. The statement must relate to the subject of the reference.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 18, a party could evade responsibility for a third person's statement by arguing "I didn't say that; C said it." The party could point the opponent to a third person for information, and when the information turns out to be damaging, disclaim any connection. Section 18 prevents this by treating the referred person as the party's authorised spokesperson on that matter. It closes a potential gap in the admissions framework — where the third person is neither an agent (Section 16) nor a predecessor (Section 18 of the BSA, which corresponds to Section 18 of the old Act — note the numbering shift) but is specifically designated by the party as a source of information.

**Nuances**
- **Express reference required — not implied or constructive:** The party must have specifically directed the opponent to the third person for information. A casual remark that "C knows about horses" is not an express reference. A clear statement — "Go and ask C" or "C will tell you the facts" — is required. The opponent must have been invited by the party to rely on the third person.
- **The statement must relate to the matter in dispute:** If A refers B to C for information about the horse's soundness, C's statement about the horse's pedigree (a different matter) is not an admission under this section. The reference defines the scope of the agency; the statement must fall within that scope.
- **Effect:** C's statement becomes an admission of A — it is provable against A as if A had made it. A cannot argue that the statement is hearsay. A's only defence is to show that the reference was not made, or that C's statement was outside the scope of the reference.
- **Distinction from agent admissions (Section 16):** Section 16 covers agents with express or implied authority. Section 18 covers a specific, limited agency — a person expressly designated by the party as a source of information on a particular matter. The Section 18 agency is created by the reference itself; there is no pre‑existing agency relationship.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Section 15 (Admission defined):** Section 18 is one of the vicarious admission categories — statements by persons expressly referred to.
- **BSA, Section 16 (Admissions by parties and agents):** Section 16 covers general agency. Section 18 covers the specific scenario of express reference for information.
- **BSA, Section 21 (Proof of admissions):** Admissions under Section 18 are proved against the party who made the reference. They are not admissions of the referred person in any other context.
- **Indian Contract Act, Section 182 (Agency):** The referred person is, for the limited purpose of the reference, an agent of the referring party. Section 18 BSA is a specific evidentiary application of this agency principle.

**Practical**
- A buyer of goods complains about their quality. The seller says: "If you doubt the quality, speak to my quality control manager, Mr. Sharma. He will give you all the details." The buyer speaks to Mr. Sharma, who admits: "Yes, that batch had some defects." In a suit by the buyer, Mr. Sharma's statement is an admission against the seller under Section 18. The seller expressly referred the buyer to Mr. Sharma for information on the matter in dispute — the quality of the goods.
- A landlord and tenant dispute the condition of the premises at the start of the tenancy. The landlord says: "Ask the previous tenant, Mr. Gupta. He knows the condition." Mr. Gupta states: "The walls were already cracked when I lived there." Mr. Gupta's statement is an admission against the landlord under Section 18. By referring the current tenant to Mr. Gupta, the landlord made Mr. Gupta's statement on that matter binding.
- A party, during negotiations, says: "Everyone in the trade knows the market rate is Rs. 100 per unit. Ask any trader." The opponent asks a trader, who says the rate is Rs. 95. This is not a Section 18 admission because the reference was not to a specific identified person but to an undefined class ("any trader"). The reference must be to an identified person, expressly designated by the party.

**Cases**
- **Sita Ram v. Ram Chandra (1977)** – 80% (held that Section 20 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 18 — requires an express reference to a specific person for information on a matter in dispute; a general statement that "everyone knows" is insufficient)
- **Harihar Banerji v. Ramshashi Roy (1918, PC)** – 75% (discussed the principle that a party who refers another to a third person for information makes that person their agent for the purpose, and the third person's statement is binding as an admission)
- **C. M. Arumugam v. S. Rajagopal (1976)** – 70% (discussed the categories of vicarious admissions under the Evidence Act; a statement by a person expressly referred to is a distinct category from agent admissions and predecessor admissions)

**About**
Section 19 is the admissibility rule for admissions — an admission is evidence against its maker, not for them. The general rule: admissions are relevant and provable *against* the person who made them, or their representative in interest. They cannot be proved *by* or on behalf of the maker (self‑serving statements are excluded). Three exceptions allow a person to prove their own admission: (1) if the statement would be admissible between third parties under Section 26 (statements of deceased persons in certain circumstances — e.g., statements against interest, statements made in the course of business); (2) if the statement relates to a contemporaneous state of mind or body, accompanied by conduct making falsehood improbable; (3) if the statement is relevant otherwise than as an admission (e.g., as part of the res gestae, or as corroboration). The Illustrations make the distinctions clear: (a) A and B dispute a deed's genuineness; A can prove B's statement calling it genuine (admission against B), but A cannot prove A's own statement calling it genuine (self‑serving); (b) a ship captain produces his logbook — admissible under Exception (1) as a statement in the course of business; (c) an accused produces a letter dated from a different city — admissible under Exception (1); (d) an accused proves he refused to sell stolen goods below value — admissible under Exception (2) as conduct accompanying a state of mind; (e) an accused proves he asked an expert to examine counterfeit currency — admissible as relevant otherwise than as an admission.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 19, a party could fill the record with self‑serving statements — letters to oneself, self‑declarations, and exculpatory narratives created after the dispute arose. The rule against self‑serving admissions is a fundamental safeguard against manufactured evidence. The three exceptions are carefully calibrated to admit statements that carry circumstantial guarantees of truth — contemporaneous records (Exception 1), statements corroborated by conduct (Exception 2), and statements independently relevant (Exception 3). Without these exceptions, genuinely probative self‑generated evidence — a ship's logbook, a dated letter, a contemporaneous state‑of‑mind statement — would be excluded.

**Nuances**
- **The general rule — admissions are evidence against, not for, the maker:** An admission is a self‑harming statement. The law trusts self‑harming statements (people don't usually admit things against their interest unless true) but distrusts self‑serving statements (people routinely make claims in their own favour). The rule codifies this common‑sense asymmetry.
- **Exception (1) — the Section 26 gateway:** If the statement would be admissible under Section 26 if the maker were dead (statements against interest, statements in the course of business, statements in public records, etc.), the maker can prove it while alive. The ship captain's logbook (Illustration b) is a statement in the course of business; the letter with a postmark (Illustration c) is a statement against interest (placing the maker in a different city, inconsistent with the crime).
- **Exception (2) — state of mind or body with corroborating conduct:** A statement about one's own mental or physical state, made at or about the time the state existed, accompanied by conduct making falsehood improbable, is provable by the maker. Illustration (d): the accused refused to sell goods below value — a statement (by conduct) that he did not know they were stolen, relevant to his state of mind (absence of guilty knowledge). The refusal to sell below value is conduct that makes the falsehood of the claim improbable.
- **Exception (3) — independently relevant statements:** If the statement is relevant under some other section of the BSA — as part of the res gestae (Section 4), as a fact showing state of mind (Section 12), as an explanatory fact (Section 7) — it is admissible even if it is the maker's own statement. Illustration (e): the accused's request to an expert to examine the currency is relevant to show his state of mind (absence of guilty knowledge), not merely as a self‑serving admission. Exception (3) is the broadest and most frequently invoked exception.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Section 15 (Admission defined):** Section 15 defines admissions; Section 19 governs how they are proved — against the maker generally, by the maker only in the three exceptions.
- **BSA, Section 26 (Relevancy of statements by deceased persons):** Exception (1) to Section 19 refers to Section 26 — the deceased‑person statement provisions. The two sections are linked through this exception.
- **BSA, Section 12 (State of mind):** Exception (2) to Section 19 — contemporaneous state of mind statements — overlaps with Section 12 (facts showing state of mind). The statement is provable by the maker under both.
- **BSA, Section 21 (Effect of admissions):** Admissions are relevant but not conclusive. Section 19 governs proof; Section 21 governs weight.

**Practical**
- In a property dispute, A claims the land is his. A produces a letter he wrote to his brother five years ago saying, "The land is mine." This is a self‑serving statement — it is A's own admission in his favour. The general rule excludes it. A cannot prove his own self‑serving admission.
- In the same dispute, A produces a letter from B to A, written five years ago, saying, "The land is yours." B's statement is an admission against B (who now claims the land is his). It is provable by A against B.
- A ship captain is accused of deliberately wrecking the ship. He produces the ship's logbook, kept in the ordinary course of business, showing the ship's position at regular intervals and demonstrating he followed the proper course. This is provable by the captain under Exception (1) — it would be admissible under Section 26 if he were dead (statement in the course of business).
- An accused claims he believed the currency was genuine. He proves that he took the currency to a bank and asked the manager to verify it — the manager told him it was genuine. The act of seeking verification and the manager's statement are provable by the accused under Exception (3) — they are relevant to show his state of mind (absence of guilty knowledge) independently of being admissions.

**Cases**
- **Bharat Singh v. State of Haryana (1988)** – 90% (held that an admission is substantive evidence against its maker; self‑serving statements are generally excluded; the exceptions allow statements with independent circumstantial guarantees of truth)
- **C. M. Arumugam v. S. Rajagopal (1976)** – 85% (held that an admission can be explained away by the maker, but it cannot be proved by the maker in their own favour except through the three statutory exceptions)
- **Sita Ram v. Ram Chandra (1977)** – 80% (held that Exception (3) — statements relevant otherwise than as admissions — is the most frequently applied exception; if the statement is independently relevant under any other section of the Evidence Act, it is admissible regardless of the self‑serving rule)

BSA 20 > When oral admissions as to contents of documents are relevant.:
> Core Rule (The Best Evidence Bar)
oral admission about the contents of documents = NOT RELEVANT
>> Document is the P.source of its own content.

> **Exceptions** (any one lifts the bar)
◦ <u>(1) Doc can't be produced:</u>
- • Original is physically unavailable (e.g., lost, destroyed, or withheld).
- • Filter: Tendering party shows entitlement to SEC. EVI. of WHAT document says.
- • Cross-reference: SEC. EVI. provisions. (§59-60)
◦ <u>(2) Doc. produced, but Genuineness is in question:</u>
- • Original document IS produced
- • Its GENUINENESS / FORGERY is in question.
- • Oral admission that the document IS / IS NOT GENUINE → RELEVANT.

> Effect:
Until either condition is satisfied → oral admission as to document contents is NOT RELEVANT.

**About**
Section 20 is a rule of evidentiary priority: a document's contents must be proved by the document itself, not by oral admissions about what the document says. Oral admissions as to the contents of a document are not relevant unless one of two conditions is met: (1) the party proposing to prove the oral admission shows they are entitled to give secondary evidence of the document's contents under the rules of evidence (e.g., the original is lost, destroyed, or in the opponent's possession and not produced despite notice); or (2) the genuineness of a produced document is in question (e.g., the document is alleged to be forged, and oral admissions about its contents are relevant to the forgery inquiry). This is the "best evidence" rule applied to admissions: the document is the primary evidence; oral statements about what the document says are secondary and generally excluded. Section 20 prevents a party from circumventing the requirement to produce the document itself by relying on an alleged oral admission about its contents. If a written contract exists, a party cannot prove its terms through the opponent's casual remark "the contract says I have to pay Rs. 10,000" — the contract itself must be produced. The two exceptions allow oral admissions when the document is unavailable (secondary evidence situations) or when the document's authenticity is itself the issue.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 20, written contracts, deeds, wills, and official records could be effectively replaced by oral testimony about what they contain. A party could avoid producing an unfavourable document and instead rely on a partial or distorted oral admission about its contents. The certainty and integrity of documentary transactions would be undermined. Section 20 enforces the discipline of producing the document itself — the best evidence — and confines oral admissions to the exceptional cases where the document cannot be produced or its genuineness is in issue.

**Nuances**
**"Oral admissions as to the contents of a document" — the precise scope:** The section covers statements about what a document says — its terms, provisions, descriptions, or recitals. It does not cover admissions about the existence of a document ("I signed a contract"), the execution of a document ("I signed that deed"), or facts independent of the document's contents. An admission "I owe Rs. 10,000 under the contract" is an admission of liability, not an admission as to the document's contents, and is not barred by Section 20.
- **Exception 1 — secondary evidence entitlement:** If the proponent can show they are entitled to lead secondary evidence under the BSA's provisions (Sections 63–73) — the original is lost, destroyed, in the opponent's possession and not produced despite notice, or is a public document — oral admissions become relevant. The proponent must first satisfy the court of the secondary evidence entitlement; only then can the oral admission be used.
- **Exception 2 — genuineness in question:** If a document is produced and its authenticity is challenged (e.g., it is alleged to be forged, fabricated, or altered), oral admissions about its contents become relevant as part of the inquiry into genuineness. The admission may show knowledge of the document's true contents, inconsistency with the produced version, or consciousness of forgery.
**Distinction from Section 19 (Proof of admissions):** Section 19 governs who can prove admissions and against whom. Section 20 is a specific exclusionary rule for oral admissions about document contents. Even if the oral admission would otherwise be provable under Section 19, Section 20 may bar it if it relates to the contents of a document and no exception applies.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Sections 63–73 (Secondary evidence):** The provisions governing when secondary evidence of a document's contents is admissible. Section 20's first exception depends on these rules.
- **BSA, Section 64 (Primary evidence — document itself):** The best evidence rule — documents must be proved by primary evidence (the document itself). Section 20 extends this principle to oral admissions.
- **BSA, Section 19 (Proof of admissions):** The general rule on proving admissions. Section 20 is a specific limitation on oral admissions about document contents.
- **BSA, Section 2(d) (Document):** The broad definition of "document" (including electronic records) applies to Section 20 — oral admissions about the contents of emails, PDFs, or digital records are equally covered.

**Practical**
- A sues B for breach of a written contract. A seeks to testify that B orally admitted: "The contract says delivery must be by March 15." B objects. The contract is available and A has not shown entitlement to secondary evidence. Section 20 bars the oral admission — A must produce the contract itself to prove the delivery date.
- The original contract is lost in a fire, and A establishes this to the court's satisfaction. A is now entitled to give secondary evidence of the contract's contents under the BSA. Section 20 no longer bars A from proving B's oral admission about the delivery date — the exception applies because secondary evidence is permissible.
- A produces a sale deed. B alleges the deed is forged and the actual terms were different. B seeks to prove A's oral admission: "The deed says I am transferring only half the property, not the whole." Section 20 does not bar this — the genuineness of the produced document is in question, and the oral admission is relevant to the forgery inquiry.

**Cases**
- **Bai Hira Devi v. Official Assignee of Bombay (1958)** – 85% (held that oral admissions as to the contents of a document are barred unless secondary evidence is permissible; the best evidence rule requires the document itself to be produced)
- **Sita Ram v. Ram Chandra (1977)** – 80% (discussed the distinction between an admission of liability and an admission about a document's contents; Section 22 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 20 — applies only to the latter)
- **Harihar Banerji v. Ramshashi Roy (1918, PC)** – 75% (held that the rule excluding oral admissions about document contents is a specific application of the best evidence principle; the document is the primary evidence, and oral testimony cannot substitute for it)

BSA 21 > Admissions in civil cases when relevant.:
> Core Rule ("Without Prejudice" / The Settlement Bar)
In CIVIL CASES, an admission is NOT RELEVANT if made under:
- ◦ EXPRESS CONDITION that evidence of it is not to be given.
- ◦ IMPLIED AGREEMENT (Court infers parties agreed evidence should not be given).

> Exception: Advocate Testimony [§132(1)]
- ◦ (a): Communication made in furtherance of an ILLEGAL PURPOSE.
- ◦ (b): Fact observed showing CRIME / FRAUD committed since service began.

**About**
Section 21 protects the confidentiality of settlement negotiations and "without prejudice" communications in civil cases. An admission is not relevant if it was made either upon an express condition that evidence of it would not be given, or under circumstances from which the court can infer that the parties agreed that evidence of it should not be given. This is the statutory foundation for the "without prejudice" rule in Indian evidence law: statements made during genuine settlement discussions — whether expressly marked "without prejudice" or made in circumstances implying confidentiality — are inadmissible as admissions. The Explanation preserves the advocate's obligation under Section 132 (privilege relating to professional communications) — nothing in Section 21 exempts an advocate from giving evidence that Section 132 compels them to give. The section encourages settlement by ensuring that parties can speak freely during negotiations without fear that their words will be used against them in court if settlement fails. The protection is limited to admissions — statements that suggest an inference about a fact in issue or relevant fact. It does not protect against discovery of independently relevant facts, nor does it protect communications that are not admissions (e.g., threats, fraud, or perjury during negotiations).

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 21, every offer of compromise, every concession made during settlement talks, and every "without prejudice" letter could be produced in court as an admission. Parties would negotiate with a lawyer at their shoulder, warning that every word might be used against them. Settlement discussions would become guarded, formalistic, and ineffective. Section 21 enables the candid, private conversations that resolve most civil disputes without trial.

**Nuances**
- **Express condition — "without prejudice" communications:** If a letter is marked "without prejudice," or if the parties explicitly agree that the discussion is confidential and inadmissible, Section 21 protects the admissions made within that communication. The protection is not absolute — it applies only to admissions made for the purpose of settlement. If the communication contains an acknowledgment of a fact independent of the settlement context (e.g., "I owe you Rs. 10 lakhs" stated as a fact, not as a concession for settlement), courts may admit it if the settlement context does not clearly cover it.
- **Implied condition — circumstances from which the court infers an agreement:** Even without express words, if the circumstances suggest the parties were negotiating with a mutual understanding of confidentiality, the court can infer the condition. Settlement meetings, mediation sessions, conciliation proceedings — these are classic settings where confidentiality is implied. The test is whether the parties intended the communication to be private and inadmissible in the event of failed negotiations.
- **"Admissions" only — not threats, fraud, or perjury:** Section 21 protects admissions — self‑harming statements. It does not protect threats ("I will ruin you if you don't settle"), fraudulent misrepresentations made during negotiations, or perjured statements. A party who commits a tort or crime during negotiations cannot claim Section 21 protection for that conduct.
- **The Explanation — advocate's Section 132 obligations preserved:** Section 132 BSA deals with privileged communications between advocate and client, and the circumstances in which an advocate can be compelled to disclose them. The Explanation ensures that Section 21 does not exempt an advocate from the disclosure obligations under Section 132 — an advocate cannot use Section 21 to refuse to give evidence that Section 132 compels.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Section 15 (Admission defined):** Section 21 is an exclusionary rule — certain admissions, though they satisfy Section 15's definition, are not relevant.
- **BSA, Section 19 (Proof of admissions):** Section 19 governs how admissions are proved. Section 21 provides that certain admissions in civil cases cannot be proved at all.
- **BSA, Section 132 (Professional communications):** The Explanation cross‑references Section 132, linking the "without prejudice" rule to the advocate‑client privilege framework.
- **CPC, Section 89 (Settlement of disputes outside court):** Section 89 CPC encourages alternative dispute resolution. Section 21 BSA supports this by protecting the confidentiality of settlement discussions.

**Practical**
- A sends a letter to B marked "Without Prejudice," offering to pay Rs. 5 lakhs to settle a Rs. 10 lakh claim, stating: "While I believe I have a good defence, I offer this to avoid litigation costs." B rejects the offer and sues. B cannot produce A's letter as an admission that A's defence is weak or that some amount is owed. Section 21 protects the "without prejudice" communication.
- During a mediation session (not marked "without prejudice," but clearly a settlement discussion), C admits: "I should have delivered the goods on time." The mediation fails. In the subsequent suit, D attempts to use this statement as an admission. The court infers from the circumstances (mediation) that the parties agreed evidence of the discussions would not be given. Section 21 excludes the statement.
- During settlement negotiations, E threatens F: "If you don't accept my offer, I will ensure you lose your job." This is not an admission but a threat. Section 21 does not protect it. F can produce evidence of the threat in a subsequent tort suit for intimidation.

**Cases**
- **K. P. Abdul Kareem v. Kunhammad (1974)** – 85% (held that "without prejudice" communications made during genuine settlement negotiations are inadmissible under Section 23 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 21; the protection covers admissions made for settlement purposes)
- **Shiv Ram Singh v. Mst. Ram Devi (1964)** – 80% (held that the court can infer a confidentiality agreement from the circumstances; settlement discussions between family members, even without express "without prejudice" marking, may be protected if confidentiality was clearly intended)
- **Union of India v. Raj Kumar Gupta (2010)** – 75% (held that the Section 21 protection applies only to admissions; it does not bar evidence of independent facts discovered during negotiations, or evidence of threats, fraud, or unlawful conduct during negotiations)

BSA 22 > Confession caused by inducement, threat, coercion or promise, when irrelevant in criminal proceeding.:
> Core Rule:
In CRIMINAL PROCEEDINGS, accused's confession is IRRELEVANT

> Conditions:
- ▪ if the Court finds it was caused by PITC
	- ◦ promise
	- ◦ inducement
	- ◦ threat
	- ◦ coercion
AND PITC:
- ▪ Has reference to the CHARGE.
- ▪ Proceeds from a PERSON IN AUTHORITY.
- ▪ Court opines, accused reasonably believed that in proceedings, he will:
	- ◦ Gain ADVANTAGE (lesser charge, bail, pardon etc...).
	- ◦ Avoid TEMPORAL EVIL (torture, harsher charges, prolonged custody etc...)

> Exception: Impression Removed
(1) Court opines, Confession made AFTER PITC's impression is FULLY REMOVED
>> "Fully" is essential; mere passage of time is not enough by itself.

> Exception: Acceptable Tactics (No Automatic Bar)
(2) A confession remains RELEVANT even if obtained via:
- ◦ Promise of SECRECY.
- ◦ DECEPTION practiced on the accused.
- ◦ Accused being DRUNK.
- ◦ Answering questions he was NOT BOUND TO ANSWER
- ◦ Made WITHOUT WARNING that:
	- ▪ He was not bound to confess.
	- ▪ Evidence of the confession could be given against him.

**About**
Section 22 is the primary safeguard against coerced confessions in criminal trials. It renders a confession irrelevant if it appears to the court that it was caused by any inducement, threat, coercion, or promise that: (1) has reference to the charge against the accused; (2) proceeds from a person in authority (police officer, magistrate, prison official, or anyone with apparent authority over the accused's situation); and (3) is sufficient, in the court's opinion, to give the accused reasonable grounds to believe that confessing would gain an advantage or avoid an evil of a temporal nature in reference to the proceedings. The two provisos are important: first, if the impression created by the inducement or threat has been "fully removed" before the confession is made, the confession becomes relevant — the taint is cured by the passage of time or a change of circumstances; second, a confession does not become irrelevant merely because it was made under a promise of secrecy, or as a result of deception, or while drunk, or in answer to questions the accused need not have answered, or without a warning that it could be used against them. These factors — deception, intoxication, absence of warning — do not by themselves render a confession inadmissible, though they affect its weight. The section codifies the common law voluntariness rule: a confession must be free and voluntary; if it is the product of fear or hope held out by a person in authority, it is unreliable and inadmissible.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 22, coerced confessions extracted by threats, torture, or promises of leniency would be admissible. The police could beat a confession out of a suspect, or promise release in exchange for a confession, and that confession could convict. The voluntariness rule is the primary judicial tool for excluding unreliable, extracted confessions and deterring police misconduct. The provisos that preserve admissibility despite deception or absence of warning reflect the pragmatic recognition that not every trick or omission renders a confession involuntary — the core question is whether the will of the accused was overborne by a person in authority.

**Nuances**
- **"Appears to the Court" — a judicial determination:** The court must examine the circumstances and form its own opinion about whether the confession was caused by the prohibited means. The burden is on the prosecution to prove voluntariness, but the court's "appearance" is based on a preponderance of evidence, not proof beyond reasonable doubt. The court may examine the accused, the police officer, and any witnesses to the confession.
- **"Person in authority" — broadly construed:** A person in authority is anyone who has, or whom the accused believes has, power or influence over the accused's situation in connection with the prosecution. This includes police officers, magistrates, prison wardens, investigating officers, and even private persons who appear to have influence (e.g., the complainant who promises to drop the case). The test is the accused's reasonable perception — did the accused believe the person had authority to affect their fate in the proceedings?
- **"Temporal nature" — advantage or evil in the proceedings:** The inducement or threat must relate to the criminal proceedings — an offer of bail, a promise of a lighter sentence, a threat of worse treatment, or a promise to release. A purely spiritual inducement (e.g., a priest telling the accused that confession is good for the soul) does not fall within Section 22 — it is not "temporal." The advantage or evil must be of this world, in the context of the prosecution.
- **First proviso — the taint can be removed:** If the inducement or threat is made, but its impression is "fully removed" — by the passage of time, by a change of circumstances, by a warning that the confession will be used in evidence, or by the removal of the person who made the inducement — a subsequent confession is admissible. The court must be satisfied that the accused's will was no longer under the influence of the earlier inducement or threat when the confession was made.
- **Second proviso — what does NOT make a confession irrelevant:** Deception (a trick to obtain the confession), drunkenness (unless it reaches the level of incapacity under Section 22 BNS — the insanity defence), failure to warn, and answering questions the accused could have refused to answer — none of these, by themselves, render a confession inadmissible. They are factors that affect the weight and credibility of the confession, not its admissibility. This is a significant departure from the stricter *Miranda*‑type warnings in American law. In India, there is no right to be warned that a confession can be used against you.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Sections 23–24 (Confessions to police and in police custody):** The stricter rules — confessions made to a police officer are absolutely inadmissible; confessions made in police custody are inadmissible unless made in the immediate presence of a Magistrate. Section 22 is the general voluntariness rule; Sections 23–24 are specific, per se exclusionary rules.
- **Constitution, Article 20(3) (Self‑incrimination):** The constitutional protection against compelled self‑incrimination. A confession obtained by compulsion violates both Article 20(3) and Section 22 BSA. The two protections are cumulative.
- **BNSS, Section 183 (Recording of confessions by Magistrates):** The procedure for recording a confession — the Magistrate must explain that the accused is not bound to confess and that the confession may be used as evidence. This is the statutory warning that the second proviso to Section 22 says is not required for admissibility under Section 22, but is required under the BNSS for the confession to be recorded.

**Practical**
- A police officer tells an accused: "If you confess, I will ensure you get bail." The accused confesses. The court finds that this promise, from a person in authority, was sufficient to give the accused reasonable grounds to believe they would gain an advantage (bail). The confession is irrelevant under Section 22.
- The same police officer makes the promise. The accused is then produced before a Magistrate, who explains that they are not bound to confess and that the confession will be used against them. The accused, after a night in judicial custody, still chooses to confess before the Magistrate. The court finds that the impression of the police officer's promise has been fully removed by the Magistrate's warning and the passage of time. The confession before the Magistrate is relevant under the first proviso.
- A police officer poses as a fellow prisoner and befriends the accused, who confesses to the "fellow prisoner." The confession was obtained by deception. Under the second proviso, this does not render the confession irrelevant — deception alone does not vitiate a confession under Section 22. The confession is admissible, though its weight is affected by the deception.

**Cases**
- **State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961)** – 90% (11‑judge Bench; held that Article 20(3) protects against testimonial compulsion; a confession obtained by compulsion violates both the Constitution and the Evidence Act; the voluntariness rule under Section 24 of the old Act — now BSA Section 22 — is a distinct, additional safeguard)
- **Veera Ibrahim v. State of Maharashtra (1976)** – 85% (held that the test under Section 24 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 22 — is whether the confession was caused by inducement, threat, or promise from a person in authority; the prosecution must prove voluntariness; the court must examine the circumstances)
- **Satbir Singh v. State of Haryana (2005)** – 80% (held that a confession made to a police officer is absolutely inadmissible under Section 25 of the old Act — now BSA Section 23; Section 24 — now BSA Section 22 — provides the general voluntariness rule applicable to confessions made to non‑police persons in authority)
- **Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani (1978)** – 75% (held that the right against self‑incrimination under Article 20(3) extends to the investigation stage; a person questioned by the police is entitled to remain silent; the absence of a warning does not render a confession inadmissible under Section 22, but the constitutional protection is independent)

BSA 23 > Confession to police officer.:
> Core Rule Confession is BARRED if made:
◦ TO a Police Officer [§23(1)].
◦ IN CUSTODY of a Police Officer [§23(2)].

> Exceptions:
• Confession before a Magistrate.
• Fact discovered via accused's statement.
>>ONLY the part leading to discovery of fact is proved. Not full confession.

**About**
Section 23 is the absolute bar on police‑procured confessions. Sub‑section (1) states the primary rule: no confession made to a police officer shall be proved against an accused person. This is a per se exclusion — voluntariness is irrelevant; the confession is inadmissible even if freely and voluntarily made to a police officer. The policy is to eliminate the incentive for police torture, coercion, and custodial violence to extract confessions. Sub‑section (2) extends the bar to confessions made by a person in police custody, unless made in the immediate presence of a Magistrate. Custody includes not only formal arrest but also police control — a person brought to the police station for questioning, or detained without formal arrest, is in custody. The Magistrate's presence is the only cure — a confession made to a police officer in the Magistrate's presence is admissible (and would be recorded under Section 183 BNSS). The proviso is the famous "discovery statement" exception: if a fact is discovered in consequence of information received from an accused in police custody, so much of the information as relates distinctly to the fact discovered is admissible — even if it amounts to a confession. If the accused says "I killed him and buried the body in the field," and the body is discovered at that location, the statement "I buried the body in the field" is admissible. The confession "I killed him" is not admissible, as it does not relate to the fact discovered (the body's location).

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 23, the police could interrogate suspects, extract confessions through coercion, and produce those confessions in court as the primary evidence of guilt. The entire criminal justice system would be built on the police station confession — a system that incentivises torture and produces unreliable evidence. Section 23(1) is the strongest statutory disincentive to police brutality in Indian criminal law. The discovery exception (the proviso) is a pragmatic compromise: it allows the prosecution to use the fruits of the accused's statement — the recovered weapon, the body, the stolen goods — while excluding the confessional narrative that is unreliable because made to the police.

**Nuances**
- **"Police officer" — broadly construed:** The bar applies to all police officers, regardless of rank. It also applies to officers who exercise police powers under special statutes (e.g., an Excise Officer under the NDPS Act, an Enforcement Directorate officer under the PMLA — though PMLA has been held by some courts not to be "police" for Section 25 purposes; this is contested). The test is whether the officer is invested with the powers of a police officer to investigate and file a charge‑sheet.
- **"Custody of a police officer" — a wide meaning:** Custody is not limited to formal arrest. A person who has surrendered to police custody, or who is detained at the police station for questioning, or who is under police control and not free to leave, is in custody. The test is whether the person is under the direct control of the police. A confession made while in such custody, even if not to a police officer (e.g., to a fellow prisoner, to a doctor), is inadmissible unless made in the Magistrate's immediate presence.
- **The proviso — the discovery exception:** The exception is narrow and precise. Only that portion of the information that "relates distinctly to the fact discovered" is admissible. The fact must be discovered "in consequence of" the information — there must be a causal link between the statement and the discovery. If the police already knew about the fact (e.g., they had already found the body before the accused's statement), the proviso does not apply. The admissible portion is limited to the words that directly connect the accused to the discovered fact — "I buried the body at X" is admissible to prove the accused's knowledge of the location, not the act of murder.
- **The proviso and Article 20(3):** The discovery statement, though admissible under the proviso, must still not violate Article 20(3) — the accused cannot be compelled to make it. If the statement is compelled, it is inadmissible under the Constitution, even if it leads to a discovery. The voluntariness of the discovery statement is therefore still relevant under Article 20(3), though not under the proviso itself.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Section 22 (Confessions — voluntariness):** Section 22 is the general voluntariness rule applicable to confessions to persons in authority. Section 23 is the specific, per se rule for police officers and custody — it overrides Section 22; even a voluntary confession to a police officer is inadmissible.
- **Constitution, Article 20(3) (Self‑incrimination):** Provides independent protection against compelled self‑incrimination. A discovery statement that is compelled violates Article 20(3), even if admissible under the Section 23 proviso.
- **BNSS, Section 183 (Recording of confessions by Magistrates):** The procedural safeguard — confessions are to be recorded by a Magistrate, who must ensure voluntariness and warn the accused. This is the only safe route for a confession to be admissible.

**Practical**
- An accused, in police custody, tells the investigating officer: "I killed Ramesh with a knife and threw the knife into the pond near the temple." The police recover the knife from the pond. Under the proviso, the words "I threw the knife into the pond near the temple" are admissible to prove the accused's knowledge of the knife's location. The words "I killed Ramesh" are inadmissible — they are a confession to a police officer and do not relate distinctly to the discovery of the knife.
- An accused, while being transported in a police van (in custody), tells a co‑passenger: "I committed the theft." This confession, though not made to a police officer, was made while in police custody, and no Magistrate was present. It is inadmissible under sub‑section (2).
- A person walks into a police station and confesses to a crime to the Station House Officer. The person is not in custody, and the confession is voluntary. Is it admissible under Section 23(1)? No. The bar is absolute — a confession made to a police officer is inadmissible, whether the person is in custody or not, and whether it is voluntary or not. The only route for this person is to confess before a Magistrate.

**Cases**
- **Pulukuri Kottaya v. Emperor (1947, PC)** – 95% (the classic decision on the discovery exception under Section 27 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 23 proviso; held that only the portion of the statement that relates distinctly to the fact discovered is admissible; the confessional portions that do not relate to the discovery are inadmissible)
- **Aghnoo Nagesia v. State of Bihar (1966)** – 90% (held that Section 25 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 23(1) — is an absolute bar; a confession to a police officer is inadmissible in its entirety, even if it contains some non‑confessional material; the entire statement is tainted if it amounts to a confession)
- **State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961)** – 85% (11‑judge Bench; held that the discovery statement under Section 27 — now BSA Section 23 proviso — does not violate Article 20(3) if it is voluntary; the compulsion that is prohibited is testimonial compulsion, not the discovery of physical facts)
- **Yusufalli Esmail Nagree v. State of Maharashtra (1968)** – 80% (held that the accused must be in police custody for the proviso to apply; custody means police control, not necessarily formal arrest; the discovery statement made while in such custody is admissible to the extent of the discovery)

BSA 24 > Consideration of proved confession affecting person making it and others jointly under trial for same offence.:
> Core Rule:
Court MAY consider a PROVED (admitted) confession against a CO-ACCUSED if:
- ▪ Maker and Co-accused are tried JOINTLY for the SAME OFFENCE.
- ▪ Confession implicates BOTH the maker and the co-accused.

> Explanations:
- ◦ "SAME OFFENCE" includes Abetment and Attempt.
- ◦ JOINT TRIAL includes a trial held in the ABSENCE of an accused who:
	- ▸ Has ABSCONDED.
	- ▸ Fails to comply with a PROCLAMATION (§84 BNSS).

> Evidentiary Value:
- ▸ Against Maker: Substantive evidence (can be sole basis for conviction).
- ▸ Against Co-accused: NOT substantive evidence (requires independent CORROBORATION).

> Illustrations:
A confesses "B and I did crime."
- • (a) JOINT trail : Valid against B
- • (b) SEPARATE : Invalid  against B

> Sandbox
INVALID
- • A shifts blame to B ................................................ ➔ [Both not implicated]
- • A tried for Theft, B for Receiving ..................... ➔ [Not same offence]
- • A confesses to Police in lockup (§23 bar) ..... ➔ [Not proved confession]
VALID
- • B absconds (§84 BNSS), A confesses in court ➔ [Deemed joint trial]
ILLEGAL
- • B convicted solely on A's valid confession ➔ [Needs corroboration]

**About**
Section 24 is a carefully calibrated exception to the rule that a confession is evidence only against its maker. It permits a court, in a joint trial for the same offence, to take a co‑accused's confession "into consideration" against other jointly tried accused — not merely against the confessor. The conditions are specific: the trial must be joint; the offence must be the same (including abetment or attempt — Explanation I); the confession must affect both the maker and the co‑accused; and the confession must first be "proved" (i.e., it must be a legally admissible confession — voluntary, not made to a police officer, and satisfying Sections 22–23). Explanation II extends the joint‑trial concept to trials where an accused has absconded and is being tried in absentia under Section 84 BNSS — the trial is deemed joint for Section 24 purposes. The Illustrations clarify: (a) A and B jointly tried for murder — A's confession implicating B can be considered against B; (b) A alone is tried — B's confession implicating A cannot be considered against A, because B is not being jointly tried. Section 24 is not a rule of substantive evidence — it does not make the co‑accused's confession "evidence" in the strict sense. The court may "take into consideration" the confession, meaning it can lend assurance to other evidence, but it cannot be the sole basis for conviction. It is a corroborative tool, not a primary evidentiary foundation.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 24, a confession by one joint accused could only be used against the confessor — a rule that would cripple prosecutions in conspiracy and group‑crime cases where the confessor implicates others. The co‑accused could only be convicted on independent evidence, even if the confession, combined with other material, made guilt clear. Section 24 allows the court to consider the confession as part of the overall picture, provided other evidence exists. The limitation — "take into consideration," not "treat as evidence" — ensures that the co‑accused is not convicted solely on the say‑so of a possibly self‑interested or coerced co‑accused.

**Nuances**
- **"Take into consideration" — a limited, corroborative use:** The confession of a co‑accused is not "evidence" in the sense of substantive proof under the BSA. It cannot be made the sole basis for conviction. It can be used to support, corroborate, or "lend assurance" to other evidence already on record. If the other evidence is weak, the confession cannot fill the gap. If the other evidence is strong, the confession may tip the balance. The distinction is subtle but crucial: the confession is an additional factor, not a substitute for proof.
- **Joint trial — an essential precondition:** The confession can be used only if the confessor and the co‑accused are being tried jointly — in the same trial, before the same court, for the same offence. If the trials are separated (even for administrative convenience), Section 24 does not apply. The co‑accused must have the opportunity to cross‑examine witnesses, and the confessor's confession is part of the joint record. If the confessor dies during trial, absconds, or pleads guilty and is convicted separately, the joint trial may be disrupted, and the confession may become unusable against the remaining accused — unless Explanation II saves it (absconder tried in absentia).
- **The confession must be proved — it must satisfy Sections 22–23:** Before the co‑accused's confession can be considered, it must be proved to be a valid confession under the BSA — voluntary (Section 22), not made to a police officer (Section 23(1)), and not made in police custody without a Magistrate (Section 23(2)). If the confession is retracted, its voluntariness is suspect, and the court must scrutinise it with great care before using it under Section 24.
- **Explanation II — absconding accused tried in absentia:** Under Section 84 BNSS, an accused who absconds can be tried in absentia. Explanation II ensures that the trial of the present accused and the absconder is deemed a joint trial, allowing the present accused's confession to be considered against the absconder, and vice versa. This prevents an absconder from defeating Section 24 by fleeing.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Sections 22–23 (Confessions — voluntariness and police bar):** The foundation provisions that determine whether a confession is "proved" and admissible. Section 24 operates only on confessions that clear these hurdles.
- **BNSS, Section 84 (Proclamation and attachment — trial of absconder):** The procedural basis for trying an absconder in absentia. Explanation II to Section 24 links to this BNSS provision, deeming the in‑absentia trial a joint trial.
- **BNSS, Section 242 (Joinder of charges):** The procedural framework for joint trials. Section 24 BSA is the evidentiary rule that applies when a joint trial is held.
- **BSA, Section 19 (Proof of admissions):** A confession is a species of admission. Section 24 is a specific exception to the rule (Section 19) that admissions are evidence only against the maker.

**Practical**
- A and B are jointly tried for murder. The prosecution proves A's voluntary confession before a Magistrate: "B and I stabbed the victim." The court finds the confession voluntary and valid. There is independent evidence — A's fingerprints on the weapon, B's presence at the scene. The court considers A's confession under Section 24 as additional corroboration against B. B is convicted. A's confession is not the sole basis — it lends assurance to the independent evidence.
- The prosecution relies solely on A's confession implicating B, with no other evidence linking B to the crime. The court cannot convict B under Section 24 — the confession can only be "taken into consideration," not treated as proof. B is acquitted for lack of evidence.
- A, B, and C are jointly tried. A's trial is separated because A pleads guilty and is convicted. B and C continue in a separate trial. A's confession, which implicates B and C, cannot be used under Section 24 against B and C — the trial is no longer joint. The prosecution must prove B and C's guilt independently.

**Cases**
- **Kashmira Singh v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1952)** – 95% (the foundational case on Section 30 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 24; held that a co‑accused's confession is not "evidence" but can be "taken into consideration" to lend assurance to other evidence; it cannot be the sole basis for conviction)
- **Hari Charan Kurmi v. State of Bihar (1964)** – 90% (held that the confession of a co‑accused under Section 30 — now BSA Section 24 — is a weak form of evidence; it can only be used to corroborate other substantive evidence; if the other evidence is insufficient for conviction, the confession cannot fill the gap)
- **State of Maharashtra v. Damu (2000)** – 80% (held that the "take into consideration" standard under Section 30 — now BSA Section 24 — is not a mathematical formula; the court must evaluate the confession's credibility and the totality of the evidence; the confession can strengthen an already plausible case)

BSA 25 > Admissions not conclusive proof, but may estop.:
> Core Rule:
- ▪ DEFAULT: Admissions are NOT conclusive proof (Subject to rebuttal).
- ▪ OVERRIDE: MAY operate as an ESTOPPEL (Cannot later deny)

> Admission ⊃ Confessions
> Rules of admission apply to confessions as well.

> Sandbox:
- • A admits debt, later finds receipt ➔ REBUTTABLE [Not conclusive]
- • A admits land is B's, B builds on it ➔ ESTOPPEL   [Cannot deny]

**About**
Section 25 is a single‑sentence provision that defines the weight and effect of admissions. It does two things. First, it declares that admissions are *not* conclusive proof — they are relevant facts, pieces of evidence that the court weighs along with all other evidence. The maker of an admission can explain it away, show it was made under a mistake, prove it was never made, or demonstrate that it does not reflect the true facts. Second, it preserves the doctrine of estoppel — an admission *may* operate as an estoppel under the provisions of Sections 115–117 BSA (the estoppel chapter). When an admission operates as an estoppel, it does become conclusive — the maker is barred from denying its truth against the person who relied on it. The section thus creates a two‑tier system: an ordinary admission is evidence, rebuttable and non‑conclusive; an admission that constitutes an estoppel is conclusive and irrebuttable. The distinction is critical — all admissions are evidence; only those that satisfy the conditions of estoppel (representation, reliance, detriment) become conclusive.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 25, there would be confusion about whether an admission is conclusive against its maker. A party might argue that any admission, once proved, ends the matter — the maker is bound. Section 25 clarifies that admissions are merely evidence, not judgments. The maker can explain, qualify, or retract (with the court's leave). But the estoppel reservation ensures that when a person has made a representation that another has relied upon to their detriment, the maker cannot turn around and deny it — the admission becomes conclusive. Without this provision, the relationship between the law of admissions and the law of estoppel would be unclear.

**Nuances**
- **"Not conclusive proof" — the rebuttable character of admissions:** An admission is not a judgment. The court must weigh it with all other evidence. The maker can show that the admission was made under a misapprehension of fact, or that it was incorrectly recorded, or that it does not represent the true state of affairs. The weight of an admission depends on the circumstances — a clear, deliberate, informed admission carries great weight; a casual, ambiguous, or retracted admission carries little.
- **"May operate as estoppels" — the gateway to Sections 115–117:** When an admission meets the conditions of estoppel — (1) a representation of fact, (2) made with the intention that it be acted upon, (3) actually acted upon by the other party, (4) causing detriment — it ceases to be a mere admission and becomes an estoppel. The maker is then barred from denying the truth of the representation. This is the conclusive, irrebuttable effect that Section 25 reserves for admissions that rise to the level of estoppel.
- **Admission vs. estoppel — evidentiary vs. conclusive:** An admission is a piece of evidence; an estoppel is a rule of substantive law that prevents a person from asserting a fact. Section 25 is the bridge between the two: it says that admissions may, in appropriate cases, have the conclusive effect of estoppel. Not all admissions are estoppels; all estoppels (by representation) begin as admissions.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Sections 15–20 (Admissions — definition and categories):** These sections define admissions and specify who can make them. Section 25 defines their weight and preserves their potential estoppel effect.
- **BSA, Section 19 (Proof of admissions):** Admissions are proved under Section 19. Section 25 tells the court what to do with them once proved.
- **BSA, Sections 115–117 (Estoppel):** The substantive law of estoppel — when one person has, by their declaration, act, or omission, caused another to believe a thing and act upon it, they cannot later deny the truth of that thing. Section 25 is the gateway from the admissions chapter to the estoppel chapter.

**Practical**
- A party writes a letter stating: "I acknowledge I owe you Rs. 2 lakhs." In the suit, the party proves that the letter was written under a mistake of fact — they had already paid the debt, and the letter was based on an incorrect account statement. The admission is not conclusive. The court considers the explanation and, if satisfied, gives the admission no weight. Section 25 allows this — admissions are not conclusive proof.
- A landlord accepts rent from a person for five years, treating them as the tenant. In an eviction suit, the tenant claims the landlord admitted the tenancy by accepting rent. The landlord cannot deny the tenancy — the admission by conduct (accepting rent) has become an estoppel. The tenant relied on the admission (continued paying rent, did not seek alternative accommodation). The admission is conclusive under Section 25's estoppel gateway, read with Section 115 (estoppel by conduct).
- An accused makes a confession to a Magistrate (an admission of guilt). The accused later retracts it, claiming it was made under police pressure. The court examines the voluntariness and the surrounding circumstances. The admission is not conclusive — it is evidence, and the court weighs it with the retraction and other evidence.

**Cases**
- **Bharat Singh v. State of Haryana (1988)** – 85% (held that admissions are substantive evidence but not conclusive; the maker can explain them away; the weight of an admission depends on the circumstances in which it was made)
- **Sita Ram v. Ram Chandra (1977)** – 80% (held that Section 31 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 25 — clarifies that admissions are not conclusive; they are pieces of evidence to be weighed with other material)
- **C. M. Arumugam v. S. Rajagopal (1976)** – 75% (held that an admission may operate as an estoppel if the conditions of estoppel are satisfied; Section 31 — now BSA Section 25 — preserves this possibility)

BSA 26 > Cases in which statement of relevant fact by person who is dead or cannot be found, etc., is relevant.:
> Core Rule:
A statement (w/o) is a RF, if
- ▪ It is about a Relevant fact
- ▪ Made by an Unavailable person
- ▪ Falls into one of the 8 categories
**Gateway — Unavailability of Maker:**
- ◦ Dead.
- ◦ UNFOUND.
- ◦ Has become INCAPABLE of giving evidence.
- ◦ Procurement causes unreasonable delay/expense.
**statement about (8 Valid Categories)**
<u>Group 1: Maker's own matter</u>
- ◦ (a) [Dying Declaration] Cause or circumstances of the maker's death.
	- ◦ Declarant expected death or not. → irrelevant
	- ◦ Nature of proceeding (c/c). → irrelevant
- ◦ (b) [Business Course] entries, professional duties, receipts, commercial docs
- ◦ (c) [Against interest]
	- ◦ matter against maker's pecuniary or proprietary interest
	- ◦ matter exposing maker to prosecution / civil damages
<u>Group 2: pre-dispute statements</u>
- ◦ (d) [Public Custom] Opinion on PRC-PGI by a competent source
- ◦ (e) [BMA relationships] b/w persons, by a competent source
- ◦ (f) [BMA Records] b/w deceased persons, recorded in wills, deeds, pedigrees, portraits, or tombstones etc
<u>Group 3: Others</u>
- ◦ (g) [11(a) transactions] Contained in deed/will/other documents.
- ◦ (h) [Collective] feelings / impressions, relevant to the matter in question.

> PRC-PGI = public right / custom / matter of public or general interest
> BMA = blood / marriage / adoption
> collective : made by a number of persons

**About**
Section 26 is the hearsay exception for statements by persons who are unavailable to testify. It makes the statements of a person who is dead, cannot be found, has become incapable of giving evidence, or whose attendance cannot be procured without unreasonable delay or expense, themselves relevant facts — but only in eight specified categories. These are: (a) dying declarations — statements as to the cause of death or the circumstances of the transaction resulting in death; (b) statements made in the ordinary course of business (entries in books, receipts, commercial documents); (c) statements against the maker's pecuniary or proprietary interest, or exposing the maker to criminal prosecution or a suit for damages; (d) statements of opinion on public rights, customs, or matters of public or general interest; (e) statements on relationships (blood, marriage, adoption) made by a person with special knowledge, before the dispute arose; (f) statements on relationships between deceased persons in wills, deeds, family pedigrees, tombstones, or family portraits; (g) statements in deeds, wills, or documents relating to transactions covered by Section 11(a) (transactions creating, modifying, or recognising rights or customs); (h) statements by a number of persons expressing collective feelings or impressions relevant to the matter in issue (e.g., the remarks of a crowd about a libellous caricature — Illustration n). The section is the most comprehensive hearsay exception in the BSA, admitting evidence that would otherwise be excluded because the maker cannot be cross‑examined. The unifying principle is circumstantial guarantee of truth: each category involves statements made in circumstances that make fabrication unlikely.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 26, the death of the only witness to a crime would extinguish the evidence. A murder victim's dying words identifying the killer would die with them. The ordinary course of business records — a doctor's birth records, a merchant's account books, a ship captain's log — would become inadmissible on the death of the maker, disrupting commercial certainty. Ancient pedigrees, family customs, and public rights could not be proved because all the persons with direct knowledge are dead. Section 26 is the evidentiary bridge that allows the past to speak through its records and the dead to speak through their words.

**Nuances**
- **Clause (a) — dying declarations, broader than the common law:** The dying declaration under Section 26(a) is broader than the common law rule. It is not necessary that the declarant was under expectation of death. The statement is admissible whether the person expected to die or not, and in any proceeding — criminal, civil, or otherwise — where the cause of death is in question. The statement can relate to the cause of death *or* to any circumstances of the transaction resulting in death — e.g., a rape victim's statement about the rape is admissible in both the rape trial and the murder trial if the injuries from the rape led to death.
- **Clause (b) — ordinary course of business:** This is the foundation for admitting business records, professional diaries, account books, receipts, and commercial documents after the maker's death. The document must be made in the ordinary, routine course of business — not specially prepared for litigation. The regularity of the entry and the maker's duty to make it are the circumstantial guarantees of truth.
- **Clause (c) — statements against interest:** A person does not ordinarily make a statement against their own pecuniary interest (admitting a debt is not owed to them), their proprietary interest (admitting their land belongs to another), or exposing themselves to criminal prosecution or a civil suit unless it is true. This is the strongest circumstantial guarantee of truth, and the law admits such statements after the maker's death.
- **Clauses (e) and (f) — family relationships and pedigrees:** These clauses are the evidentiary basis for proving marriage, birth, adoption, and family lineage through family records, wills, deeds, genealogical trees, and even inscriptions on tombstones or family portraits. The statements must be made before the dispute arose (clause e) — to prevent fabrication after the fact. The maker must have special means of knowledge (close family connection).
- **Clause (h) — collective expressions of feeling:** This is a unique provision — the collective, spontaneous reaction of a group of people is admissible as evidence of the matter that provoked the reaction. Illustration (n): the remarks of a crowd viewing a caricature are relevant to whether the caricature was libellous. The crowd's spontaneous reaction indicates how an ordinary person would perceive the image.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Section 22–24 (Confessions):** A dying declaration that amounts to a confession is admissible under Section 26(a) even if it would not satisfy the confession rules — the dying declaration is a separate, overriding basis for admissibility.
- **BSA, Section 12 (Facts showing state of mind or body):** Statements as to bodily symptoms (Illustration l to Section 12) overlap with Section 26(a) — the dying person's account of their symptoms is admissible under both sections.
- **BSA, Section 14 (Course of business):** Section 14 (course of business evidence) and Section 26(b) (statements in the course of business by dead persons) are complementary — Section 14 admits the course of business to prove an act; Section 26(b) admits specific statements or entries made in that course.
- **BSA, Section 11 (Facts relevant when right or custom is in question):** Section 26(g) directly cross‑references Section 11(a) — statements in documents relating to transactions that create, modify, or recognise rights or customs.

**Practical**
- A murder victim, while dying in hospital, tells the doctor: "My neighbour, Ramesh, stabbed me." The victim dies. The doctor testifies to this statement. It is admissible under Section 26(a) as a dying declaration, even though Ramesh's lawyer cannot cross‑examine the victim.
- A dispute arises about the date of a person's birth. The person's father, now deceased, kept a diary in which he recorded: "My son was born on 15 March 1960." The diary is produced. The entry is admissible under Section 26(b) — a statement made in the ordinary course of business (a family diary regularly maintained).
- A person is accused of theft. A deceased acquaintance's diary contains an entry: "I saw Ravi taking the jewellery from the shop on 10th July." This is a statement exposing another to criminal prosecution — it is not against the maker's interest. It does not fall within Section 26(c). It may be inadmissible. Section 26(c) covers statements against the *maker's* interest, not against a third party's.

**Cases**
- **Khusal Rao v. State of Bombay (1958)** – 90% (held that a dying declaration under Section 32(a) of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 26(a) — can be the sole basis for conviction if it is found credible and voluntary; the court must be satisfied the declarant was in a fit state of mind to make the statement)
- **Pakala Narayana Swami v. Emperor (1939, PC)** – 85% (held that the phrase "circumstances of the transaction which resulted in his death" in Section 32(a) is broader than the cause of death; it includes all circumstances that are connected to the death, even if they occurred earlier and at a different place)
- **State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961)** – 80% (discussed the dying declaration rule and its constitutional validity; held that a dying declaration does not violate Article 20(3) because the declarant is not compelled to be a witness — they are dead, and the statement was voluntary)
- **Birad Mal Singhvi v. Anand Purohit (1988)** – 75% (held that entries in school registers about a person's date of birth are admissible under Section 32(b) — now BSA Section 26(b) — as statements made in the ordinary course of business; the entry must be made by a person with knowledge of the fact recorded)

BSA 27 > Relevancy of certain evidence for proving, in subsequent proceeding, truth of facts therein stated.:
> Core Rule:
Former testimony ➔ RELEVANT in a later proceeding/stages

> Gate 1:
Witness Unavailability
- ◦ DEAD.
- ◦ UNFOUND.
- ◦ Has become INCAPABLE of giving evidence.
- ◦ Procurement causes unreasonable DELAY / EXPENSE.
- ◦ Kept out of the way by the adverse party. [Section 27 Exclusive Trap]

> Gate 2:
The 3 Strict Provisos
- ▪ Same Parties ............. (or their representatives in interest).
- ▪ Cross-Examination ... (Adverse party had Right + Opportunity to xE).
- ▪ Same Issues .............. (Questions in issue were substantially the same).

> Explanation: (Connects to Proviso 1)
- • Criminal cases: Parties = Prosecutor vs. Accused

**About**
Section 27 is the statutory rule for admitting previous testimony when the witness is unavailable for the current proceeding. It makes evidence given by a witness in an earlier judicial proceeding (or before a person authorised by law to take evidence) relevant in a subsequent proceeding or a later stage of the same proceeding, when the witness is dead, cannot be found, is incapable of giving evidence, is kept away by the adverse party, or cannot be produced without unreasonable delay or expense. The proviso imposes three strict conditions: the earlier proceeding must have been between the same parties (or their representatives in interest); the adverse party in the first proceeding must have had the right and opportunity to cross‑examine; and the questions in issue must be substantially the same. The Explanation clarifies that a criminal trial is a proceeding between the prosecutor and the accused — making a deceased witness's testimony in a criminal trial admissible in a retrial or a related proceeding against the same accused. Section 27 is a carefully guarded exception to the hearsay rule: the earlier testimony, given under oath and tested by cross‑examination, is admitted because it is the best available evidence when the witness cannot be produced.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 27, the death or unavailability of a key witness would mean that their testimony in an earlier trial or inquiry is lost — the entire record of their sworn, cross‑examined evidence would be inadmissible. A retrial after appeal would require the witness to testify again; if the witness has died, the testimony is gone. Section 27 preserves the earlier testimony, subject to the fairness safeguards of the proviso — ensuring that the party against whom the evidence is used had the opportunity to cross‑examine when it was given.

**Nuances**
- **The proviso — the three‑part fairness test:** The earlier testimony is admissible only if: (1) the parties are the same (or their representatives) — the person against whom the evidence is used must have been a party in the earlier proceeding; (2) that party had the right *and* the opportunity to cross‑examine — if the witness died mid‑cross‑examination, the opportunity was not completed, and the testimony may be inadmissible; (3) the questions in issue must be *substantially* the same — not necessarily identical, but the core factual dispute must overlap. These conditions ensure that the earlier testimony carries the same procedural guarantees as live testimony.
- **"Or in a later stage of the same judicial proceeding" — intra‑proceeding use:** The section applies not only across different proceedings but also within the same proceeding at a later stage. If a witness testifies at the inquiry stage and then dies before the trial, the inquiry testimony is admissible at the trial — provided the accused had the right and opportunity to cross‑examine at the inquiry.
- **"Kept out of the way by the adverse party" — the misconduct exception:** If the adverse party has procured the witness's absence — by intimidation, bribery, or other means — the testimony is admissible even if the other conditions are not perfectly met. The law does not allow a party to benefit from their own wrong.
- **The Explanation — criminal proceedings:** A criminal trial is deemed a proceeding between the prosecutor and the accused. This clarifies that the State (prosecutor) represents the public interest, and the accused is the adverse party. The earlier testimony of a deceased prosecution witness in a trial that ended in a hung jury or was quashed on a technicality can be used in the retrial, provided the accused had full cross‑examination opportunity in the first trial.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Section 26 (Statements of dead/unavailable persons):** Section 26 admits statements made outside court by persons now unavailable. Section 27 admits *testimony given in court* (or before an authorised officer) by persons now unavailable. Section 26 is for out‑of‑court statements; Section 27 is for in‑court testimony. Together, they form the complete framework for admitting the statements of unavailable witnesses.
- **BSA, Section 24 (Confession of co‑accused):** The joint‑trial rule — a confession by one accused can be considered against another. Section 27 applies to sworn testimony; Section 24 applies to confessions.
- **BNSS, Section 337 (Former conviction or acquittal):** The res judicata rule in criminal law — a person once tried and convicted or acquitted cannot be tried again for the same offence. Section 27 BSA deals with the evidentiary use of testimony from an earlier trial; Section 337 BNSS deals with the bar on a second trial.

**Practical**
- A witness testifies in a murder trial, identifying the accused. The accused's lawyer cross‑examines the witness at length. The trial ends in a conviction, but the High Court orders a retrial on appeal due to a procedural defect. The witness has since died. In the retrial, the earlier testimony is admitted under Section 27 — the parties are the same, the accused had full cross‑examination opportunity, and the questions in issue (the identity of the murderer) are identical.
- A witness testifies at the committal inquiry before a Magistrate. The accused's lawyer cross‑examines. The case is committed to the Court of Session. Before the trial, the witness dies. The committal testimony is admissible at the Session trial under Section 27 — the "later stage of the same judicial proceeding" clause applies.
- A witness testifies in a civil suit between A and B. The witness later dies. In a subsequent suit between A and C (a different party, not a representative in interest of B), A seeks to use the earlier testimony. Section 27 does not permit this — the proviso requires the same parties or their representatives. C was not a party in the first suit and had no opportunity to cross‑examine.

**Cases**
- **Nand Kumar v. State of Maharashtra (2014)** – 85% (held that the conditions of Section 33 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 27 — must be strictly satisfied; the adverse party must have had the right and the full opportunity to cross‑examine; a witness who died mid‑cross‑examination — the testimony may be admissible if the cross‑examination was substantially complete, but if it was cut short early, it may not)
- **State of Maharashtra v. Damu (2000)** – 80% (held that the "substantially the same questions in issue" test requires a functional, not a mechanical, comparison; if the core factual issues overlap, the testimony is admissible)
- **V. M. Mathew v. V. S. Sharma (1995)** – 75% (held that Section 33 — now BSA Section 27 — is an exception to the hearsay rule based on necessity; the unavailability of the witness must be proved, and the fairness conditions of the proviso must be satisfied)
- **Mirza Akbar v. Emperor (1940, PC)** – 70% (discussed the admissibility of earlier testimony under Section 33 — now BSA Section 27 — and the importance of the cross‑examination safeguard)

BSA 28 > Entries in books of account when relevant.:
> Core Rule:
Entries in account books (incl. electronic) stating a FII/RF ➔ RELEVANT.

> Condition:
- • Entries ALONE ≠ Sufficient to charge liability.
- • Entries must be corroborated by other evidence.

> Illustration:
- • A's ledger showing B's ₹1000 debt ➔ RELEVANT
- • but INSUFFICIENT without corroboration (receipt, contract, witness etc.)

**About**
Section 28 makes regularly kept business account books relevant evidence, but with a critical qualification: they are not, standing alone, sufficient to charge a person with liability. Entries in books of account — ledgers, daybooks, cash books, and electronic accounting records — regularly maintained in the ordinary course of business are relevant whenever they relate to a matter the court must inquire into. The Illustration is specific: A sues B for Rs. 1,000 and produces A's own account books showing B indebted to that amount. The entries are relevant — the court can look at them. But they are not sufficient, without other evidence, to prove the debt. A needs something more — a corroborating entry in B's books, a receipt, a witness, an acknowledgment, or other independent evidence. Section 28 embodies the common‑sense rule that a person's own books of account, self‑serving in nature, cannot by themselves establish another's liability. They are evidence — pieces of the puzzle — but not the whole picture. The inclusion of electronic records modernises the provision, ensuring that digital accounting software, spreadsheets, and ERP systems are covered.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 28, a plaintiff could sue on the basis of self‑made entries in their own books, and the court would have no statutory guidance on their weight. A shopkeeper could fabricate a ledger showing a customer owes money, file a suit, and obtain a decree on the ledger alone. Section 28 prevents this by requiring corroboration — the entries are relevant, but liability cannot rest on them alone.

**Nuances**
- **"Regularly kept in the course of business" — the reliability threshold:** The books must be maintained systematically and routinely, not specially prepared for litigation. The entries must be contemporaneous with the transactions — made at or near the time of the event. The regularity of the entries is the circumstantial guarantee of their reliability.
- **"Shall not alone be sufficient evidence to charge any person with liability" — the corroboration rule:** The entries are evidence, not proof. The plaintiff must produce independent corroboration — a corresponding entry in the defendant's books, a signed receipt, a witness who saw the transaction, an acknowledgment, or any other evidence that supports the entries. What constitutes sufficient corroboration is fact‑specific; there is no fixed formula.
- **Entries in the plaintiff's own books — the typical scenario:** Section 28 primarily addresses the plaintiff's own account books. Entries in the defendant's books showing indebtedness to the plaintiff are stronger evidence and may, depending on the circumstances, be sufficient on their own — because they are admissions against the defendant's interest. Section 28 applies to all books of account, but the corroboration requirement is most strictly applied to the party's own books.
- **"Including those maintained in an electronic form" — modernisation:** Electronic accounting records (Tally, SAP, QuickBooks, spreadsheets) are expressly covered. The same rules apply — they must be regularly maintained, and they require corroboration.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Section 14 (Course of business — relevance):** Section 14 makes the existence of a course of business relevant to prove a particular act was done. Section 28 makes the *entries* in the books maintained in that course relevant — two different uses of business routine evidence.
- **BSA, Section 26(b) (Statements in course of business by dead/unavailable persons):** Section 26(b) admits entries made by persons now unavailable. Section 28 admits entries in books of account regardless of the maker's availability — the foundation is the regularity of the books, not the unavailability of the maker.
- **BSA, Section 21 (Admissions — proof):** An entry in the defendant's books acknowledging a debt is an admission against the defendant's interest and is provable as such under Section 19. It may also be relevant under Section 28. The admission route is stronger — admissions can be sufficient on their own.
- **BSA, Section 62 (Primary evidence):** The books of account, if produced in original or as certified electronic records, are primary evidence of their contents.

**Practical**
- A sues B for Rs. 50,000 on a money lent transaction. A produces A's ledger showing a debit entry of Rs. 50,000 against B's name. A also produces a signed receipt from B acknowledging receipt of Rs. 50,000. The receipt is the independent corroboration. The ledger entries plus the receipt together prove the debt. The ledger entries alone would not have sufficed.
- A trader sues a customer for non‑payment. The trader produces the trader's computer‑generated account statement showing the outstanding amount. The trader also produces the corresponding entry in the customer's account in the same software (a credit entry) and a witness who testifies to the delivery of goods. The combination of electronic entries, the customer's own account entry, and the witness is sufficient.
- A plaintiff produces only their own handwritten bahi khata (ledger) showing the defendant owes money. No receipts, no witnesses, no independent corroboration. The entries are relevant and admissible under Section 28. The court looks at them. But the defendant denies the debt. Without corroboration, the suit is dismissed — Section 28's "shall not alone be sufficient" applies.

**Cases**
- **Chandradhar Goswami v. Gauhati Bank Ltd. (1967)** – 85% (held that entries in books of account are relevant but not sufficient alone to charge a person with liability; independent corroboration is required; the corroboration need not be mathematical proof but must give the court confidence in the entries)
- **Ishwar Dass Jain v. Sohan Lal (2000)** – 80% (held that entries in account books regularly kept are admissible under Section 34 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 28 — but the plaintiff must lead independent evidence to prove the debt; corroboration is mandatory)
- **Central Bureau of Investigation v. V. C. Shukla (1998)** – 75% (held that Section 34 — now BSA Section 28 — applies to entries in books of account; the corroboration rule is a safeguard against self‑serving documentary evidence)

BSA 29 > Relevancy of entry in public record or an electronic record made in performance of duty.:
> Core Rule:
An entry in a public/official record (incl. electronic) stating a FII/RF ➔ RELEVANT.

> Condition:
The "Maker" must be:
- ◦ Public Servant ... (acting in discharge of official duty).
- ◦ Any Person ......... (acting under a specific duty mandated by law).

> Contrast Note (Sec 28 vs Sec 29):
- • Sec 28 (Private Ledger) ➔ Needs corroboration.
- • Sec 29 (Public Record) ➔ Relevant BY ITSELF (no corroboration trap).

**About**
Section 29 makes official records relevant as evidence of the facts they state. An entry in any public or official book, register, or record — including electronic records — is itself a relevant fact if it states a fact in issue or a relevant fact, and was made by a public servant in the discharge of official duty, or by any other person under a legal duty to maintain the record. The section is the foundation for admitting a vast range of public documents: birth and death registers, land records, court registers, police station diaries, government survey records, electoral rolls, and now electronic databases maintained by public authorities. The entry is not merely evidence of the fact that the entry was made; it is itself evidence of the fact stated — e.g., a birth register entry is evidence that the birth occurred on the stated date, not merely that someone recorded it. The rationale is the presumption of regularity of official acts — public servants are presumed to do their duty correctly, and entries made in the discharge of that duty are presumptively reliable.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 29, every entry in a public register would have to be proved by the official who made it — a practical impossibility when records are decades old and the recording officer is long deceased or untraceable. A person proving their date of birth would have to locate the hospital staff who recorded the birth. A person proving land ownership would have to find the long‑retired revenue officer who made the survey entry. Section 29 admits the record itself as evidence, without requiring the maker's personal attendance, subject to the record being proved as genuine.

**Nuances**
- **"Public or other official book, register or record" — broad scope:** The section covers all records maintained by public servants in discharge of duty — birth and death registers (under the Registration of Births and Deaths Act), land revenue records, mutation registers, court registers (plaint register, judgment register), police station daily diaries, electoral rolls, and any register maintained under a statutory duty. The record must be official — not a private notebook kept by a public servant.
- **"Or an electronic record" — modernisation:** The BSA explicitly includes electronic records — digitised land records, computerised birth registers, government databases, and online public records. The same presumption of regularity applies to electronic entries made in the discharge of official duty.
- **"By any other person in performance of a duty specially enjoined by law" — non‑public servants under legal duty:** The section also covers records maintained by private persons under a specific legal duty — e.g., a company registrar maintaining statutory registers under the Companies Act, a religious institution maintaining a register of marriages under a statutory obligation. The duty must be "specially enjoined by law" — not merely a customary practice.
- **The entry is evidence of the fact stated — not merely of the making of the entry:** Under Section 29, the entry "A was born on 1 January 2000" in a birth register is evidence that A was born on that date — not merely that the registrar recorded it. This is a significant evidentiary shortcut, and courts rely on it heavily for proof of age, marriage, death, and property rights.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Sections 76–81 (Public documents and proof of public records):** The procedural provisions for proving public documents — certified copies, presumptions as to genuineness. Section 29 makes the entry relevant; Sections 76–81 provide the mode of proof.
- **BSA, Section 35 (Presumption as to official acts):** The presumption that official acts are regularly performed — this supports the reliability of entries under Section 29.
- **Registration of Births and Deaths Act, 1969; Land Revenue Acts; Companies Act, 2013:** The specific statutes that impose the duty to maintain public records. Section 29 BSA makes entries in those records relevant as evidence.

**Practical**
- A dispute arises about a person's age. A certified copy of the birth register entry, showing the person was born on 15 March 1990, is produced. The entry is relevant under Section 29 — it is a public record made by a public servant (the Registrar of Births and Deaths) in discharge of official duty. The entry is evidence that the person was born on that date.
- A land ownership dispute turns on when a mutation entry was made in the revenue records. The certified copy of the mutation register, maintained by the village accountant under the State Land Revenue Act, is produced. The entry is relevant under Section 29 — it states the fact of mutation and the date, made by a person under a statutory duty.
- The police station daily diary (General Diary) records that a complaint was received at 10:30 PM on a given date. In a trial where the timing of the complaint is crucial, the GD entry is relevant under Section 29 — it is an official record made by a public servant (the Station House Officer) in discharge of duty.

**Cases**
- **Birad Mal Singhvi v. Anand Purohit (1988)** – 85% (held that entries in school registers and birth registers are relevant under Section 35 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 29; the entry must be made by a person with knowledge of the fact recorded and in discharge of official or legal duty)
- **State of Punjab v. Mohinder Singh (2005)** – 80% (held that revenue records — jamabandi entries — are admissible under Section 35 — now BSA Section 29; they are official records made by public servants in discharge of duty and carry a presumption of correctness)
- **Ramanandan Prasad v. State of Bihar (1991)** – 75% (held that the General Diary entry in a police station is an official record under Section 35 — now BSA Section 29; it is relevant evidence of the facts recorded, though it may be rebutted by other evidence)

BSA 30 > Relevancy of statements in maps, charts and plans.:
> Core Rule:
Maps/Charts/Plans showing a FII/RF ➔ RELEVANT.

> Condition:
- ◦ Commercially maps/charts/plans ... (published for public sale).
- ◦ Government maps/charts/plans ... (made under Central/State Govt authority).

> Limit:
- • Relevant ONLY for matters which are usually represented in such maps.
- • Not for private titles/ownership (e.g., a specific house).

**About**
Section 30 makes statements of fact contained in maps, charts, and plans relevant evidence. It covers two categories: (1) published maps or charts generally offered for public sale — e.g., commercially published road maps, survey maps, Google Maps prints, navigational charts; and (2) maps or plans made under the authority of the Central or State Government — e.g., Survey of India topographical maps, cadastral maps, municipal plans, land acquisition plans. The statements must relate to "matters usually represented or stated in such maps, charts or plans" — boundaries, distances, locations, topography, landmarks, and other geographical facts. The map or plan itself is the evidence; the surveyor or cartographer need not be called to testify. The rationale is that maps, particularly official ones, are prepared systematically, based on surveys and measurements, and carry a presumption of accuracy. Section 30 is the evidentiary foundation for using cartographic evidence in boundary disputes, land acquisition cases, and any litigation where location, distance, or geographical features are in issue.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 30, a party seeking to prove a boundary through a Survey of India map would have to call the surveyor who prepared it — often impossible for historical maps. A person relying on a published city map to prove the distance between two locations would need to summon the publisher's cartographer. Section 30 admits the map itself as evidence of the facts it states, subject to the map being proved as genuine and the facts being of the kind usually represented in maps.

**Nuances**
- **"Published maps or charts generally offered for public sale" — commercial and public maps:** This includes commercially available maps, atlases, Google Maps, and any cartographic material available to the public. The map must be "generally offered for public sale" — not a privately prepared sketch made for litigation. The publication and public availability provide a circumstantial guarantee of reliability.
- **"Maps or plans made under the authority of Government" — official cartographic evidence:** Survey of India topographical sheets, cadastral maps prepared under land revenue laws, village maps, municipal ward maps, land acquisition plans — these are the most commonly used category in Indian litigation. The map must be made under governmental authority — a statutory or administrative mandate for the survey. A mere internal office sketch not part of an official survey may not qualify.
- **"As to matters usually represented or stated in such maps, charts or plans" — the scope of relevant facts:** The map is evidence of the facts typically shown on maps — boundaries, distances, areas, locations of features (rivers, roads, buildings), topographical contours, and survey numbers. It is not evidence of facts that are not usually represented on maps — e.g., the ownership of a plot (which is a legal conclusion, not a cartographic fact) or the date a building was constructed. The entry must be the kind of information that maps are designed to convey.
- **The map must be proved as genuine:** Section 30 makes the statements in the map relevant; it does not relieve the party from proving that the map is what it purports to be. The map must be produced and authenticated — a certified copy for government maps, or proof of publication and purchase for commercial maps.

**Interconnections**
- **BSA, Section 29 (Public records — entries):** Section 29 covers entries in public registers; Section 30 covers maps, charts, and plans — a specific category of public and commercial records. Both are based on the presumption of regularity in the preparation of official and published documents.
- **BSA, Sections 76–81 (Public documents and proof):** The procedural provisions for proving public documents. A government map under Section 30 is proved under these sections — by a certified copy.
- **BSA, Section 57 (Facts judicially noticeable):** Certain geographical facts — the location of a well‑known river, the boundaries of a State — may be judicially noticed. Section 30 applies when the fact is not judicially noticed and must be proved through a map.
- **Indian Evidence Act, 1872, Section 36 (now BSA Section 30):** The predecessor provision, widely used in boundary and land disputes.

**Practical**
- A boundary dispute between two villages turns on the location of a stream. A certified copy of the Survey of India topographical map (prepared under the authority of the Central Government) shows the stream's course. The map is produced. Section 30 makes the depiction of the stream relevant evidence of the boundary — the surveyor need not be called.
- In a motor accident case, the distance between the accident site and the hospital is relevant to determine the delay in medical treatment. A printout from Google Maps showing the route and distance is produced. This falls within "published maps or charts generally offered for public sale" (Google Maps is publicly available). Section 30 makes the distance statement relevant.
- A land acquisition dispute involves the area of the acquired plot. The land acquisition plan, prepared under the authority of the State Government and certified by the Land Acquisition Officer, shows the plot's dimensions and boundaries. Section 30 makes these statements relevant — the plan is admissible evidence of the plot's area.

**Cases**
- **Ram Kishore Sen v. Union of India (1966)** – 85% (held that Survey of India maps are admissible under Section 36 of the old Evidence Act — now BSA Section 30 — as statements of fact in maps made under government authority; the maps are evidence of boundaries and geographical features)
- **State of Bihar v. Radha Krishna Singh (1983)** – 80% (held that maps and plans made under government authority carry a presumption of accuracy; the party relying on the map must prove it is genuine and relates to the area in dispute)
- **Harihar Prasad Singh v. Deonarain Prasad (1956)** – 75% (held that a cadastral survey map prepared under the Land Revenue Act is admissible under Section 36 — now BSA Section 30 — as evidence of boundaries and survey numbers; the map is relevant but not conclusive — it can be rebutted by other evidence)
