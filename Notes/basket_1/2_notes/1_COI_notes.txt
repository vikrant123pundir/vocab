COI 1 > Name and territory of the Union.:
**Core_Rule:** India = Bharat → A Union of States.

**Reference:** States + their territories → specified in First Schedule.

**Territory of India:**
- (a) State territories
- (b) UTs in First Schedule
- (c) Other acquired territories

**Key distinction:**
- “Union of States” ≠ “territory of India.”
- • The former describes the constitutional character of India.
- • The latter defines the territorial composition.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 1 is the constitutional pole that plants the Indian republic in space and name. Clause (1) declares “India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States”. Clause (2) makes the First Schedule the conclusive list of States and Union territories. Clause (3) then defines the territory of India as comprising the territories of the States, the Union territories specified in the First Schedule, and—crucially—“such other territories as may be acquired”. This third head is a forward‑looking power that allows the Union’s territory to grow without a constitutional amendment to Article 1 itself. During the Constituent Assembly debates, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar insisted on the phrase “Union of States” rather than “Federation of States” to make it clear that the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement among sovereign States and that no State has a right to secede.

|||Counterfactual|||
Had Article 1 not been enacted, there would be no constitutionally defined sovereign entity. The Constitution would not know its own name, its own territory, or the units it comprises. Without the expression “Union of States”, centrifugal forces could legitimately argue that India is a mere treaty‑based federation from which a State can withdraw. Without clause (3)(c), every addition of territory—Goa, Daman and Diu, Pondicherry—would have required a constitutional amendment not only for their representation in Parliament but also for the bare fact of their absorption into India, leaving their inhabitants in legal limbo. The entire edifice of constitutional jurisdiction would hang in the air.

|||NUANCES|||

“India, that is Bharat”: The phrase is not disjunctive. “That is” makes the two names synonymous and equally official, a deliberate compromise between modern and traditional identities.

“Union of States” is a term of art deliberately chosen over “Federation”. Ambedkar explained that the Union is indestructible, while the States are not; the Centre can alter their boundaries and even extinguish them under Articles 2–4, but they cannot secede.

Dynamic territorial clause: Clause (3)(c) makes India’s territory ambulatory. Territory may be acquired by conquest, treaty, cession, occupation, or any other mode recognised by international law, and on acquisition it becomes part of India ipso facto. This clause was the constitutional gateway through which Goa, Daman and Diu, Pondicherry, and Sikkim became integral parts of India.

“First Schedule” as a moving reference: The Schedule is not frozen; Parliament amends it whenever new States are formed or boundaries are altered under Articles 2, 3 and 4. Article 1 simply refers to the First Schedule as the authentic map at any given time.

|||Interconnections|||

Articles 2, 3 and 4: Article 1 defines what India is; Articles 2 and 3 empower Parliament to admit new States, alter boundaries, and even dissolve existing States, and Article 4 makes such amendments possible without triggering the Article 368 amendment procedure.

Article 368 and the Berubari doctrine: Territory may be acquired under Article 1(3)(c) without an amendment, but the cession of Indian territory to another country requires a constitutional amendment under Article 368, per the Supreme Court’s advisory opinion in the Berubari case.

First Schedule: Lists the States and Union territories from time to time, giving concrete shape to Article 1(2) and (3).

General Clauses Act, 1897, Section 3(28B): Defines “India” as the territory of India for the purposes of any Central Act, reinforcing Article 1’s definition.

Article 371A and the Sixth Schedule: Special provisions that qualify the uniform application of law within the territory, recognising internal constitutional asymmetry while still under the umbrella of Article 1.

|||Practical|||

Integration of Goa, Daman and Diu (1961): After the military action, these territories fell under Article 1(3)(c) as “acquired territories”. The Constitution (Twelfth Amendment) Act, 1962, and the subsequent Government of Union Territories Act made them Union territories. The acquisition itself did not need an Article 1 amendment.

Pondicherry and other French establishments: The Treaty of Cession with France (1962) transferred these territories to India. They became “acquired territories” under Article 1(3)(c) and were later constituted as the Union Territory of Pondicherry through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Sikkim’s merger (1975): Sikkim was first made an associate State by the Thirty‑fifth Amendment, and after a referendum, the Thirty‑sixth Amendment made it a full State, amending the First Schedule. Article 1(3)(c) provided the initial constitutional space for its admission.

Redrawing of State boundaries: Every time a new State is created (Telangana in 2014) or boundaries change, the First Schedule is updated; Article 1(2) automatically picks up the new configuration without needing a separate amendment to Article 1 itself.

|||Cases|||

In re: Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves (1960) – 90% (directly on Article 1(3)(c) and whether territory can be ceded without amendment; held that cession requires Article 368 amendment)

N. Masthan Sahib v. Chief Commissioner, Pondicherry (1962) – 80% (interpreted “acquired territory” under Article 1(3)(c); held that Pondicherry, after the Treaty of Cession, became an acquired territory and its residents could not automatically claim Indian citizenship without parliamentary law)

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) – 75% (not directly on Article 1 but held that the “Union of States” indicates an indestructible Union; States do not have sovereignty independent of the Constitution and cannot secede)

State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963) – 70% (examined the federal structure and the nature of the Union; observed that India is not a federation of independent States joined together for limited purposes, reinforcing Article 1’s description)

COI 2 > Admission or establishment of new States.:
**Authority:** Parliament
**Power:**
By law, Parliament MAY:
- (a) admit new States into the Union; OR
- (b) establish new States.
**Limits:** Terms + conditions → as Parliament thinks fit.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 2 gives Parliament a plenary, one‑sentence power: it may “by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit.”

“Admit” refers to bringing an already existing political entity (like a princely state or a neighbouring territory) into the Indian Union as a State.

“Establish” means creating an entirely new State out of territory that was not previously a State — for example, from freshly acquired territory or from a Union Territory that Parliament chooses to constitute as a State directly.

“On such terms and conditions as it thinks fit” makes the power virtually untrammelled. Parliament can impose special political, administrative or financial conditions that may even derogate from other constitutional provisions, so long as they do not destroy the basic structure.

Crucially, by virtue of Article 4, a law made under Article 2 is not treated as a constitutional amendment under Article 368; it can amend the First and Fourth Schedules and contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions as an ordinary legislation.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 2, the Union would have no constitutional tool to add a new State. Every admission  and establishment would require a formal constitutional amendment under Article 368, which is politically heavy and legally uncertain (could it even be done if the new State is not mentioned in the original text?). The territorial map would be frozen, barring an amendment, and the Union could not respond flexibly to geopolitical changes, referendums, or the voluntary accession of territories. Acquired territories under Article 1(3)(c) could be administered, but could never become full States without an Article 368 amendment.

|||NUANCES|||

Plenary but not unlimited: “On such terms and conditions as it thinks fit” is one of the widest grants of legislative power in the Constitution. However, the Supreme Court in R.C. Poudyal (1993) held that the terms must not violate the Constitution’s basic structure; e.g., Parliament could not admit a new State on a condition that abolishes democracy there.

Ordinary law vs constitutional amendment: Article 4 explicitly says a law under Article 2 is not an amendment under Article 368. Yet when Sikkim was admitted, Parliament chose to pass the 36th Constitutional Amendment Act because the terms required altering numerous articles (Parliamentary representation, special provisions, etc.) that went beyond amending the Schedules. This shows the practical interplay: Article 2 is the power source; the procedure can be either an ordinary law (if the changes fit within Article 4) or a constitutional amendment (if deeper changes are needed).

Distinction from Article 3: Article 2 deals with bringing an outside entity into the Union as a State, or establishing a State from non‑State territory (e.g., an acquired territory). Article 3 deals with internal reorganisation — altering boundaries, splitting or merging existing States, or changing a Union Territory into a State. The line can blur, but the original understanding is Article 2 is the “entry gate” for new States.

|||Interconnections|||

Article 1(3)(c): Allows the Union to acquire new territory. Once acquired, Article 2 can convert that territory into a State.

Article 3: Internal reorganisation of existing States and Union Territories; works alongside Article 2 to continuously reshape the Union.

Article 4: Clarifies the legal status of laws under Articles 2 and 3 — they are not amendments under Article 368, can amend the First and Fourth Schedules, and can include supplemental provisions.

First Schedule: The concrete list of States and Union Territories that Article 2 directly alters when a new State is admitted or established.

Article 368: The formal amendment procedure, used when the terms and conditions require changing provisions outside the Schedules, as in Sikkim’s case.

|||Practical|||

Sikkim’s admission (1975): Sikkim, a former protectorate, held a referendum and the Sikkim Assembly sought admission as a State. Parliament first made it an “associate State” through the 35th Amendment (inserting Article 2A, now repealed) and then admitted it as a full State through the 36th Amendment, invoking Article 2. The terms included special provisions for the Sikkim Legislative Assembly’s composition, reservation of seats, and continuation of certain laws. This remains the sole clear‑cut use of Article 2.

Acquired territories (Goa, Daman & Diu, Pondicherry): These were first administered as Union Territories after acquisition. If Parliament wanted to admit them directly as States without the UT phase, it could have done so under Article 2. The fact that it used Article 3 later to convert them into States does not diminish Article 2’s potential.

|||Cases|||

In re: Berubari Union (1960) – 95% (distinguished cession of territory, which requires an Article 368 amendment, from admission of new States under Article 2, which is an ordinary legislative power; clarified that Article 2 can be used without an amendment)

N. Masthan Sahib v. Chief Commissioner, Pondicherry (1962) – 90% (held that an acquired territory becomes part of India under Article 1(3)(c); Parliament can then “admit” it as a State under Article 2 or administer it as a Union Territory under Article 3; citizenship does not automatically follow admission)

R.C. Poudyal v. Union of India (1993) – 85% (directly upheld Sikkim’s admission under Article 2; the terms and conditions, including seat reservations for certain communities, were held valid as part of Parliament’s plenary power, limited only by the basic structure)

COI 2A > Sikkim to be associated with the Union.:
|||ABOUT|||
Article 2A was a transitional constitutional provision inserted by the 35th Amendment Act, 1974. It created a special status for Sikkim — not as a full State, but as an "associate State" of the Union of India. The article was enacted following a resolution of the Sikkim Assembly expressing the desire for closer association with India. It was an intermediate step between Sikkim's former status as a protectorate and its eventual full integration. Article 2A conferred a unique "associate" status on Sikkim: it was associated with the Union, represented in Parliament through one Lok Sabha member and one Rajya Sabha member, but retained a degree of internal autonomy distinct from full Statehood. The article was short‑lived. Following a referendum in Sikkim in 1975 that overwhelmingly supported full integration, the 36th Amendment Act abolished Article 2A and admitted Sikkim as a full State of the Union under Article 2. The article now stands omitted, a historical footnote to the integration of the last princely state into the Indian Union. It was the only instance in the Constitution's history of an "associate State" relationship — a sui generis constitutional arrangement that bridged the gap between protectorate and full Statehood.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 2A, the transition of Sikkim from a protectorate to a full State would have been a sudden constitutional leap. The associate State status provided a breathing period — it gave Sikkim's people and the Indian Union time to adjust to the new relationship before full integration. It also provided a constitutional framework for Sikkim's representation in Parliament before full Statehood, ensuring that Sikkim's voice was heard in the national legislature during the transition. The article was the constitutional expression of the democratic process — the Sikkim Assembly's resolution and the subsequent referendum — that led to integration.

|||Nuances|||
- **"Associate State" — a unique constitutional category:** This was the only use of this term in the Constitution. It was not the same as a State under Article 1 read with the First Schedule, nor was it a Union Territory. It was a sui generis status — associated with the Union, with limited representation in Parliament, but not fully integrated into the federal structure. The details of the association were spelled out in the 35th Amendment Act and the Tenth Schedule (also now omitted).
- **Omitted, not repealed:** Article 2A was omitted by the 36th Amendment — the same amendment that admitted Sikkim as a full State. The omission was not a repeal; it was a replacement. The associate State status was not rejected but superseded — it had served its purpose and was no longer needed. The omission took effect from 26 April 1975.
- **A product of democratic choice:** The 35th Amendment was enacted in response to a resolution of the Sikkim Assembly dated 10 April 1974, seeking representation in Parliament and closer association. The 36th Amendment followed a referendum in Sikkim in 1975 where the people voted for full integration. The two amendments together reflect the constitutional principle that the admission and status of territories is ultimately a matter of democratic will, with Parliament acting as the constitutional instrument of that will.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 2 (Admission or establishment of new States):** The constitutional power under which Sikkim was eventually admitted as a full State. Article 2A was the intermediate step; Article 2 was the final destination.
- **Article 3 (Formation of new States):** The power to alter the status of existing States — used to convert Sikkim from an associate State to a full State? No, Article 3 deals with internal reorganisation. Sikkim's admission was under Article 2; the 36th Amendment was an exercise of Article 2, not Article 3.
- **First Schedule:** The list of States. Sikkim was not in the First Schedule during the Article 2A period (it was not a State). After the 36th Amendment, Sikkim was added to the First Schedule as a full State.
- **Tenth Schedule (now omitted):** Contained the detailed terms and conditions of Sikkim's association with the Union during the Article 2A period — representation in Parliament, special provisions for Sikkim's legislature, and the distribution of legislative powers. Omitted along with Article 2A.

|||Practical|||
- Between 1974 and 1975, Sikkim was neither a State nor a Union Territory nor an independent country — it was an "associate State" of India. Its citizens were not Indian citizens under the Citizenship Act (they became citizens upon full Statehood), but they had representation in the Indian Parliament. This unique arrangement ended on 26 April 1975, when Sikkim became the 22nd State of India.
- A person born in Sikkim in 1974, during the Article 2A period, would have been a citizen of Sikkim (the associate State), not an Indian citizen. Upon Sikkim's full integration in 1975, the Citizenship Act was extended to Sikkim, and such persons became Indian citizens by birth or descent under the Act's provisions.

|||Cases|||
- **R. C. Poudyal v. Union of India (1993)** – 85% (upheld the terms of Sikkim's admission under Article 2 and the 36th Amendment, including the reservation of seats for certain communities; the case discusses the historical context of Sikkim's integration, including the Article 2A phase, and affirms Parliament's plenary power over the admission of new States and the terms and conditions of such admission)

COI 3 > Formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of existing States.:
**Authority:** Parliament
**Power:**
By law, Parliament MAY:
(a) form a new State
- • by separating territory from any State; OR
- • by uniting 2+ States / parts of States; OR
- • by uniting any territory to a part of any State.
(b) increase area of any State.
(c) diminish area of any State.
(d) alter boundaries of any State.
(e) alter name of any State.

**Procedure:**
→ Bill
→ President's recommendation
→ Area/boundary/name affected?
- → YES:
	- → President refers Bill to State Legislature for its views
	- → specified/further period expires
	- → Parliament proceeds with the Bill
- → NO: Parliament proceeds with the Bill
→ law

**Procedural Requirements:**
• President: recommendation ALWAYS req.
• SL: reference ONLY if A/B/N affected.
• SL: views only; consent NOT required.

**Explanation I:**
• “State” in clauses (a)–(e): includes UT.
• “State” in proviso: does NOT include UT.

**Explanation II:**
• Art. 3(a): includes forming a new State/UT by uniting
• part of any State/UT to any other State/UT.

**Sandbox:**
(1) State A/B/N affected → reference required.
(2) SL disagrees → Bill may still proceed.
(3) UT affected → State-Legislature reference proviso does NOT apply.
(4) Part of State/UT + another State/UT → new State/UT may be formed.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 3 empowers Parliament to reconfigure the internal territorial map of India by ordinary legislation. It grants five specific powers: form a new State by separating territory from an existing State or by uniting States/parts of States, increase or diminish any State's area, alter boundaries, and alter names. The proviso creates a procedural safeguard: no Bill can be introduced without the President's prior recommendation, and if the Bill affects a State's area, boundaries or name, the President must refer it to that State's Legislature for its views within a specified or extended period. Crucially, Parliament is not bound by those views — the reference is consultative, not decisive.
- Explanation I clarifies that "State" in clauses (a) to (e) includes a Union territory, but in the proviso "State" excludes Union territories, meaning Parliament can reorganise Union territories without consulting any legislature.
- Explanation II confirms that the power to form a new State includes uniting a part of one State/Union territory with another.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 3, every internal boundary change — however minor — would require a constitutional amendment under Article 368 with its special majorities and ratification by half the States. The linguistic reorganisation of the 1950s and 1960s, which created Andhra Pradesh, Gujarat, Maharashtra, and numerous other States, would have been politically impossible or endlessly delayed. The Union would be frozen in its 1950 territorial configuration, unable to respond to democratic demands for statehood based on language, ethnicity, or administrative convenience. The proviso's consultation requirement, if absent, would allow Parliament to extinguish a State without even hearing its legislature — a democratic deficit the framers deliberately avoided.

|||NUANCES|||

Parliamentary sovereignty over State boundaries: Unlike federations where State boundaries are constitutionally entrenched, India's Parliament can alter State boundaries and even extinguish a State by a simple majority law. This is the most dramatic expression of the "indestructible Union, destructible States" principle — no State has a guaranteed territorial integrity.

Consultative, not binding: The proviso makes the State Legislature's views a procedural prerequisite, not a substantive veto. Parliament can, and has, legislated in the teeth of State Legislature opposition. The Supreme Court in Babulal Parate (1961) held that Parliament is not bound by the views, and the reference period need not await the legislature's actual resolution — the expiry of the specified time is enough.

- Explanation I's dual meaning of "State": For the substantive power (clauses a–e), "State" includes Union territories — Parliament can merge, divide or rename a Union territory just as it can a State. But for the procedural safeguard (proviso), Union territories are excluded; no local legislature need be consulted, reflecting their constitutionally different status.

- Explanation II — a clarifying, not enlarging, power: It confirms that uniting a part of one State/UT with another State/UT is "formation of a new State," resolving any doubt that clause (a) covers partial unions as well as complete mergers. This was used extensively in creating linguistic States.

Law under Article 3 is not a constitutional amendment (Article 4): A law made under Article 3 can amend the First and Fourth Schedules and include supplemental provisions without following the Article 368 procedure. This makes territorial reorganisation administratively swift.

|||Interconnections|||

Article 2: Deals with admission or establishment of new States from outside the Union. Article 3 covers internal reorganisation. Together they are the twin engines of India's territorial flexibility.

Article 4: Provides that laws under Articles 2 and 3 are not constitutional amendments and can amend the First and Fourth Schedules — the procedural key that makes Article 3 practically workable.

First Schedule: The list of States and Union Territories; every Article 3 law amends this Schedule.

Article 368: The formal amendment procedure deliberately bypassed by Article 3 read with Article 4, except where the reorganisation requires changes to provisions beyond the Schedules.

State Reorganisation Act, 1956: The foundational statute exercising Article 3 powers wholesale, creating linguistic States and redrawing India's internal map.

Article 5: Citizenship provisions are affected by State reorganisation, as domicile and territory change; Parliament typically provides for citizenship adjustments in reorganisation laws.

|||Practical|||

The creation of Telangana (2014) involved the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, passed despite the Andhra Pradesh Legislature's rejection of the reference. The President's reference was made, the period expired, and Parliament legislated — a textbook Article 3 operation.

The alteration of a State's name — e.g., Orissa to Odisha, Bombay to Mumbai (though the State name was already Maharashtra, the city's official name change had cascading legal effects) — requires only a Bill with Presidential recommendation and reference to the State Legislature. No boundary change, no land transfer, just a legislative name change.

The merger of Dadra and Nagar Haveli with Daman and Diu (2019) as a single Union territory was done under Article 3's Union territory-inclusive power; no local legislature was consulted.

|||Cases|||

Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay (1959) – 95% (Constitution Bench; held that the proviso's reference to the State Legislature is not mandatory in the sense that Parliament must wait for the legislature's actual resolution; Parliament can proceed once the specified or extended period expires; Parliament is not bound by the views expressed)

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) – 80% (while primarily on Article 356, it extensively discussed the "Union of States" and federal balance; held that the power under Article 3 does not permit destroying the federal structure or basic features; a State cannot be wiped out altogether — though a full extinction would likely trigger basic structure review, and Bommai did not directly rule on Article 3)

Mullaperiyar Environmental Protection Forum v. Union of India (2006) – 65% (recognised Parliament's plenary power under Article 3 to alter boundaries, including for purposes like implementing inter‑State river water agreements; the power is not limited to linguistic or cultural reorganisation)

COI 4 > Laws made under articles 2 and 3 to provide for the amendment of the First and the Fourth Schedules and supplemental, incidental and consequential matters.:
**Scope:** Laws under Arts. 2/3

**Effect:** Such laws:
(1) SHALL contain necessary amendments to 1st & 4th Sch.
(2) MAY contain S/I/C provisions by Parliament.
> incl. representation in Parliament / affected SLs.

**Constitutional status:**
Art. 2/3 law ≠ Art. 368 amendment.

> S/I/C: supplemental, incidental & consequential

|||ABOUT|||
Article 4 is the procedural enabler for Articles 2 and 3. It does two things. Clause (1) says that any law admitting a new State (Article 2) or reorganising existing States (Article 3) shall contain amendments to the First Schedule (list of States and Union Territories) and the Fourth Schedule (allocation of Rajya Sabha seats) as necessary, and may contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions — including those relating to parliamentary and legislative representation. Clause (2) delivers the crucial procedural shortcut: such a law “shall not be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of article 368.” This means Parliament can redraw India’s internal map by ordinary legislative procedure — simple majority, no special majority, no State ratification — without triggering the formal amendment process. Article 4 thus insulates territorial reorganisation from the political and procedural hurdles of constitutional amendment.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 4, every law under Article 2 or 3 would be a constitutional amendment requiring the Article 368 procedure: two‑thirds majority of members present and voting in each House, plus ratification by at least half the States. The linguistic reorganisation of the 1950s and 1960s — creating Andhra Pradesh, Maharashtra, Gujarat, and over a dozen other States — would have required dozens of constitutional amendments, each facing potential defeat in State legislatures whose territory was being diminished. The creation of Telangana (2014) would have required ratification by half the States, many with no stake in the matter. The entire project of flexible, responsive federalism would have been politically unworkable. The Fourth Schedule (Rajya Sabha seats) would remain frozen, preventing any rebalancing of the Council of States after territorial changes.

|||NUANCES|||

“Shall” vs. “may”: Amendment of the First and Fourth Schedules is mandatory (“shall contain such provisions”). Supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions — including adjustments to parliamentary and legislative representation — are discretionary (“may also contain”). This ensures that the constitutional map (First Schedule) and the federal chamber’s composition (Fourth Schedule) are always updated, while other changes are left to Parliament’s judgment.

“Including provisions as to representation” — a broad power: The parenthetical inclusion confirms that Parliament can adjust Lok Sabha and Legislative Assembly seats in affected States through the same ordinary law. This was crucial when new States were formed — Andhra Pradesh’s creation required reallocating Lok Sabha seats between the new State and the rump Madras State, done entirely through the reorganisation Act, not an amendment.

The Article 368 firewall — what it does and does not protect: Clause (2) only insulates the reorganisation law from being deemed an Article 368 amendment. If the reorganisation also requires altering provisions outside the First and Fourth Schedules (e.g., creating a new Article 371‑type special provision, or altering the Fifth/Sixth Schedule), a separate Article 368 amendment is needed. Sikkim’s admission required the 36th Constitutional Amendment Act precisely because the terms needed changes beyond the Schedules.

Ordinary law, not ordinary procedure in Parliament: While the law is passed by simple majority, the proviso in Article 3 (Presidential recommendation and State Legislature reference) remains a procedural prerequisite. Article 4 does not dispense with those safeguards; it only clarifies the legal character of the resulting Act.

|||Interconnections|||

Article 2 (Admission/establishment of new States): The source of power to bring new territory into the Union; Article 4 provides the legislative mechanism for consequential changes.

Article 3 (Reorganisation of existing States): The source of internal reorganisation power; Article 4 ensures the Schedules and representation can be adjusted alongside.

Article 368 (Amendment of the Constitution): The special procedure deliberately bypassed by Article 4(2), creating a clear distinction between constitutional amendments and territorial reorganisation laws.

First Schedule: Lists the States and Union Territories; every Article 2/3 law mandatorily amends it through Article 4.

Fourth Schedule: Allocates Rajya Sabha seats; similarly mandatorily amended. This ensures federal balance in the upper house is recalibrated after territorial changes.

State Reorganisation Act, 1956: The first and most comprehensive exercise of Article 4, amending the Schedules and redistributing Lok Sabha seats across dozens of reorganised States by ordinary law.

|||Practical|||

When Telangana was created in 2014, the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014, amended the First Schedule (adding Telangana as State No. 29), amended the Fourth Schedule (adjusting Rajya Sabha seats for Andhra Pradesh and Telangana), and adjusted Lok Sabha and Legislative Assembly seats — all by a simple majority law. No constitutional amendment was needed.

When the names of States were changed — Orissa to Odisha, Uttaranchal to Uttarakhand — Parliament passed ordinary legislation amending the First Schedule; Article 4 ensured this was not an Article 368 amendment.

The merger of Dadra and Nagar Haveli with Daman and Diu (2019) into a single Union Territory was done by ordinary legislation, amending the First Schedule without invoking Article 368, courtesy of Article 4.

|||Cases|||

Mangal Singh v. Union of India (1967) – 95% (Constitution Bench; upheld the reorganisation of Punjab and the creation of Haryana under Article 3 read with Article 4; held that a law under Article 3 is not an amendment under Article 368, and the power to adjust representation is validly exercised under Article 4)

In re: Berubari Union (1960) – 90% (Advisory Opinion; clarified the distinction between admission/reorganisation under Articles 2‑4 and cession of territory; held that while Article 4 allows ordinary legislation for Schedules and representation, the cession of Indian territory to another country requires an Article 368 amendment — a crucial limit on the Article 4 shortcut)

Babulal Parate v. State of Bombay (1961) – 85% (upheld the State Reorganisation Act, 1956; held that the proviso in Article 3 requiring State Legislature reference is a procedural safeguard that Article 4 does not override; Article 4 only deals with the legal character of the resulting Act, not the pre‑enactment procedure)

N. Masthan Sahib v. Chief Commissioner, Pondicherry (1962) – 75% (recognised that an acquired territory can be admitted as a State under Article 2 read with Article 4; the consequential provisions on representation and Schedules are validly made by ordinary law)

COI 5 > Citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution.:
**Core Rule:**
At COI commencement, Indian citizens are:

**Conditions:**
(1) Person with Domicile in India; AND
(2) ANY ONE:
- (a) Born in India; OR
- (b) Either parent born in India; OR
- (c) Ordinarily resident in India ≥ 5 years immediately preceding commencement.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 5 is the original citizenship clause — the constitutional gatekeeper that determined who automatically became an Indian citizen on 26 January 1950. It uses a two‑part test. First, the person must have domicile in the territory of India — a permanent home coupled with an intention to reside indefinitely. Second, the person must satisfy one of three alternative conditions: (a) born in India, or (b) either parent born in India, or (c) ordinarily resident in India for at least five years immediately before the commencement. These three prongs reflect a graduated connection to India: birth, descent, and long residence. The article is a snapshot provision — it froze citizenship on 26 January 1950 and does not govern anyone born after that date. The Constituent Assembly consciously rejected religion as a criterion and expressly denied requests for dual citizenship, opting for a single, unitary citizenship model. Article 5, read with Articles 6–11, forms the Constitution's original citizenship code, though Parliament was empowered under Article 11 to legislate prospectively — which it did through the Citizenship Act, 1955.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 5, there would be no constitutional baseline for citizenship at the Republic's birth. No person would have an automatic, constitutionally guaranteed claim to Indian citizenship on 26 January 1950. The State would lack a constitutional foundation for issuing passports, conferring fundamental rights tied to citizenship (Articles 15, 16, 19), or determining eligibility for offices reserved for citizens (President, Vice‑President, judges, etc.). Mass statelessness could have resulted, or citizenship would have been left entirely to ordinary legislation, subject to the vicissitudes of parliamentary majorities rather than anchored in the constitutional text. The absence of a domicile requirement would have extended citizenship to transient visitors with a tenuous connection, while the omission of the five‑year residence prong would have excluded long‑settled residents who lacked birth or descent connections — both outcomes the framers deliberately avoided.

|||NUANCES|||

Domicile as the non‑negotiable foundation: Every pathway under Article 5 begins with domicile. It is not merely residence; it requires both physical presence and an intention to make India one's permanent home. A person born in India to foreign parents temporarily visiting would not acquire domicile (and thus not citizenship) unless they formed the requisite intention. Domicile is a civil‑law concept imported into constitutional citizenship law, and its content is drawn from general principles rather than defined in the Constitution.

The three prongs are alternatives: Once domicile is established, any one of birth in India, parent's birth in India, or five years' ordinary residence suffices. They are not cumulative. This meant that a person domiciled in India whose parents were born in what became Pakistan could still claim citizenship if they themselves were born in India or had five years' residence.

"Ordinarily resident" is not domicile: The five‑year residence prong (c) uses "ordinarily resident," a lesser standard than domicile. This allowed a person who had not yet formed the intention to permanently reside in India to satisfy the residence limb even while domicile was separately required. The two concepts work at different levels: domicile is the threshold; ordinary residence is one of three alternative additional connectors.

A snapshot, not a continuing source: Article 5 does not confer citizenship on anyone born after 26 January 1950. Citizenship by birth thereafter is governed by the Citizenship Act, 1955. This temporal limitation is often misunderstood — Article 5 is a commencement‑day provision, not an ongoing birthright citizenship clause. India does not have unconditional jus soli; the Citizenship Act progressively restricted birthright citizenship, requiring at least one Indian parent.

Rejection of dual citizenship and religion: The Assembly debated and expressly rejected proposals for dual citizenship (with the Commonwealth or otherwise) and for citizenship based on religion. The secular, unitary character of Indian citizenship was a conscious constitutional choice embedded in Article 5's text.

|||Interconnections|||

Articles 6–8: Deal with special categories — migrants from Pakistan (Articles 6–7) and Indian diaspora returning from abroad (Article 8). These articles operate as supplements and qualifications to Article 5, not alternatives.

Article 9: Automatically terminates Indian citizenship for any person who voluntarily acquires foreign citizenship — reinforcing the single‑citizenship principle.

Article 10: Guarantees that every person who is a citizen under Articles 5–8 shall continue to be a citizen subject to any law made by Parliament, providing continuity of rights.

Article 11: Empowers Parliament to make any provision for acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship. This is the constitutional bridge to the Citizenship Act, 1955.

Citizenship Act, 1955, Sections 3–7: The post‑constitutional statutory framework for citizenship by birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, and incorporation of territory.

Part III (Fundamental Rights): Several rights are reserved to citizens — Article 15 (non‑discrimination), Article 16 (public employment), Article 19 (freedoms). Article 5 determines who enjoyed these rights from day one.

|||Practical|||

On 26 January 1950, every person who had Indian domicile and satisfied one of the three prongs automatically became an Indian citizen. No application, registration, or declaration was needed — citizenship was automatic by constitutional mandate.

A person born in Lahore (now Pakistan) in 1920 who moved to Delhi in 1945 and had been ordinarily resident for five years by January 1950 became a citizen under Article 5(c), even though neither they nor their parents were born in what became Indian territory — domicile plus five years' residence sufficed.

A child born in Bombay in 1948 to a British father and an Indian mother would be a citizen under Article 5(a) if the father had Indian domicile, or under Article 5(b) if the mother had been born in India (satisfying the parent prong). If neither parent had Indian domicile, the child would not be a citizen under Article 5 despite birth in India.

The Citizenship Act, 1955, now governs all citizenship questions for persons born after the Constitution's commencement. Article 5 is today invoked primarily in historical citizenship claims, partition‑era litigation, and cases determining whether a person was a citizen at the Constitution's birth for purposes of rights that flow from that status.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 6 is the first of three articles (along with Articles 7 and 8) addressing the citizenship fallout of Partition. It creates a special pathway to citizenship for persons who migrated from the territory that became Pakistan to the territory of India. The article operates “notwithstanding anything in article 5” — it overrides Article 5’s domicile requirement for this specific class of migrants.

The eligibility test has two cumulative requirements. First, the person (or a parent or grandparent) must have been born in “India as defined in the Government of India Act, 1935” — pre‑Partition, undivided India. This is a descent‑based connection to the territory. Second, the migration date determines the further condition. For those who migrated before 19 July 1948, the requirement is simple: ordinary residence in India since the date of migration — a continuous residence test. For those who migrated on or after 19 July 1948, a more formal requirement applies: registration as a citizen by an appointed officer on an application made before the Constitution’s commencement, with a proviso requiring at least six months’ residence immediately before the application.

The date 19 July 1948 is not arbitrary; it marks the coming into force of the Influx from Pakistan (Control) Act, 1948, which introduced a permit system for migration. Pre‑permit migrants were treated as having moved freely; post‑permit migrants were subject to a more formal verification process. Article 6 thus distinguishes between spontaneous migration during the chaos of Partition and regulated migration after the permit system was in place.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 6, millions of Partition migrants who lacked Indian domicile (having migrated from what was their permanent home) would have no constitutional pathway to Indian citizenship on 26 January 1950. Article 5’s domicile requirement would exclude them — they had been domiciled in what became Pakistan, not in India. They would be stateless or dependent on ordinary legislation for citizenship. The Constituent Assembly, deeply conscious that Partition had displaced millions who had deep ancestral roots in undivided India, created Article 6 specifically to ensure that Hindu, Sikh, and other migrants from Pakistan who had ties to undivided India could claim citizenship without the domicile barrier. The omission of the grandparent descent test would have narrowed eligibility dramatically, excluding those whose only connection to India was through grandparents born in Lahore, Peshawar, or other areas that became Pakistan. The pre‑/post‑19 July 1948 distinction was a practical administrative necessity; without it, the post‑permit migrants would either be given automatic citizenship (undermining the permit system) or denied any pathway (abandoning legitimate migrants).

|||NUANCES|||

“Notwithstanding anything in article 5” — an override, not a repeal: Article 5 remains the general rule. Article 6 is a special provision for a specific class. A person who qualifies under both Article 5 and Article 6 can claim under either. A person who fails Article 6 but has Indian domicile and five years’ residence may still claim under Article 5(c).

The three‑generation descent test — deep ancestral connection: The eligibility prong includes a grandparent born in undivided India. This is a notably broad connection, reflecting the framers’ desire to include those whose family roots in India spanned generations, even if the migrant and their parents were born in areas that became Pakistan. The reference to “India as defined in the Government of India Act, 1935” means pre‑Partition British India, including the territories that became Pakistan — a deliberate choice to recognise ancestral ties to the undivided subcontinent.

The significance of 19 July 1948: This date is the cut‑off between the two migration categories. Before it, free movement was the norm; after it, the permit system was in place. The pre‑July 1948 migrant needed only residence; the post‑July 1948 migrant needed registration. This distinction has generated litigation: proving the exact date of migration, proving continuous residence, and proving registration all present evidentiary challenges in contemporary citizenship disputes.

“Ordinarily resident since the date of his migration” — a lesser standard than domicile: The pre‑July 1948 migrant does not need domicile, only ordinary residence — a factual, less rigorous standard. But the residence must be continuous from the date of migration until the Constitution’s commencement (or until the relevant date). Gaps in residence, or return to Pakistan, could break the chain.

Registration under clause (b)(ii) — an administrative, not judicial, process: The post‑July 1948 migrant had to apply to an appointed officer before 26 January 1950. The proviso adds a six‑month residence requirement before the application. This was a time‑bound window that closed at the Constitution’s commencement. A person who migrated after July 1948 but did not apply for registration before 26 January 1950 cannot claim under Article 6(b)(ii).

|||Interconnections|||

Article 5 (Citizenship at commencement): The general rule Article 6 overrides. The two operate in parallel; a migrant who satisfies both can rely on either.

Article 7 (Migrants to Pakistan): The mirror provision dealing with persons who migrated from India to Pakistan. Article 7 provides that such persons lose Indian citizenship, subject to a return‑migration exception. Articles 6 and 7 together constitute the Partition citizenship framework.

Article 8 (Overseas Indians returning): Deals with persons of Indian origin resident abroad who return to India. Completes the trilogy of special citizenship provisions.

Article 9 (Loss of citizenship on acquiring foreign citizenship): Applies to all citizens, including those who acquire citizenship under Article 6. If a person who became a citizen under Article 6 later voluntarily acquires Pakistani citizenship, Article 9 terminates their Indian citizenship.

Citizenship Act, 1955, Sections 3–7: The statutory framework for citizenship after the Constitution’s commencement. Article 6 is a commencement‑day provision; the Citizenship Act governs post‑1950 citizenship.

Foreigners Act, 1946, and Citizenship Rules, 1956: The administrative machinery for determining citizenship status, often invoked in contemporary litigation where persons are alleged to be illegal migrants rather than Article 6 citizens.

|||Practical|||

In contemporary NRC (National Register of Citizens) and Foreigners Tribunal proceedings in Assam, Article 6 is frequently invoked. A person claiming citizenship through a Partition‑era migrant ancestor must prove that the ancestor qualified under Article 6 — showing the ancestral birth in undivided India, the date of migration (before or after 19 July 1948), and the residence or registration. Decades‑old documentary evidence (land records, electoral rolls, refugee registration cards) becomes critical.

A Hindu family migrated from Lahore to Amritsar in September 1947. The father was born in Lahore, but the grandfather was born in Amritsar (undivided India). Under Article 6, the grandfather’s birth in Amritsar satisfies the descent test. Since the migration was before 19 July 1948, ordinary residence in India since migration confers citizenship. The father is a citizen under Article 6(a) and (b)(i).

A Muslim trader migrated from Karachi to Bombay in November 1948, after the permit system was in force. He applied for and obtained registration as a citizen before 26 January 1950. He is a citizen under Article 6(b)(ii). If he did not apply for registration, he cannot claim under Article 6 and must rely on Article 5 (if he had domicile and five years’ residence) or the Citizenship Act.

**Core Rule:**

**Exception/Effect:**

(1) India → Pak after 1 Mar 1947 → NOT citizen.
(2) Return w− permit → exception unavailable.

(4) Return date → irrelevant; deemed ≥19 July 1948.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 7 is the disqualification clause for Partition migrants. It provides that any person who, after 1 March 1947, migrated from India to the territory now included in Pakistan "shall not be deemed to be a citizen of India." This operates "notwithstanding anything in articles 5 and 6" — overriding both the general citizenship rule and the Pakistan‑to‑India migrant pathway. The cut-off date, 1 March 1947, is significant: it predates the formal Partition (August 1947) and captures the early communal migration that began in the months before independence. The article thus disqualifies persons who left India for Pakistan during the Partition period, irrespective of their previous domicile, birth, or residence in India.

The proviso creates a critical exception: a person who returned to India under a permit for resettlement or permanent return is not disqualified. Such a returnee is deemed, for the purposes of Article 6(b), to have migrated to India after 19 July 1948 — bringing them under the registration pathway of Article 6(b)(ii). This was the constitutional mechanism for "return migrants" — those who initially went to Pakistan but later returned to India under a legal permit, typically issued under the Influx from Pakistan (Control) Act, 1948. The proviso thus converts a disqualifying migration (India to Pakistan) into a qualifying migration (Pakistan to India) for those who returned legally.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 7, a person who migrated to Pakistan after March 1947 could claim Indian citizenship under Article 5 (by showing domicile and birth/residence) or under Article 6 (by showing ancestral ties to undivided India and migration back). The Constitution would have no mechanism to disqualify those who had made a deliberate choice to leave India for Pakistan during Partition. The citizenship framework would be unworkable — both India and Pakistan would be claiming the same populations, and individuals could claim citizenship in both countries. The proviso's return‑migration exception was essential to avoid penalising those who returned under a legal permit, often due to communal violence or changed circumstances. Without it, returnees would be stateless: disqualified in India under Article 7, and no longer welcome in Pakistan. The proviso ensured that the door remained open for those who returned through legal channels.

|||NUANCES|||

"Migrated" — intention of permanent residence: The Supreme Court has consistently held that "migrated" in Articles 6 and 7 means movement with the intention of permanent residence. Temporary visits, business trips, or forced displacement without the intention to settle do not constitute migration for constitutional purposes. This is a fact‑intensive inquiry in citizenship litigation.

1 March 1947 — the cut‑off before Partition: The date is earlier than the formal Partition (14‑15 August 1947) because large‑scale communal migration began in early 1947. The framers chose this date to capture the reality that the population exchange had begun well before the formal boundary was drawn. Any person who left for what became Pakistan after this date is covered.

The proviso — return under a permit: The proviso requires a "permit for resettlement or permanent return issued by or under the authority of any law." This is a legal, documented return — not a surreptitious crossing. The permit system was administered under the Influx from Pakistan (Control) Act, 1948. The returnee is then "deemed to have migrated to the territory of India after the nineteenth day of July, 1948," bringing them under Article 6(b)(ii), which requires registration as a citizen before the Constitution's commencement. The returnee must have been registered; a mere physical return without permit and registration does not qualify.

No separate pathway — the proviso funnels into Article 6(b)(ii): The proviso does not create an independent citizenship pathway. It brings the returnee within Article 6(b)(ii), meaning they must satisfy all the conditions of that clause: application for registration before 26 January 1950, in the prescribed form and manner, and six months' residence before the application. A returnee who failed to register before the Constitution's commencement cannot claim citizenship under Article 7's proviso.

Relationship with Article 5: A person who migrated to Pakistan but later returned and can independently satisfy Article 5 (domicile plus birth/residence) may not need Article 7's proviso. But Article 7's "notwithstanding" clause means that Article 5 cannot be used to circumvent Article 7 — if the person's migration to Pakistan was with permanent intent, Article 7 disqualifies them unless the proviso applies.

|||Interconnections|||

Article 5 (Citizenship at commencement): The general rule. Article 7 overrides it — a person disqualified under Article 7 cannot claim under Article 5 even if they satisfy domicile and residence.

Article 6 (Migrants from Pakistan to India): The Pakistan‑to‑India migrant pathway. Article 7's proviso brings returnees within Article 6(b)(ii). The two articles are the twin halves of the Partition citizenship framework: Article 6 covers Pakistan‑to‑India migrants; Article 7 covers India‑to‑Pakistan migrants (with the return exception).

Article 8 (Overseas Indians): Completes the special citizenship provisions. Not directly linked to Article 7 but shares the "notwithstanding anything in article 5" structure.

Article 9 (Loss of citizenship on acquiring foreign citizenship): Applies independently. A person who became a Pakistani citizen by migration and naturalisation loses any claim to Indian citizenship under Article 9 as well, independently of Article 7.

Influx from Pakistan (Control) Act, 1948: The statutory framework for the permit system referenced in the proviso. The Act and its rules defined the "permit for resettlement or permanent return."

Citizenship Act, 1955: The post‑constitutional framework. Article 7 operates only at the Constitution's commencement. Post‑1950 migration and citizenship are governed by the Citizenship Act.

|||Practical|||

In Foreigners Tribunal proceedings in Assam, a person alleged to be an illegal migrant from Bangladesh claims citizenship through a Partition‑era ancestor. The tribunal examines whether that ancestor migrated from India to East Pakistan (now Bangladesh) after 1 March 1947. If yes, the ancestor is disqualified under Article 7. If the ancestor later returned to India under a permit and was registered before 26 January 1950, the proviso saves citizenship. Documentary evidence of the permit and registration is critical.

A Hindu family left Delhi for Lahore in June 1947, intending to settle permanently. They returned to India in October 1947 without a permit, crossing the border informally. The father is disqualified under Article 7. The proviso does not apply because there was no permit. The family must rely on the Citizenship Act, 1955, or subsequent legislation for any claim to Indian citizenship; Article 7 does not help them.

A Muslim trader travelled from Bombay to Karachi in April 1947 for business, intending to return. He returned in December 1947. This is not "migration" under Article 7 because there was no intention of permanent residence. Article 7 does not disqualify him. He may claim under Article 5.

A Sikh man migrated to Lahore in August 1947, obtained a permit for permanent return in 1949, returned to Amritsar, and applied for and obtained registration as a citizen before 26 January 1950. He is a citizen under Article 7's proviso read with Article 6(b)(ii). His children, born after his return, are citizens by birth under the Citizenship Act, 1955.

The pathway is registration, not automatic citizenship. The person must apply to the Indian diplomatic or consular representative in their country of residence, in the prescribed form and manner. The Government of India (or the Dominion Government, for pre‑commencement registrations) prescribes the form and manner. Article 8 thus provides a standing constitutional pathway for the overseas diaspora to claim Indian citizenship by registration, based on ancestral ties, without being physically present in India or satisfying the domicile requirement of Article 5. This was a conscious choice by the framers to maintain legal bonds with the millions of persons of Indian origin spread across Africa, Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, and the Pacific who might wish to retain or claim Indian citizenship.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 8, persons of Indian origin residing abroad at the Constitution's commencement would have no constitutional pathway to citizenship. Article 5 requires domicile in India — precisely what they lack. Article 6 covers migrants from Pakistan — they are not migrants from Pakistan. They would be left dependent on ordinary legislation (the Citizenship Act, 1955) to claim citizenship, with no constitutional guarantee. The framers, deeply conscious of the large and dispersed Indian diaspora — indentured labourers in Fiji, traders in East Africa, professionals in Southeast Asia — wanted to ensure that the Constitution itself offered them a path back to Indian citizenship. The three‑generation descent test is consistent with Articles 5 and 6, creating a uniform ancestral‑connection framework. The registration mechanism (rather than automatic citizenship) was a practical administrative requirement — overseas applicants needed to be documented, and the diplomatic channel was the most feasible route.

|||NUANCES|||

"Notwithstanding anything in article 5" — the domicile override: Article 5's requirement of Indian domicile is the single biggest barrier for overseas Indians. Article 8 removes it entirely. The overseas resident need not have ever set foot in India, let alone formed a domicile. The ancestral birth connection and registration suffice.

The three‑generation descent test — deep roots, not recent connection: Like Article 6, Article 8 reaches back to grandparents. A person whose grandfather was born in undivided India but whose parents and themselves were born in Fiji can claim registration under Article 8. This broad temporal reach was deliberate — the Indian diaspora had been overseas for generations, and the framers did not want to exclude those whose connection was through grandparents.

"India as defined in the Government of India Act, 1935" — undivided India: The reference is to pre‑Partition British India, including the territories that became Pakistan. A person of Indian origin whose grandparent was born in Lahore (now Pakistan) is eligible under Article 8, because Lahore was within the 1935 definition of India. This is a significant inclusion — it covers persons of undivided Indian origin regardless of whether their ancestral home ended up in India or Pakistan after Partition.

Registration — an application‑based pathway, not automatic: Unlike Article 5 citizenship (automatic at commencement) or Article 6 citizenship (automatic for pre‑July 1948 migrants with residence, or by registration for later migrants), Article 8 requires a positive act: an application to the diplomatic or consular representative. The registration may be "before or after the commencement of this Constitution" — pre‑commencement registrations by the Dominion Government are validated and recognised. Post‑commencement registrations continue under the Constitution's authority, later supplemented by the Citizenship Act, 1955.

"Ordinarily residing" — a factual test, not domicile: The person must be "ordinarily residing" in a country outside India. This is a lesser standard than domicile — it requires actual residence, not a permanent intention to remain. Temporary absence from the country of residence would not break ordinary residence.

Post‑1955: The Citizenship Act overlay: Article 8 provides the constitutional foundation, but after the Citizenship Act, 1955, came into force, the registration of overseas Indians is now primarily governed by the Act (Sections 4–5, dealing with citizenship by registration). Article 8 remains the constitutional source of the power; the Citizenship Act provides the detailed statutory framework.

Article 6 (Migrants from Pakistan): Uses the same three‑generation descent test and the same "India as defined in the Government of India Act, 1935" reference. Articles 6, 7, and 8 together form the special citizenship provisions for Partition migrants and the diaspora.

Article 7 (Migrants to Pakistan): The disqualification article. An overseas Indian who had previously migrated to Pakistan after 1 March 1947 would be disqualified under Article 7, and Article 8 would not revive citizenship. |||ABOUT|||
Article 9 is the constitutional prohibition on dual citizenship. In a single sentence, it declares that no person shall be a citizen of India under Article 5, or be deemed a citizen under Article 6 or Article 8, if they have voluntarily acquired the citizenship of any foreign State. The key word is "voluntarily" — the acquisition must be a conscious, intentional act, not an automatic or involuntary conferral of citizenship by a foreign State's law (e.g., by birth on its soil). The article operates as a disqualification at the Constitution's commencement: a person who, before 26 January 1950, had voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship cannot claim Indian citizenship under the Constitution's citizenship provisions. The article embodies the Constituent Assembly's deliberate rejection of dual citizenship. Unlike some federations (e.g., the United States, which tolerates dual citizenship), India opted for a single, unitary citizenship model. Article 9 is the constitutional expression of that choice — a bright‑line rule that Indian citizenship is exclusive. It works alongside the Citizenship Act, 1955, which provides the statutory framework for loss of citizenship by voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship (Section 9 of the Act), creating a constitutional‑statutory continuum.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 9, a person could claim Indian citizenship under Articles 5, 6, or 8 while simultaneously holding citizenship of another State. This would create divided loyalties, complicate diplomatic protection, and undermine the unitary conception of Indian citizenship the framers chose. The Constitution would have to accommodate dual nationals in provisions reserving rights and offices to citizens (Articles 15, 16, 19, 58, 66, etc.), creating difficult questions about which citizenship prevails for which purpose. The post‑colonial context was particularly sensitive — the framers wanted a clean break from the colonial past and a clear, exclusive bond between the citizen and the new Republic. The prohibition also served the practical purpose of clarifying the status of persons of Indian origin who had acquired citizenship of other Commonwealth countries or other foreign States: they could not simultaneously claim Indian citizenship under the Constitution.

|||NUANCES|||

"Voluntarily acquired" — the critical qualifier: The prohibition applies only to voluntary acquisition. A person upon whom foreign citizenship is automatically conferred by operation of law (e.g., by birth in a country with unconditional jus soli) without any voluntary act has not "voluntarily acquired" it. The Supreme Court has distinguished between voluntary acts (applying for a passport, taking an oath of allegiance, formally accepting citizenship) and involuntary conferral. This is a fact‑intensive inquiry.

The scope — Articles 5, 6, and 8 only: Article 9 does not refer to Article 7 (migrants to Pakistan), which is a disqualification article in its own right, or Article 8's overseas Indian registration pathway — wait, it does refer to Article 8. It refers to Articles 5, 6, and 8 — the three pathways to citizenship. Article 7 is a disqualification, not a pathway, so it is not listed. Article 9 thus covers all three constitutional citizenship pathways.

Temporal application — at the Constitution's commencement: Article 9 operates at 26 January 1950. It disqualifies persons who, before that date, had voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship. Post‑commencement acquisition is governed by the Citizenship Act, 1955, Section 9, which provides for termination of Indian citizenship upon voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship. The constitutional and statutory provisions together create a continuous prohibition.

No exception for Commonwealth citizens: During the Constituent Assembly debates, some members proposed an exception for Commonwealth countries, arguing that Commonwealth citizenship was a common bond. The Assembly rejected this. Article 9 makes no distinction between foreign States — Commonwealth or otherwise, the prohibition is uniform.

Burden of proof: In citizenship disputes, the burden of proving that a person voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship lies on the party asserting disqualification under Article 9. This is a significant evidentiary point — the State cannot simply allege foreign citizenship; it must prove voluntary acquisition.

|||Interconnections|||

Article 5 (Citizenship at commencement): The general rule. Article 9 disqualifies Article 5 citizens if they voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship.

Article 6 (Migrants from Pakistan): Article 9 disqualifies Article 6 migrants if they voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship.

Article 8 (Overseas Indians): Article 9 disqualifies Article 8 registrants if they voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship.

Article 10 (Continuance of citizenship): Guarantees continued citizenship to persons who are citizens under Articles 5–8, subject to any law made by Parliament. Article 9 is one of the constitutional grounds on which citizenship can be lost, and Article 10 preserves Parliament's power to legislate further.

Article 11 (Parliament's power): Empowers Parliament to make laws for acquisition and termination of citizenship. The Citizenship Act, 1955, Section 9, is the statutory expression of Article 9's principle — it provides for termination of Indian citizenship upon voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship.

Citizenship Act, 1955, Section 9: The statutory termination provision. It provides that if a citizen voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country, they cease to be an Indian citizen. The Act also provides the procedural mechanism (determination by the Central Government, rules of evidence, etc.).

Passport Act, 1967: Governs the issuance and revocation of passports. A person who has lost Indian citizenship under Article 9 is not entitled to an Indian passport.

|||Practical|||

A person born in India in 1940, domiciled in India, satisfies all Article 5 conditions for citizenship. However, in 1948, they applied for and obtained citizenship of the United Kingdom, taking an oath of allegiance. As of 26 January 1950, they are disqualified under Article 9 from Indian citizenship — the voluntary acquisition of UK citizenship bars them.

A child born in the United States in 1948 to Indian parents automatically acquired US citizenship by birth (jus soli). The parents returned to India in 1950. The child did not "voluntarily acquire" US citizenship — it was automatic. Article 9 does not disqualify the child, who may claim Indian citizenship under Article 5 (if domiciled) or Article 8 (if registered). However, under the Citizenship Act, 1955, the child's Indian citizenship may later be affected if they voluntarily assert US citizenship (e.g., by obtaining a US passport as an adult).

A person of Indian origin residing in Kenya applies for and is registered as an Indian citizen under Article 8. They also hold Kenyan citizenship. If the Kenyan citizenship was voluntarily acquired before the Indian registration, Article 9 disqualifies them from Article 8 citizenship. The registration is void ab initio. If they acquire Kenyan citizenship after valid Indian registration, Article 9 does not directly apply (post‑commencement), but the Citizenship Act, 1955, Section 9, terminates their Indian citizenship.

In a Foreigners Tribunal proceeding, the State alleges that the proceedee voluntarily acquired Bangladeshi citizenship by obtaining a Bangladeshi passport. The proceedee denies this. The burden lies on the State to prove voluntary acquisition — the passport application, the oath of allegiance, or other evidence of a voluntary act. Mere possession of a foreign passport may raise a presumption but is not conclusive.

COI 10 > Continuance of the rights of citizenship.:
Core_Rule:
Indian citizenship CONTINUES by default under foregoing provisions.

**Though:**
Parliament MAY legislate to regulate:
CONTINUANCE of citizenship.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 10 is the constitutional guarantee of continuity. It provides that every person who is or is deemed to be a citizen of India under Articles 5–9 "shall, subject to the provisions of any law that may be made by Parliament, continue to be such citizen." It does two things simultaneously: it guarantees that citizenship, once acquired under the Constitution, continues — and it subjects that continuance to parliamentary legislation. The citizen's status is constitutionally protected, not merely statutory. Parliament cannot arbitrarily strip citizenship; the "subject to" clause means Parliament can legislate on acquisition, termination, and deprivation, but such legislation must be consistent with the constitutional framework. Article 10 is the bridge between the constitutional citizenship code (Articles 5–9) and the statutory citizenship framework (the Citizenship Act, 1955, enacted under Article 11). It ensures that those who became citizens at the Constitution's commencement do not lose that status by executive action or by the silence of the law — they remain citizens unless and until Parliament provides otherwise, and only in accordance with such parliamentary law. It is a constitutional anchor for the rights and status that flow from citizenship.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 10, the citizenship acquired under Articles 5–9 would be constitutionally fragile. A person who was a citizen on 26 January 1950 could lose that status through executive action, administrative decision, or a subsequent constitutional interpretation without any parliamentary law. The guarantee of "continue to be such citizen" provides security — citizenship is not a temporary grant but a continuing status. Conversely, without the "subject to" clause, Parliament would have no power to regulate the continuation of citizenship by ordinary legislation, and every instance of loss of citizenship (renunciation, acquisition of foreign citizenship, deprivation for fraud) would require a constitutional amendment. Article 10 creates the Goldilocks balance: citizenship is constitutionally guaranteed, but Parliament can legislate within constitutional bounds to regulate its continuance.

|||NUANCES|||

"Is or is deemed to be" — covering all constitutional pathways: The phrase covers both persons who are citizens (under Article 5 — automatic citizenship at commencement) and those who are deemed to be citizens (under Articles 6 and 8 — migrants from Pakistan and overseas Indians, whose citizenship is by deeming fiction or registration). Article 9's disqualification (voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship) is the exclusion; anyone not excluded under Article 9 and covered by Articles 5, 6, or 8 is protected by Article 10.

"Subject to the provisions of any law that may be made by Parliament" — a legislative power, not an executive one: Parliament may make laws affecting the continuance of citizenship. The executive cannot, by itself, deprive a person of citizenship. The Citizenship Act, 1955, enacted under Article 11, is the law Parliament has made. It provides for termination (Section 9 — voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship), renunciation (Section 8), and deprivation (Section 10 — fraud, disloyalty, etc.). These are parliamentary laws, not executive decrees. The "subject to" clause thus channels all citizenship regulation through the legislative process.

Continuity, not irrevocability: Article 10 does not make citizenship irrevocable. It guarantees continuance "subject to" parliamentary law. This means citizenship can be lost, but only in accordance with the Citizenship Act. A person who voluntarily acquires Pakistani citizenship loses Indian citizenship under Section 9 of the Act — this is consistent with Article 10 because the loss is under a parliamentary law.

The constitutional floor: Article 10, read with Article 11, creates a constitutional floor: citizenship status is governed by the Constitution and parliamentary law, not by executive policy. The Supreme Court has held that the right to citizenship is a fundamental right under Article 19 (available only to citizens), and Article 10 provides the constitutional guarantee of its continuance. Deprivation of citizenship without parliamentary authority would violate Article 10 and the fundamental rights that depend on citizenship.

|||Interconnections|||

Articles 5–9 (Constitutional citizenship provisions): Article 10 guarantees the continuance of citizenship acquired under these articles. It is the final provision in the constitutional citizenship chain, before Article 11 hands over to Parliament.

Article 11 (Parliament's power to legislate on citizenship): Article 11 empowers Parliament to make laws for acquisition, termination, and all other matters relating to citizenship. Article 10 makes the continuance of constitutional citizenship subject to such laws. Together, they create the constitutional‑statutory citizenship framework.

Citizenship Act, 1955: The parliamentary legislation enacted under Article 11. It governs acquisition, termination, renunciation, and deprivation of citizenship. Article 10 ensures that the Act operates within the constitutional framework.

Part III (Fundamental Rights): Several fundamental rights are reserved to citizens — Article 15 (non‑discrimination), Article 16 (public employment), Article 19 (freedoms). Article 10 guarantees the continuance of the status on which these rights depend.

Article 19(1)(e) (Right to reside and settle): Available only to citizens. Deprivation of citizenship affects this right. Article 10 ensures that such deprivation can only occur under parliamentary law.

|||Practical|||

A person became a citizen on 26 January 1950 under Article 5. In 1960, they voluntarily acquire United States citizenship. Under Section 9 of the Citizenship Act, 1955 (a law made by Parliament), their Indian citizenship is terminated. Article 10 is satisfied — the loss is under a parliamentary law. The person cannot claim that Article 10 guarantees them Indian citizenship forever.

The Government issues an executive order purporting to cancel the citizenship of a person of Pakistani origin who migrated in 1947 and became a citizen under Article 6. The person challenges the order. The Supreme Court holds that Article 10 guarantees continuance of citizenship, subject only to parliamentary law. An executive order cannot deprive a person of citizenship. The order is struck down.

A person born in India after 1950 acquires citizenship under the Citizenship Act, 1955 (by birth). They are not covered by Article 10 directly (which covers Articles 5–9 citizens), but their citizenship is equally protected under the Act and Article 11. The principle of Article 10 — that citizenship continues subject to parliamentary law — applies by analogy.

The Citizenship Amendment Act, 2019, provides a pathway to citizenship for certain classes of illegal migrants. Opponents challenge it as violating Article 10. The court examines whether Article 10 is violated. Article 10 guarantees continuance, not the exclusion of new pathways. The challenge is assessed under Article 14 and other provisions, not Article 10.

Government of Andhra Pradesh v. Syed Mohd. Khan (1962) – 85% (held that Article 10, read with Articles 9 and 11, creates a complete constitutional‑statutory scheme; loss of citizenship under the Citizenship Act is consistent with Article 10's "subject to" clause)

COI 11 > Parliament to regulate the right of citizenship by law.:
**Core_Rule:**
Arts. 5–10 do NOT derogate from Parliament's power over citizenship.

**Power:**
Parliament MAY legislate to regulate:
acquisition / termination / other matters.
on citizenship.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 11 is the constitutional handover of citizenship from the Constituent Assembly to Parliament. It declares that nothing in the preceding Articles 5–10 shall derogate from Parliament's power to make any provision with respect to the acquisition and termination of citizenship and "all other matters relating to citizenship." The phrase is sweeping — Parliament's legislative competence over citizenship is plenary, subject only to the Constitution's fundamental rights and basic structure. Article 11 is not itself a source of power; it is a savings clause that preserves Parliament's power to legislate on citizenship despite the detailed constitutional citizenship code in Articles 5–10. Those articles were the transitional provisions — they determined who was a citizen at the Constitution's commencement. Article 11 makes clear that Parliament can build on, modify, supplement, and even override (within constitutional limits) that framework by ordinary legislation. The Citizenship Act, 1955, is the product of this article, governing every aspect of citizenship for all persons born after 26 January 1950. Article 11, read with Entry 17 of the Union List (Seventh Schedule), confers exclusive legislative competence on Parliament over citizenship. It is the constitutional bridge between the frozen citizenship of 1950 and the dynamic citizenship law of the Republic.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 11, there would be a constitutional vacuum after Articles 5–10. Those articles only address citizenship at the Constitution's commencement. Who is a citizen born in India in 1951? Can a foreigner be naturalised? Can citizenship be terminated for disloyalty? The Constitution would be silent, and Parliament's power to legislate would be doubtful — the detailed provisions of Articles 5–10 could be read as an exhaustive code, leaving no room for parliamentary supplementation. Article 11 dispels that doubt: Parliament's power is not derogated from, but preserved in full. Without it, every amendment to citizenship law might require a constitutional amendment, freezing the citizenship framework in 1950. The Citizenship Act, 1955, and its amendments (including the 2019 Amendment) would lack a clear constitutional foundation. Article 11 is the enabling provision for the entire statutory edifice of Indian citizenship law.

|||NUANCES|||

"Nothing in the foregoing provisions... shall derogate from" — a savings clause, not a grant of power: Article 11 does not confer legislative power; it clarifies that Articles 5–10 do not limit Parliament's pre‑existing legislative power under the Constitution (Entry 17, Union List). It is a non‑derogation clause — the constitutional citizenship provisions are not exhaustive, and Parliament can legislate freely.

"Acquisition and termination... and all other matters relating to citizenship" — the widest possible scope: The phrase "all other matters" is deliberately comprehensive. It includes: citizenship by birth, descent, registration, naturalisation, incorporation of territory; renunciation, termination, deprivation; registration of overseas citizens; issuance of certificates of citizenship; determination of citizenship disputes; and any ancillary matter. The Supreme Court has consistently upheld Parliament's broad power under Article 11, read with Entry 17.

Parliament's power is plenary but not absolute — fundamental rights and basic structure apply: While Article 11 preserves Parliament's power in the widest terms, legislation made under it must comply with Part III (Fundamental Rights). The Citizenship Amendment Act, 2019, was challenged (inter alia) under Article 14 (equality) and Article 15 (non‑discrimination on grounds of religion). Article 11 does not immunise citizenship legislation from fundamental rights scrutiny. The Supreme Court has affirmed that citizenship legislation under Article 11 is subject to judicial review on constitutional grounds.

Articles 5–10 are transitional; Article 11 is prospective: Articles 5–10 determined citizenship at the commencement. Article 11 empowers Parliament to govern citizenship thereafter. The two sets of provisions do not conflict; they operate in different temporal domains. However, Parliament can also legislate retrospectively — e.g., the Citizenship Act, 1955, contains provisions that affect persons who were citizens under Articles 5–10 (termination, deprivation), and Article 11 makes clear that this is within Parliament's power.

Exclusive Union subject — no State role: Entry 17 of the Union List is "Citizenship, naturalisation and aliens." This, read with Article 11, means citizenship is exclusively a Union subject. States have no legislative competence over citizenship, and State laws affecting citizenship status are void under Article 246.

|||Interconnections|||

Articles 5–10 (Constitutional citizenship provisions): The transitional framework that Article 11 preserves Parliament's power to supplement and modify. Article 11 is the final article in Part II, completing the constitutional citizenship code.

Article 10 (Continuance of citizenship): Guarantees continuation of citizenship under Articles 5–9 "subject to the provisions of any law that may be made by Parliament." Article 11 is the source of Parliament's power to make those laws. Together, they ensure that constitutional citizenship is subject to parliamentary regulation.

Article 246 and Entry 17, Union List (Seventh Schedule): The distribution of legislative powers. Entry 17 gives Parliament exclusive competence over citizenship. Article 11 reinforces this by ensuring that Articles 5–10 do not derogate from it.

Citizenship Act, 1955: The principal parliamentary legislation enacted under Article 11. It governs acquisition, termination, renunciation, deprivation, and all ancillary matters. All amendments to the Act (1986, 1992, 2003, 2005, 2015, 2019) derive their constitutional validity from Article 11.

Passport Act, 1967, and Foreigners Act, 1946: Ancillary statutes governing travel documents and the regulation of aliens. They operate in the penumbra of Article 11, though their primary legislative entries are different.

Article 14 (Equality) and Article 15 (Non‑discrimination): Citizenship legislation under Article 11 is subject to these fundamental rights. The Supreme Court's review of the Citizenship Amendment Act, 2019, centres on whether the classification based on religion and country of origin violates Article 14.

|||Practical|||

Parliament enacts the Citizenship Act, 1955, under Article 11. The Act provides for citizenship by birth (Section 3), descent (Section 4), registration (Section 5), naturalisation (Section 6), and incorporation of territory (Section 7). It also provides for termination (Section 9), renunciation (Section 8), and deprivation (Section 10). All these provisions flow from Article 11's plenary grant.

In 2019, Parliament amends the Citizenship Act to provide a pathway to citizenship for non‑Muslim illegal migrants from three specified countries. The amendment is challenged under Article 14. The Supreme Court examines whether the classification is reasonable. The fact that the amendment is made under Article 11 does not preclude fundamental rights scrutiny — Article 11 is a power‑conferring provision, not an immunity‑granting one.

A State legislature passes a law purporting to define State citizenship for purposes of domicile‑based reservations in employment. The Supreme Court strikes down the law — citizenship is exclusively a Union subject under Article 11 and Entry 17, Union List. States cannot create their own citizenship or define "State domicile" in a manner that conflicts with national citizenship.

A person born in India in 1960 claims citizenship by birth. The claim is governed by the Citizenship Act, 1955, Section 3 (as it stood at the time of birth) — a law made under Article 11. The person does not need to rely on Articles 5–10, which apply only to persons born before the Constitution's commencement.

COI 12 > Definition.:
Deconstruction
(1) For the purposes of this Part, unless the context otherwise requires, the expression “the State” includes—
- (a) the Government of India (President, Prime Minister, Council of Ministers);
- (b) Parliament of India (President + Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha);
- (c) the Government of each State (Governor, Chief Minister, Council of Ministers of a State);
- (d) the Legislature of each State (Legislative Assembly + Legislative Council (if exists));
- (e) all local authorities within the territory of India (Municipalities, Panchayats, Municipal Corporations.); and
- (f) all other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India (Public Service Commissions, Electricity Boards, State Transport Corporations, Universities).
(2) The term “authorities” includes bodies exercising governmental functions, whether statutory or non-statutory, and whether established by the Constitution or by law.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 12 is the definitional gateway to Part III (Fundamental Rights) and, by extension, to much of the Constitution's rights architecture. It defines "the State" for the purposes of Part III as including: (1) the Government and Parliament of India, (2) the Government and Legislature of each State, and (3) "all local or other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India." The definition is inclusive ("includes"), not exhaustive. Its purpose is to identify the entities against whom fundamental rights can be enforced — fundamental rights are primarily vertical rights against the State, not horizontal rights against private persons (with exceptions like Article 17 — untouchability). Article 12 thus determines the reach of constitutional remedies under Article 32 (direct enforcement by the Supreme Court) and Article 226 (High Courts). The most litigated phrase is "other authorities" — the Supreme Court has progressively expanded this to include a vast range of bodies that are functionally, financially, and administratively under State control. The definition is "unless the context otherwise requires," meaning a body may be "State" for one fundamental right but not for another, though this flexibility is rarely exercised in practice. Article 12 is not merely definitional; it is the constitutional pivot on which the enforceability of fundamental rights against the vast apparatus of the modern Indian State — ministries, legislatures, municipalities, public sector undertakings, statutory corporations, and instrumentalities — turns.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 12, "the State" in Part III would be undefined. Fundamental rights would be enforceable against the Union and State Governments (named in the articles themselves) but against whom else? Would a municipality be the State? A nationalised bank? A public sector university? A State electricity board? The judiciary would have to import a definition from the General Clauses Act (which does not exist for this purpose) or develop one from common law. The expansion of fundamental rights jurisprudence — holding BCCI, AIIMS, Indian Airlines, LIC, and countless other bodies accountable under Part III — would lack a statutory anchor. The phrase "other authorities" has been the engine of the Supreme Court's rights jurisprudence for seven decades; without it, each new form of State activity (public‑private partnerships, regulatory bodies, government companies) would require a separate judicial determination of its constitutional character. Article 12 provides the framework for that determination.

|||NUANCES|||

"Unless the context otherwise requires" — a flexible definition: The definition is not rigid. A body may be "State" for the purpose of Article 14 (equality) but not for Article 19(1)(g) (freedom of trade), though in practice the Supreme Court has applied a uniform test. The phrase also allows the court to exclude certain bodies from "State" in contexts where applying fundamental rights would be inappropriate (e.g., a purely private entity performing a public function might be "State" for Article 14 but not for Article 16 — public employment).

"Local or other authorities" — the expansive jurisprudential evolution: The Supreme Court's interpretation of "other authorities" has evolved through several phases: (a) the ejusdem generis phase — only authorities of the same kind as local authorities (government‑like); (b) the "agency or instrumentality" phase — the Rajasthan State Electricity Board v. Mohan Lal (1967) test: the body must be an agency or instrumentality of the government; (c) the Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981) phase — a five‑factor test: (i) who created the body, (ii) what functions does it perform — are they public functions, (iii) is there pervasive State control, (iv) is there deep and pervasive financial control, (v) does the body enjoy monopoly status conferred by the State. No single factor is conclusive; it is a cumulative, fact‑intensive test.

"Under the control of the Government of India" — extraterritorial State organs: The phrase extends "the State" to bodies located outside India but under the control of the Indian Government — embassies, consulates, cultural missions, and potentially Indian public sector undertakings operating abroad. This ensures that fundamental rights can be enforced against Indian State organs even when they operate extraterritorially, subject to territorial jurisdictional limits of Indian courts.

Article 12 and private entities — the functional test: A purely private entity (e.g., a private school, a private company) is generally not "the State" under Article 12. However, if a private entity performs a public function or is deeply controlled by the State, it may be treated as an instrumentality of the State and bound by fundamental rights. The BCCI, for instance, has been held to perform a public function (regulation of cricket) and is subject to judicial review under Article 226, but the Supreme Court has stopped short of declaring it "State" under Article 12 — a nuanced distinction.

|||Interconnections|||

Part III (Fundamental Rights): Article 12 defines the entity against which all fundamental rights (Articles 14–35) are primarily enforceable. The rights are vertical, not horizontal.

Article 13 (Laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights): Article 13 voids laws inconsistent with Part III. "Law" includes actions of "the State" as defined in Article 12. The two articles together form the enforcement mechanism: Article 12 defines the wrongdoer; Article 13 invalidates its unconstitutional actions.

Article 32 (Constitutional remedies) and Article 226 (High Court writs): Article 32 gives the Supreme Court power to issue writs for enforcement of fundamental rights — against "the State" as defined in Article 12. Article 226 gives High Courts power to issue writs against any "person or authority," which is broader than Article 12 — the High Court can issue writs against private persons in certain circumstances, a power the Supreme Court does not have under Article 32.

Article 36 (Definition of "State" for Part IV — Directive Principles): Article 36 defines "State" for Part IV in terms identical to Article 12. The definition of the State is thus uniform for Parts III and IV, linking the enforcement of rights with the implementation of directive principles.

Article 298 (Power to carry on trade): The Union and States may carry on trade and business. When they do, they act through corporations and companies. Article 12 ensures that these commercial entities remain "State" and bound by fundamental rights — the State cannot escape Part III by incorporating a company.

|||Practical|||

A professor in a government‑aided private college is dismissed without hearing. She challenges the dismissal under Article 14 (equality) and Article 21 (livelihood). The college argues it is a private body, not "the State" under Article 12. The court applies the Ajay Hasia test: the college receives 95% government funding, the government controls its governing body, and it performs the public function of education. The court holds it is an instrumentality of the State under Article 12 and bound by fundamental rights.

An employee of a public sector bank (nationalised under the Banking Companies Act) challenges a disciplinary action. The bank is a statutory corporation created by a Central Act, performing a public function, and is an "other authority" under Article 12. The employee can enforce Article 14 and Article 16 (equality in public employment) against the bank.

BCCI cancels a cricket tournament without hearing the participating teams. The aggrieved teams file a writ petition in the High Court under Article 226, arguing BCCI performs a public function. The High Court issues the writ — Article 226 extends to "any person or authority," not just "the State." But if the petition were filed in the Supreme Court under Article 32 (which is limited to fundamental rights against "the State"), the Court would examine whether BCCI is "State" under Article 12 — a more stringent test that BCCI may not meet.

An Indian embassy in a foreign country refuses to renew the passport of an Indian citizen without reasons. The citizen challenges the action under Article 21 (right to travel). The embassy is an organ of the Government of India and "under the control of the Government of India" — it is "the State" under Article 12. The writ jurisdiction of Indian courts extends to it, subject to territorial limitations.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 13 is the enforcement sword of Part III. It mandates judicial review of legislation for fundamental rights compliance. Clause (1) deals with pre‑Constitution laws: all existing laws that are inconsistent with fundamental rights are void to the extent of the inconsistency. Clause (2) is the prohibition on future legislation: the State shall not make any law that takes away or abridges fundamental rights, and any law made in contravention is void to the extent of the contravention. The voidness is not total — only the inconsistent portion is struck down, and if the inconsistent portion is severable, the rest survives. Clause (3) defines "law" broadly to include Ordinances, orders, bye‑laws, rules, regulations, notifications, and even customs or usages having the force of law — ensuring that fundamental rights bind all forms of State action, not just parliamentary legislation. "Laws in force" is defined to include all pre‑Constitution legislation that was not repealed, even if not in operation in all areas. Clause (4) is the critical exception: constitutional amendments under Article 368 are not subject to Article 13. This clause was the battleground of the basic structure doctrine — the Supreme Court held in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) that while Article 13 does not apply to constitutional amendments, amendments that destroy the basic structure are void under the inherent limitations of the amending power. Article 13 thus operates as the constitutional nullification clause: any law, in any form, from any source, that violates fundamental rights is void from inception (for post‑Constitution laws) or void from the Constitution's commencement (for pre‑Constitution laws). It is the textual foundation of judicial review in India.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 13, fundamental rights would be mere declarations. The legislature could pass laws that violate equality, free speech, or personal liberty, and courts would have no constitutional authority to strike them down. Pre‑Constitution laws that were inconsistent with fundamental rights — and there were many, from sedition laws to preventive detention statutes — would continue in force unchallenged. The definition of "law" in clause (3) would be missing, allowing the State to circumvent fundamental rights through delegated legislation, administrative orders, and notifications. The exception for constitutional amendments in clause (4) was essential to allow Parliament to amend the Constitution; without it, the amending power itself would be subject to Article 13, and the Constitution would be frozen. Article 13 is the keystone of the fundamental rights arch — it transforms rights from rhetoric into enforceable constitutional commands.

|||NUANCES|||

Clause (1) vs. Clause (2) — pre‑Constitution and post‑Constitution laws: The distinction is temporal. Clause (1) applies to laws existing before 26 January 1950; they are void from that date, not from their enactment. Clause (2) applies to laws made after 26 January 1950; they are void from inception — stillborn, as the Supreme Court has said. The practical difference: a pre‑Constitution law inconsistent with fundamental rights may have validly governed transactions before 1950; a post‑Constitution law never had any valid operation.

"To the extent of such inconsistency" — the doctrine of severability: Article 13 does not strike down the entire statute; it strikes down only the inconsistent portion. If the inconsistent portion is severable — that is, if the remainder can stand independently and the legislature would have enacted the remainder without the inconsistent part — the rest survives. This is the doctrine of severability, a judicial tool to minimise the disruption caused by striking down legislation. If the inconsistent portion is so intertwined with the rest that severance is impossible, the entire law falls.

Clause (3)(a) — "law" includes customs and usages: This is a uniquely Indian feature. Personal laws, caste customs, and community usages that have the force of law are "law" under Article 13. If a custom violates fundamental rights — e.g., a custom excluding women from inheritance — it is void under Article 13. This brings the entire body of uncodified personal law within the fundamental rights framework. The Shayara Bano (triple talaq) case invoked this reasoning.

Clause (4) — the constitutional amendment exception and the basic structure doctrine: Clause (4) was inserted by the 24th Amendment Act, 1971, to overturn the Supreme Court's decision in Golak Nath (1967), which had held that constitutional amendments were "law" under Article 13 and could be challenged for violating fundamental rights. The 24th Amendment made clear that Article 13 does not apply to Article 368 amendments. But in Kesavananda Bharati (1973), the Supreme Court held that while clause (4) is valid, Parliament cannot use the amending power to destroy the basic structure of the Constitution — a limitation inherent in the amending power itself, not derived from Article 13. Clause (4) thus protects amendments from Article 13 challenge, but not from basic structure challenge.

The definition of "law" — an inclusive, not exhaustive, list: The list in clause (3)(a) — Ordinance, order, bye‑law, rule, regulation, notification, custom, usage — is illustrative. The test is whether the instrument has "the force of law." Government circulars that are merely advisory do not have the force of law. A bye‑law of a municipal corporation, a notification under a statute, a rule made by a regulatory authority — all have the force of law and are subject to Article 13.

|||Interconnections|||

Part III (Fundamental Rights): Article 13 is the enforcement mechanism for all fundamental rights (Articles 14–35). Without Article 13, Part III would be unenforceable against legislation.

Article 12 (Definition of State): Article 13(2) says "The State shall not make any law." "State" is defined in Article 12. The two articles together define who is bound (Article 12) and the consequence of breach (Article 13).

Article 32 (Constitutional remedies) and Article 226 (High Court writs): The procedural pathways for challenging laws under Article 13. A person whose fundamental rights are violated by a void law can petition the Supreme Court under Article 32 or the High Court under Article 226.

Article 368 (Amendment of the Constitution): Clause (4) of Article 13 specifically exempts Article 368 amendments. This is the textual hook for the basic structure doctrine — the Supreme Court's holding that the amending power is not unlimited.

Article 245 (Legislative competence) and Article 246 (Distribution of powers): A law may be challenged both for legislative incompetence (violation of Article 245/246) and for violation of fundamental rights (Article 13). These are distinct grounds of judicial review. A law within legislative competence can still be void under Article 13.

Article 372 (Continuance of pre‑Constitution laws): Article 372 saves pre‑Constitution laws. Article 13(1) cuts into that saving — pre‑Constitution laws are saved only to the extent they are consistent with fundamental rights.

|||Practical|||

A pre‑Constitution sedition law (Section 124A IPC, enacted 1860) is challenged as violating Article 19(1)(a) (free speech). The court examines whether the law is inconsistent with Article 19(1)(a) read with Article 19(2) (reasonable restrictions). If the law goes beyond the permissible restrictions, it is void under Article 13(1) to the extent of the inconsistency. The court may read down the law to make it consistent with Article 19, rather than striking it down entirely.

A State passes a law reserving 100% of government jobs for local residents. The law is challenged under Article 13(2) read with Article 16 (equality in public employment). The Supreme Court holds the law void to the extent it exceeds the permissible limit on reservation. The remaining portion (a lower percentage of reservation) may survive if severable.

A village panchayat passes a resolution banning the entry of Dalits into the village temple. The resolution is an "order" having the force of law under Article 13(3)(a). It violates Article 17 (abolition of untouchability). The resolution is void under Article 13(2). The affected persons can directly petition the High Court under Article 226 to quash the resolution.

The 99th Constitutional Amendment (NJAC) is challenged as violating the basic structure by compromising judicial independence. Article 13(4) prevents an Article 13 challenge — the amendment is not void under Article 13. But the Supreme Court strikes it down under the basic structure doctrine — a separate, judge‑made limitation on the amending power.

Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967) – 85% (overruled by the 24th Amendment and Kesavananda on this point; had held that constitutional amendments are "law" under Article 13 and can be challenged for violating fundamental rights; the 24th Amendment inserted clause (4) to overturn this holding)

Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017) – 80% (held that triple talaq (talaq‑e‑biddat) is a "custom or usage having the force of law" under Article 13(3)(a) and is void under Article 13(1) for violating Article 14; the Muslim personal law practice was struck down under Article 13)

|||ABOUT|||
Article 14 guarantees "equality before the law" (no person is above the law) and "equal protection of the laws" (equal treatment for similarly situated persons). It binds the State and is available to any person — citizen or non‑citizen. It permits reasonable classification but prohibits class legislation. A valid classification needs an intelligible differentia and a rational nexus to the law's object. Even absent classification, State action can be struck down if manifestly arbitrary — a doctrine established in E.P. Royappa (1974). Article 14 is the most litigated fundamental right and the source of administrative law doctrines like proportionality and legitimate expectation.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 14, the State could discriminate freely, grant arbitrary privileges, and enact irrational laws. The rule of law would lack constitutional foundation. Administrative law, reservation jurisprudence, and public employment equality all rest on this basic norm.

|||NUANCES|||

Two concepts, one guarantee: "Equality before law" checks executive arbitrariness; "equal protection of the laws" checks legislative arbitrariness. Post‑Royappa, they are a single test: arbitrariness is the antithesis of equality.

The twin test: Classification must rest on a real, non‑arbitrary distinction (intelligible differentia) with a rational connection to the law's purpose (rational nexus).

Manifest arbitrariness: A law or action can fall even without a classification — if it is capricious, unreasonable, or procedurally unfair.

Universal application: "Any person" covers citizens, foreigners, corporations, and trusts.

|||Interconnections|||

Article 12: Defines the State bound by Article 14.

Article 13: Voids laws inconsistent with Article 14.

Articles 15–16: Specific applications on discrimination and public employment.

Article 21: Read with Article 14 post‑Maneka Gandhi, any procedure depriving life or liberty must be non‑arbitrary.

|||Practical|||

A government contract awarded without competitive bidding, favouring a politically connected firm, is struck down under Article 14 as arbitrary — the State gave no rational basis for the selection.

A public sector employee dismissed without a hearing or reasons challenges the dismissal. The court strikes it down — procedural unreasonableness is arbitrariness under Article 14.

A law that gives an authority unguided discretion to select cases for a special procedure, without stated criteria, is struck down — the classification between those picked and spared is arbitrary.

|||Cases|||

|||ABOUT|||
Article 15 is the specific anti‑discrimination guarantee, building on Article 14's general equality norm. Clause (1) prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen "on grounds only of" religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth, or any combination of these. Clause (2) extends the prohibition horizontally — no citizen shall face disability, liability, restriction, or condition on these grounds in accessing shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of public entertainment, or publicly funded wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads, and places of public resort. Clauses (3)–(6) are enabling exceptions — they permit the State to make special provisions for women and children (3), for socially and educationally backward classes (SEBCs), Scheduled Castes, and Scheduled Tribes in education (4) (5), and for economically weaker sections (EWS) up to 10% additional reservation in education (6). The Explanation defines EWS by reference to family income and other indicators of economic disadvantage, to be notified by the State. Article 15 is citizen‑specific (unlike Article 14, which covers all persons) and the prohibited grounds are exhaustive. The phrase "on grounds only of" means discrimination must be solely based on these grounds — if another ground is mixed in, Article 15 is not directly violated, though Article 14 may still apply. Clauses (3)–(6) are not exceptions in the sense of overriding Article 15; they are enabling provisions that confirm the State's power to make affirmative action measures that would otherwise be discriminatory.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 15, the Constitution would have no specific prohibition on caste‑based, sex‑based, or religious discrimination in public spaces and State action. The general Article 14 would apply, but its "reasonable classification" test might permit forms of discrimination that Article 15's absolute bar (on the specified grounds) prohibits. The affirmative action clauses (3)–(6) are the constitutional foundation for women's reservations, SC/ST quotas in education, and the EWS reservation; without them, all such measures would be vulnerable under Article 14. Article 15(2) is the constitutional basis for laws prohibiting untouchability in public spaces and caste‑based denial of access — a direct attack on the social practice of exclusion.

|||NUANCES|||

"On grounds only of" — the sole‑ground test: The discrimination must be exclusively on one or more of the five prohibited grounds. If gender is combined with another non‑prohibited ground (e.g., physical fitness for a job), Article 15 is not violated, though Article 14 may apply. Courts read "only" strictly but not mechanically — if the prohibited ground is the dominant reason, Article 15 bites.

Clause (2) — horizontal application: This is one of the few fundamental rights that binds private persons, not just the State. A shopkeeper refusing entry to a Dalit violates Article 15(2) directly — no need to involve Article 12's definition of State. The provision targets the social practice of exclusion from public spaces.

Clause (3) — women and children: The State can make laws or policies that favour women and children. This includes reservation of seats in local bodies, maternity benefits, differential age of consent, and protective labour laws. The clause overrides the Article 15(1) prohibition on sex discrimination.

Clauses (4)–(5) — educational reservations for SEBCs/SCs/STs: These clauses, added by amendments (the 1st Amendment in 1951 inserted clause (4); the 93rd Amendment in 2005 inserted clause (5)), permit reservation in admission to educational institutions, including private unaided institutions (except minority institutions protected under Article 30). This was the constitutional response to the Champakam Dorairajan (1951) case, which struck down caste‑based reservations in education as violative of Article 15(1).

Clause (6) — EWS reservation (103rd Amendment, 2019): This created a new category of affirmative action based solely on economic criteria, not caste or social backwardness. It permits up to 10% additional reservation in education for economically weaker sections among the general category (non‑SC/ST/SEBC). The Supreme Court upheld this in Janhit Abhiyan (2022) by a 3:2 majority.

|||Interconnections|||

Article 14 (Equality): Article 15 is a specific application of Article 14 to the five prohibited grounds. Where discrimination involves these grounds, Article 15 is the primary provision; otherwise, Article 14 applies.

Article 16 (Equality in public employment): The employment counterpart. Article 16(4) permits reservation for backward classes in public employment. Together, Articles 15(4)–(5) and 16(4) form the reservation framework.

Article 17 (Abolition of untouchability): Article 15(2) deals with denial of access to public places; Article 17 abolishes untouchability entirely. They overlap in practice — a Dalit denied entry to a temple is covered by both.

Article 29(2) (Non‑discrimination in State‑aided educational institutions): Prohibits denial of admission on grounds of religion, race, caste, or language. Article 15(4)–(5) overrides this for affirmative action purposes.

|||Practical|||

A State government announces free bus travel for women. A male citizen challenges it as sex discrimination under Article 15(1). The State defends under Article 15(3) — a special provision for women. The challenge fails.

A restaurant refuses entry to a person because of their caste. This directly violates Article 15(2), and the person can seek remedies under Article 226 (High Court writ) and civil law (damages). No need to prove State involvement.

The Central Government introduces 10% EWS reservation in undergraduate medical admissions for general‑category students from families earning less than Rs. 8 lakhs annually, under Article 15(6). The Supreme Court upholds it (Janhit Abhiyan, 2022), holding that economic criteria can be a valid basis for affirmative action, and the exclusion of SC/ST/SEBC from the EWS quota does not violate the basic structure.

Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) – 85% (Mandal case; while primarily on Article 16(4), it discussed the relationship between Articles 14, 15, and 16 in the reservation framework)

|||ABOUT|||
Article 16 is the employment equality guarantee — the specific application of Article 14's equality norm to public employment. Clause (1) guarantees equality of opportunity for all citizens in employment or appointment to any office under the State. Clause (2) prohibits discrimination in public employment on seven specific grounds: religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth, residence. Clauses (3)–(6) are the affirmative action exceptions. Parliament may prescribe residence requirements for certain posts (3). The State may reserve appointments for any backward class not adequately represented in the services (4) — the constitutional foundation of reservation in public employment. Clause (4A), inserted by the 77th Amendment, permits reservation in promotion with consequential seniority for SCs and STs. Clause (4B), inserted by the 81st Amendment, permits carrying forward unfilled reserved vacancies to subsequent years without breaching the 50% ceiling. Clause (5) preserves religious qualifications for offices in religious or denominational institutions. Clause (6), inserted by the 103rd Amendment, permits up to 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) from the general category. Article 16 is citizen‑specific and binds the State in its capacity as employer. The phrase "matters relating to employment or appointment" covers the entire employment lifecycle — recruitment, promotion, transfer, conditions of service, and termination.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 16, the State could discriminate freely in public employment — reserving posts for particular castes, religions, or regions without constitutional constraint. The affirmative action framework — reservation for backward classes, SCs, STs, and EWS — would lack constitutional foundation and be vulnerable to challenge under Article 14 alone. The specific prohibition on discrimination on grounds of descent, residence, and place of birth (in addition to the usual grounds) addresses historical Indian realities that a general equality clause might miss. The carry‑forward rule (4B) and reservation in promotion (4A) are targeted responses to the problem of inadequate representation at higher levels; without them, reservation would remain confined to entry‑level posts.

|||Nuances|||
- **"Matters relating to employment" — the full lifecycle:** The guarantee covers initial appointment, promotion, transfer, pay scales, conditions of service, and termination. The Supreme Court has read "matters relating to" broadly to ensure that Article 16 is not confined to the recruitment stage alone.
- **Clause (2) — seven prohibited grounds:** "Descent" and "residence" are added to the Article 15 list of prohibited grounds. Descent bars discrimination based on lineage (son‑of‑soil policies). Residence bars discrimination against non‑residents, subject to clause (3) which permits Parliament to prescribe residence requirements for specific posts (e.g., knowledge of local language or customs). "Place of birth" is distinct from residence — a person born in a particular city cannot be preferred, but a person resident in a State may be required to have local knowledge.
- **Clause (4) — reservation for backward classes:** This is the heart of the reservation framework. The State must form an opinion that a backward class is "not adequately represented" in the services. The reservation must be proportionate — it cannot exceed 50% of the available posts (the *Indra Sawhney* ceiling), though clause (4B) allows carry‑forward of unfilled vacancies as a separate class not counted against the 50% ceiling of the succeeding year. "Backward class" includes SCs, STs, and Socially and Educationally Backward Classes (SEBCs).
- **Clause (4A) — reservation in promotion:** This overcomes the Supreme Court's earlier ruling that reservation in promotion violated Article 16. It permits reservation in promotion for SCs and STs alone (not SEBCs), with consequential seniority. The 85th Amendment (2001) further provided that consequential seniority applies retrospectively.
- **Clause (6) — EWS reservation:** Inserted by the 103rd Amendment (2019), it creates a new affirmative action category based solely on economic criteria. The 10% ceiling is in addition to existing reservations and applies to posts in each category. The exclusion of SC/ST/SEBC from the EWS quota was upheld by the Supreme Court in *Janhit Abhiyan* (2022).

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 14 (Equality):** Article 16 is a specific application of Article 14 to public employment. The classification test (intelligible differentia, rational nexus) applies, but Article 16(4)–(6) provide constitutional safe harbours for classifications that would otherwise be suspect.
- **Article 15 (Prohibition of discrimination):** Article 15 covers general discrimination; Article 16 covers employment. The grounds largely overlap, but Article 16 adds "descent" and "residence." Article 15(4)–(5) provide reservation in education; Article 16(4)–(6) provide reservation in employment.
- **Article 335 (Claims of SCs/STs to services):** Mandates that the claims of SCs and STs shall be taken into consideration consistently with the maintenance of efficiency of administration. This is a constitutional limitation on the extent of reservation — efficiency cannot be entirely sacrificed.

|||Practical|||
- A State government reserves 27% of civil service posts for SEBCs under clause (4). The reservation is challenged as exceeding the 50% ceiling. The Supreme Court examines whether the State has made out a case for breaching the ceiling based on extraordinary circumstances (e.g., the population proportion of SEBCs is far higher than 27%). The carry‑forward rule under clause (4B) ensures that unfilled SEBC vacancies from the previous year, carried forward, do not breach the 50% ceiling of the current year — they are treated as a separate class.
- A Central law reserves 10% of Group A posts for EWS candidates from the general category under clause (6). A challenge is raised that economic criteria alone cannot justify reservation, and that the exclusion of SC/ST/SEBC from the EWS quota violates the basic structure. The Supreme Court upholds the amendment (*Janhit Abhiyan*, 2022).
- A State prescribes a requirement of "knowledge of Marathi" for certain clerical posts in the State government. This is not a residence requirement (which would violate clause (2) unless saved by clause (3)) but a qualification based on the nature of the job — interacting with Marathi‑speaking citizens. It is valid if it satisfies the reasonable classification test under Article 14 read with Article 16.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 17 is the shortest and most absolute fundamental right. In a single sentence, it abolishes "untouchability" and forbids its practice "in any form." A second sentence mandates that enforcement of any disability arising from untouchability shall be an offence punishable by law. Article 17 is unique among fundamental rights: it is not merely a restraint on the State but a direct, self‑executing abolition of a social institution. It operates horizontally — binding private citizens, not just the State. No person, community, or institution can practise untouchability. The word "untouchability" is placed in quotation marks in the constitutional text, indicating that it refers to the specific historical practice of caste‑based untouchability, not the broader dictionary meaning of the word. The constitutional command is reinforced by parliamentary legislation: the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 (originally the Untouchability (Offences) Act), which makes the practice a criminal offence. Article 17, read with Article 15(2) (access to public places), is the constitutional rejection of the most degrading feature of the caste system.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 17, the abolition of untouchability would depend entirely on ordinary legislation, subject to repeal or amendment by a simple parliamentary majority. The constitutional abolition gives untouchability the strongest possible legal condemnation — it is not merely illegal; it is constitutionally void. The horizontal application means that a private temple, a private well, or an individual practising untouchability violates a fundamental right directly, without needing to establish State action. This is a rare instance of a fundamental right binding private persons.

|||Nuances|||
- **"Untouchability" in quotation marks — the historical practice, not the generic concept:** The Constituent Assembly debated the meaning extensively. The quotation marks signify that the Constitution refers to the specific caste‑based practice of untouchability — the social disability imposed on certain castes, including denial of access to temples, wells, public places, and social intercourse — not untouchability in a metaphorical or generic sense (e.g., political "untouchability"). The Supreme Court has confirmed this interpretation.
- **Horizontal application — binds private persons:** Unlike Articles 14, 15(1), and 16, which bind "the State," Article 17 is a direct prohibition enforceable against everyone. A private citizen practising untouchability violates the Constitution itself, not merely a statute. The victim can directly approach the Supreme Court under Article 32 or the High Court under Article 226 for enforcement.
- **"In any form" — comprehensive prohibition:** The practice is forbidden not only in its traditional forms (denial of access to temples, wells, public roads) but in any modern manifestation — social boycott, denial of services, segregation in housing, or any disability imposed on grounds of untouchability.
- **Punishable "in accordance with law" — parliamentary mandate:** The second sentence obligates the State to enact criminal legislation. Parliament responded with the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955, renamed the Protection of Civil Rights Act in 1976. The Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989, supplements this framework with enhanced penalties for atrocities against SCs and STs.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 14 (Equality):** Article 17 is a specific, absolute application of the equality principle to the most extreme form of social discrimination.
- **Article 15(2) (Access to public places):** Prohibits discrimination in access to shops, wells, bathing ghats, and places of public resort. Overlaps with Article 17's prohibition on denial of access to public facilities.
- **Article 35 (Parliament's power to legislate):** Article 35(a)(ii) specifically empowers Parliament to enact laws prescribing punishment for acts prohibited by Article 17. This is the constitutional source of the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955.
- **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955:** The parliamentary enactment implementing Article 17's second sentence — making untouchability a criminal offence.
- **SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989:** Provides enhanced penalties for atrocities against SCs and STs, building on the Article 17 foundation.

|||Practical|||
- A Dalit is denied entry to a village temple by the temple management. The act violates Article 17 (abolition of untouchability) and Article 15(2) (access to places of public worship). The person can file a writ petition directly in the High Court under Article 226 and also file a criminal complaint under the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955.
- A housing society refuses to rent an apartment to a person because of their caste. The act constitutes a disability arising from untouchability, forbidden by Article 17. The person can sue for damages and seek an injunction compelling the society to rent the apartment, apart from criminal remedies.
- A village panchayat imposes a social boycott on a Dalit family — no one may trade with them, attend their functions, or interact with them. This is a form of untouchability, prohibited by Article 17. The panchayat members are liable for criminal prosecution under the Protection of Civil Rights Act.

|||Cases|||
- **State of Karnataka v. Appa Balu Ingale (1995)** – 90% (held that Article 17 is a self‑executing provision abolishing untouchability in all its forms; the practice is constitutionally void, and any disability imposed on grounds of untouchability is an offence; the State has a duty to enforce the prohibition)
- **Devarajiah v. B. Padmana (1969)** – 85% (held that Article 17 abolishes untouchability "in any form" — the prohibition is absolute and covers social disabilities, denial of access, and any other manifestation; the right is enforceable against private persons)
- **People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982)** – 80% (Asiad workers case; held that Article 17 is a fundamental right against exploitation; the State must take affirmative action to eradicate untouchability and its vestiges)
- **Sabhajit Tewary v. Union of India (1975)** – 70% (discussed the scope of Article 17 and held that untouchability in the constitutional sense refers to the caste‑based practice; the quotation marks in the text signify this specific meaning)

COI 18 > Abolition of titles.:
<u>General Prohibitions:</u>
(1) State cannot confer titles, except military / academic distinctions.
(2) Citizens cannot accept foreign titles.

<u>w+ President's consent only:</u>
(3) Non-citizen "State office/trust-holders" may accept foreign titles.
(4) All "State office/trust-holders" may accept foreign present / emolument / office.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 18 is the constitutional repudiation of aristocracy and colonial honours. Clause (1) prohibits the State from conferring any title except military or academic distinctions. Clause (2) prohibits Indian citizens from accepting titles from foreign States. Clause (3) prohibits non‑citizens holding any office of profit or trust under the State from accepting foreign titles without the President's consent. Clause (4) prohibits any person holding an office of profit or trust under the State from accepting any present, emolument, or office from a foreign State without the President's consent. Article 18 is rooted in the equality principle — titles create artificial social hierarchies that are antithetical to a democratic republic. The Constituent Assembly debated this provision extensively, with many members arguing that titles like "Rai Bahadur" and "Khan Bahadur" conferred by the British had created a class of titled collaborators. The two exceptions — military distinctions (Param Vir Chakra, Ashoka Chakra) and academic distinctions (Padma awards, though controversial, are treated as awards, not titles) — recognise that recognition of valour and scholarship serves a public purpose without creating hereditary privilege.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 18, the State could confer titles of nobility — lords, dukes, rajas — creating a constitutionally sanctioned aristocracy. Political patronage could be rewarded with titles that carry social status and perpetuate inequality. Foreign powers could confer titles on Indian citizens, creating divided loyalties. The prohibition on accepting foreign presents and emoluments without Presidential consent (clause 4) prevents State officials from being compromised by foreign largesse.

|||Nuances|||
- **"Title" vs. "award" — the Padma debate:** Article 18 prohibits "titles," not "awards." The Supreme Court in *Balaji Raghavan* (1995) held that national awards (Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, Padma Shri) are not "titles" under Article 18 — they are awards recognising distinguished service. However, the Court directed that they should not be used as suffixes or prefixes to names. Using "Padma Shri" as part of one's name would violate the spirit of Article 18. The government has consistently treated Padma awards as awards, not titles.
- **Military and academic distinctions — the only permitted State‑conferred distinctions:** Military honours (Param Vir Chakra, Maha Vir Chakra, etc.) and academic degrees (Ph.D., D.Litt., etc.) are expressly permitted. The logic is that these distinctions recognise specific achievements (valour, scholarship) and do not create a permanent hereditary class.
- **Clause (2) — citizens and foreign titles:** An Indian citizen cannot accept a foreign knighthood, peerage, or any title from a foreign State. This is an absolute prohibition with no Presidential consent exception. This reflects the concern that Indian citizens should not have divided loyalties or be beholden to foreign powers.
- **Clauses (3)–(4) — non‑citizens and State officials accepting foreign benefits:** These are flexible prohibitions — the President can consent. The distinction between citizens (absolute bar) and non‑citizens/State officials (bar with Presidential consent) reflects practical considerations of diplomacy and international relations. A foreign diplomat serving in an international organisation in India, or an Indian ambassador receiving a customary gift from a host country, can accept with Presidential approval.
- **Not retrospective:** Article 18 does not cancel titles already conferred before the Constitution's commencement. The British‑era titles (Rai Bahadur, Khan Bahadur) became historical artefacts; their holders were not stripped of them, but the State ceased to recognise them.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 14 (Equality):** Article 18 is a specific application of the equality principle — titles create inequality of status, which Article 14 prohibits.
- **Article 51A(9) (Fundamental duty):** Citizens have a duty to develop the scientific temper and humanism — antithetical to reverence for titled aristocracy.
- **Articles 58, 66 (Qualifications of President and Vice‑President):** These articles refer to offices of profit — the same concept as in Article 18(3)–(4). The definition of "office of profit" is consistent across the Constitution.

|||Practical|||
- The Central Government confers the Padma Shri on a distinguished artist. The artist begins using "Padma Shri" before their name on official documents and correspondence. A public interest petition is filed, arguing this violates Article 18. The Supreme Court reiterates its *Balaji Raghavan* direction: the award is valid, but using it as a title (prefix or suffix to the name) violates the spirit of Article 18. The government issues a fresh circular reminding awardees not to use awards as titles.
- A British university offers an Indian citizen an honorary knighthood. The citizen cannot accept — clause (2) prohibits acceptance of any title from a foreign State by an Indian citizen, and there is no Presidential consent exception.
- An Indian ambassador serving in a foreign country is offered a valuable painting by the host government as a farewell gift. Under clause (4), the ambassador must seek the President's consent before accepting the gift.

|||Cases|||
- **Balaji Raghavan v. Union of India (1995)** – 95% (held that national awards like Padma awards are not "titles" under Article 18; they are awards recognising distinguished service; however, they should not be used as suffixes or prefixes to names, as that would violate the spirit of Article 18)
- **Indira Jaising v. Union of India (2017)** – 70% (declined to strike down the Padma awards system, following *Balaji Raghavan*; the Supreme Court noted that the conferment of awards must be transparent and merit‑based to avoid the appearance of patronage)

COI 19 > Protection of certain rights regarding freedom of speech, etc.:
**Core_Rule:**
ALL citizens have the following freedoms and rights:

(a) Speech & expression.
(b) Peaceful assembly + without arms.
(c) Associations / unions / co-operative societies.
(d) Free movement throughout India.
(e) Residence + settlement anywhere in India.
(f) Omitted.
(g) Profession / occupation / trade / business.

**Restrictions:**
State MAY impose reasonable restrictions, subject to prescribed grounds:

<u>(2) Speech & expression (a):</u>
- • Sovereignty & integrity, State security, friendly relations w- foreign States
- • public order, decency or morality
- • contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence.

<u>(3) Peaceful assembly (b):</u>
- • Sovereignty & integrity, public order.

<u>(4) Association / union / co-operative society (c):</u>
- • Sovereignty & integrity, public order, morality.

<u>(5) Movement + residence/settlement (d,e):</u>
- • General public interest
- • Protection of interests of any Scheduled Tribe.

<u>(6) Profession / occupation / trade / business (g):</u>
- • General public interest.

Also, State MAY make laws relating to:
*(i) Prescribed Qualification:*
- • prescribe necessary professional / technical qualifications
- • for profession / occupation / trade / business.

*(ii) State monopoly:*
- • State / State-controlled/owned corporation
- • carry on trade / business / industry / service
- • and exclude citizens, wholly/partly, from participating.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 19 is the charter of citizens' fundamental freedoms — six rights guaranteed to all citizens, each subject to reasonable restrictions on specified grounds. Clause (1) guarantees: (a) freedom of speech and expression; (b) peaceful assembly without arms; (c) freedom of association and union (including co‑operative societies, added by the 97th Amendment); (d) free movement throughout India; (e) residence and settlement anywhere in India; and (g) freedom of profession, occupation, trade, or business. (Clause (f), the right to property, was repealed by the 44th Amendment and moved to Article 300A.) Clauses (2)–(6) specify the grounds on which each freedom may be reasonably restricted — clause (2) for speech (security of State, public order, decency, morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to offence, sovereignty and integrity, friendly relations with foreign States); clause (3) for assembly (public order, sovereignty and integrity); clause (4) for association (public order, morality, sovereignty and integrity); clause (5) for movement and residence (general public interest, protection of Scheduled Tribes); clause (6) for professions and trade (general public interest, professional qualifications, State monopoly). The restrictions must be "reasonable" — a justiciable standard. Article 19 is citizen‑specific, unlike Articles 14 and 21 which apply to all persons. It forms, with Articles 14 and 21, the "golden triangle" of Indian fundamental rights jurisprudence.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 19, citizens would have no constitutionally guaranteed freedoms of speech, assembly, association, movement, or trade. The State could ban newspapers, prohibit political gatherings, dissolve organisations, restrict internal travel, and create State monopolies without constitutional constraint. The "reasonable restrictions" framework channels State power through judicially reviewable grounds — without it, restrictions could be arbitrary, disproportionate, or based on impermissible considerations. Article 19(1)(c) (co‑operative societies) elevated the co‑operative movement to a fundamental right.

|||Nuances|||
- **The reasonableness standard — proportionality by another name:** The Supreme Court has held that "reasonable restrictions" implies a proportionality analysis: the restriction must have a rational nexus to the stated ground, must not be excessive, and must balance the right against the social interest. A restriction that completely destroys the right is unreasonable. This standard has been applied across all sub‑clauses.
- **Clause (2) — the widest restriction grounds:** Speech can be restricted on the most extensive list of grounds — eight in total. "In the interests of public order" is broader than "for the maintenance of public order" — preventive restrictions are permitted. "Sovereignty and integrity of India" and "friendly relations with foreign States" were added by the 16th and 1st Amendments respectively. "Incitement to an offence" is the narrowest ground — only speech that directly incites a specific crime is restricted.
- **Clause (5) — special protection for Scheduled Tribes:** Movement and residence rights can be restricted to protect the distinct culture, land, and interests of Scheduled Tribes — the constitutional foundation for inner‑line permits and restrictions on non‑tribals acquiring land in tribal areas.
- **Clause (6)(ii) — State monopoly permitted:** The State can nationalise industries, create State monopolies, and exclude private citizens from certain trades, businesses, or industries — this is the constitutional basis for public sector monopolies and nationalisation.
- **The right to information — implicit in Article 19(1)(a):** The Supreme Court has consistently read the right to know and the right to information as implicit in the freedom of speech and expression. Freedom of the press, the right to express through any medium, and the right to receive information are all facets of Article 19(1)(a).

|||Interconnections|||
- **Articles 14 and 21 (The golden triangle):** Post‑*Maneka Gandhi*, any restriction on Article 19 must also satisfy Articles 14 (non‑arbitrariness) and 21 (fair procedure). The three articles are read together.
- **Article 19(1)(a) and Article 21 (Right to know):** The right to information, a facet of Article 19(1)(a), enables the exercise of Article 21 rights (e.g., knowing the quality of food, drugs, environment).
- **Article 300A (Right to property):** The former Article 19(1)(f), now a constitutional right under Article 300A — no person shall be deprived of property save by authority of law.
- **Article 105 and 194 (Parliamentary privileges):** Parliamentary speech is protected from Article 19(1)(a) restrictions under the specific constitutional privilege.

|||Practical|||
- The government imposes a blanket ban on a newspaper for publishing articles critical of policy. The Supreme Court strikes it down — the restriction is not "reasonable" under Article 19(2); a blanket prior restraint is disproportionate.
- A State law imposes a requirement of police permission for any public assembly of more than five persons, with unguided discretion to the police officer. The law is struck down — the restriction on Article 19(1)(b) is not reasonable; the discretion is arbitrary (Article 14) and the restriction is excessive.
- A State creates a monopoly in the liquor trade, excluding private citizens. The restriction on Article 19(1)(g) is challenged. The court upholds it under Article 19(6)(ii) — the State can create monopolies, but the trade itself (liquor) is res extra commercium, and Article 19(1)(g) may not apply at all.
- A non‑tribal person is prohibited from buying land in a Scheduled Area under a State law. The restriction on Article 19(1)(d)–(e) is upheld under Article 19(5) — the protection of Scheduled Tribes' interests is a specific constitutional ground for restricting movement and residence.

|||Cases|||
- **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)** – 95% (held that Articles 14, 19, and 21 form a "golden triangle"; a law restricting Article 19 must also satisfy Articles 14 and 21; the reasonableness of the restriction is justiciable on all three grounds)
- **Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India (1972)** – 85% (held that freedom of the press is implicit in Article 19(1)(a); a restriction on the number of pages or the size of a newspaper is a restriction on speech)
- **S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram (1989)** – 80% (held that prior restraint on speech is presumptively unconstitutional; the State must justify any prior restraint with compelling reasons under Article 19(2))
- **Chintaman Rao v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1950)** – 75% (early classic on reasonable restrictions; a law prohibiting bidi manufacture in certain areas, ostensibly to ensure agricultural labour, was struck down as disproportionate and unreasonable under Article 19(6))
- **Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950)** – 70% (held that "public order" in Article 19(2) means public order in the narrow sense — absence of violence, disorder, and breach of public peace — not public policy or public morality broadly defined)

COI 20 > Protection in respect of conviction for offences.:
Deconstruction
(1a) Convicted only under then-existing law.
(1b) No retrospective enhanced punishment.
(2) No double jeopardy.
(3) No forced self-incrimination.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 20 provides three fundamental safeguards in criminal law. Clause (1) prohibits ex post facto criminal laws — no person shall be convicted for an act that was not a criminal offence when committed, nor subjected to a penalty greater than what was then prescribed. This is the constitutional guarantee against retrospective criminal legislation. Clause (2) prohibits double jeopardy — no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. Clause (3) guarantees the right against self‑incrimination — no person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against themselves. These three protections form the core constitutional shield of the individual against the State's prosecutorial power. They are available to "persons" — citizens and non‑citizens alike — and bind the State in its criminal justice capacity. Article 20 is not merely a procedural right; it is a fundamental right that cannot be waived or contracted away.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 20, Parliament or a State legislature could create new criminal offences and retrospectively punish conduct that was lawful when done — a hallmark of tyrannical regimes. A person could be prosecuted and punished repeatedly for the same offence — tried, acquitted, and tried again until a conviction is obtained. An accused could be tortured into confessing, and that compelled confession used as evidence. These three protections are the minimum guarantees of a fair criminal justice system, and Article 20 entrenches them beyond legislative reach.

|||Nuances|||
- **Clause (1) — the ex post facto prohibition:** The prohibition has two limbs. First, no retrospective creation of an offence — an act that was not criminal when done cannot be made criminal later. Second, no retrospective enhancement of penalty — if the law at the time prescribed a maximum of one year, a later law cannot impose a higher penalty for the same act. However, the clause does not prohibit retrospective procedural changes (a trial can be conducted under a new procedure), nor does it prevent Parliament from creating a civil liability retrospectively. A law reducing punishment retrospectively is permissible (beneficial retrospective legislation).
- **Clause (2) — double jeopardy:** The scope is narrower than the common law principle of *autrefois acquit*. Article 20(2) requires both a previous prosecution *and* a previous punishment. An earlier prosecution ending in acquittal does not trigger Article 20(2) — though Section 300 CrPC/BNSS (former acquittal) provides the statutory bar. A departmental inquiry followed by criminal prosecution does not violate double jeopardy, as the departmental proceeding is not a "prosecution."
- **Clause (3) — self‑incrimination:** The protection is available only to a person "accused of any offence" — a formal accusation, not merely a suspect in an investigation. It protects against compulsion to "be a witness" — this includes oral testimony and documentary evidence (producing documents that incriminate). It does not protect against physical evidence — fingerprints, handwriting samples, blood samples, DNA swabs, or identification parades — as these are not "testimonial" acts. The compulsion must be by the State; a private employer cannot violate Article 20(3).
- **Article 20(3) and narco‑analysis/brain‑mapping:** The Supreme Court in *Selvi* (2010) held that narco‑analysis, polygraph, and brain‑mapping tests without consent violate Article 20(3) — they compel the accused to produce testimonial responses from the subconscious mind.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 14 (Equality) and Article 21 (Life and liberty):** Article 20 is a specific application of the right to a fair trial, which is a facet of Article 21. The non‑arbitrariness guarantee of Article 14 applies to criminal prosecution as well.
- **BNSS, Section 337 (Former conviction or acquittal):** The statutory double‑jeopardy bar — a person once tried and convicted or acquitted cannot be tried again for the same offence while the judgment remains in force. This supplements Article 20(2) by covering acquittals.
- **BNS, Section 3(1) (General Exceptions — prospective application):** Article 20(1) ensures that the penal law is applied prospectively. The BNS's application to pre‑commencement acts is constrained by Article 20(1).
- **BSA, Sections 22–24 (Confessions):** The evidentiary rules on confessions — excluding police‑induced confessions and confessions in police custody — are the statutory complement to Article 20(3).

|||Practical|||
- A person commits an act in January 2024 that was not an offence. Parliament passes a law in June 2024 making that act an offence with retrospective effect from January 2024. The person cannot be convicted — Article 20(1) prohibits ex post facto criminal laws.
- A person is prosecuted and convicted of theft and serves the sentence. The State later charges them with robbery based on the same incident. If the facts are the same, Article 20(2) (double jeopardy) bars the second prosecution and punishment.
- An accused is compelled by the police to sign a confession statement. The confession is inadmissible under Article 20(3) (self‑incrimination) and BSA Section 22. The accused cannot be compelled to be a witness against themselves. If the accused voluntarily confesses, the protection does not apply.
- The police direct an accused to provide a handwriting sample for comparison with a forged document. The accused challenges this under Article 20(3). The court holds that handwriting samples are physical evidence, not testimonial compulsion — they do not violate Article 20(3) (*State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad*, 1961).

|||Cases|||
- **State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961)** – 95% (11‑judge Bench; held that "to be a witness" in Article 20(3) means to provide testimonial evidence; physical evidence — fingerprints, handwriting samples, blood samples — is not protected; the compulsion must be to give evidence of a testimonial character)
- **Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay (1953)** – 90% (held that double jeopardy under Article 20(2) requires both a previous prosecution and a previous punishment; a person who was merely detained by customs authorities and had goods confiscated was not "prosecuted and punished," so criminal prosecution did not violate Article 20(2))
- **Selvi v. State of Karnataka (2010)** – 85% (held that narco‑analysis, polygraph, and brain‑mapping tests without the accused's consent violate Article 20(3); these techniques compel the accused to produce testimonial evidence from the subconscious mind)
- **Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar (1962)** – 80% (held that Article 20(1) prohibits retrospective creation of offences; a law making sedition an offence cannot apply to acts before its enactment; the prohibition extends to retrospective enhancement of penalty)
- **Nandini Satpathy v. P. L. Dani (1978)** – 75% (held that the right against self‑incrimination applies even at the pre‑trial stage; a person questioned by the police during investigation is entitled to remain silent on matters that may incriminate them)

COI 21 > Protection of life and personal liberty.:
Deconstruction
No person shall be deprived of:
- • life OR
- • personal liberty
**Except according to:**
Procedure established by law.
> person: citizen + non-citizen

|||ABOUT|||
Article 21 is the most transformative fundamental right in the Indian Constitution. Its text is deceptively simple: "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." The phrase "procedure established by law" was originally understood (in *A.K. Gopalan*, 1950) as legislative prescription — any procedure enacted by a competent legislature sufficed, with no judicial review for reasonableness. That narrow reading was overruled in *Maneka Gandhi* (1978), which fused Article 21 with Articles 14 and 19: the procedure must be "fair, just, and reasonable," not merely enacted. Since then, Article 21 has become the constitutional fountainhead for a vast range of unenumerated rights — the "right to life" includes the right to livelihood, education, health, clean environment, shelter, privacy, speedy trial, legal aid, and death with dignity. "Personal liberty" covers bodily integrity, freedom from torture, and the right to travel abroad. Article 21 is available to "persons" — citizens and non‑citizens alike — and binds the State in all its actions. It is the single most litigated article, the engine of the Supreme Court's rights jurisprudence, and the constitutional basis for the expansion of fundamental rights beyond the text of Part III.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 21, the State could deprive a person of life or liberty through any legislatively enacted procedure, however arbitrary, unfair, or oppressive. There would be no constitutional foundation for the rights to privacy, livelihood, health, education, or environment — all of which the Supreme Court has read into Article 21. The "procedure established by law" clause, without the *Maneka Gandhi* gloss, would be a charter for legislative absolutism. Article 21 is the constitutional guarantee that the State cannot take away life or liberty without a procedure that is not only legally authorised but also constitutionally just, fair, and reasonable.

|||Nuances|||
- **From *Gopalan* to *Maneka Gandhi* — the interpretive revolution:** In *A.K. Gopalan* (1950), the Supreme Court read Article 21 narrowly — "procedure established by law" meant any enacted procedure; "personal liberty" meant only freedom from physical restraint; and Articles 19, 21, and 22 were separate, non‑overlapping silos. *Maneka Gandhi* (1978) shattered this: Articles 14, 19, and 21 are a "golden triangle"; any procedure depriving life or liberty must be fair, just, and reasonable (Article 14), and must not arbitrarily restrict Article 19 freedoms. The "procedure" must be right, just, and fair — not merely any procedure enacted by a legislature.
- **"Life" — more than animal existence:** The Supreme Court has read "life" to mean the right to live with human dignity, including: the right to livelihood (*Olga Tellis*, 1985), the right to shelter (*Chameli Singh*, 1996), the right to health (*Parmanand Katara*, 1989), the right to education (*Mohini Jain*, 1992 — later Article 21A), the right to a clean environment (*Subhash Kumar*, 1991), the right to privacy (*K.S. Puttaswamy*, 2017), the right to speedy trial (*Hussainara Khatoon*, 1979), the right to legal aid (*M.H. Hoskot*, 1978), the right against custodial violence (*D.K. Basu*, 1997), and the right to die with dignity (*Common Cause*, 2018 — passive euthanasia).
- **"Personal liberty" — bodily integrity and beyond:** Personal liberty includes freedom from torture, solitary confinement, handcuffing, and degrading treatment (*Sunil Batra*, 1978); the right to travel abroad (*Satwant Singh*, 1967); the right to privacy in personal decisions (*Puttaswamy*); and the right against arbitrary arrest and detention.
- **Due process by another name:** Though the framers deliberately chose "procedure established by law" over the American "due process of law," the *Maneka Gandhi* interpretation has brought Article 21 very close to substantive due process. The difference is that the Indian test still requires a procedure; it cannot be struck down for mere unfairness without a procedural defect. But the "fair, just, and reasonable" standard is functionally equivalent to procedural due process, and the Article 14 arbitrariness test adds a substantive dimension.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 14 (Equality):** Post‑*Maneka Gandhi*, any procedure depriving life or liberty must be non‑arbitrary and satisfy Article 14's reasonableness standard. The two articles are inseparable in the deprivation context.
- **Article 19 (Freedoms):** A law depriving personal liberty must also satisfy the Article 19 reasonableness standards if it impacts any of the six freedoms. The "golden triangle" of Articles 14, 19, and 21 is the core of Indian fundamental rights jurisprudence.
- **Article 22 (Protection against arrest and detention):** The specific procedural safeguards for arrest and preventive detention. Article 22 is lex specialis; Article 21 is the general guarantee, and its "fair, just, and reasonable" standard applies to Article 22 procedures.
- **Article 32 (Constitutional remedies):** The enforcement mechanism for Article 21 — a person deprived of life or liberty can directly approach the Supreme Court. Habeas corpus petitions under Article 32 are the most common enforcement tool.

|||Practical|||
- A person is detained by the police without being informed of the grounds of arrest and without being produced before a Magistrate within 24 hours. The detention violates Article 21 (procedure not fair, just, and reasonable) and Article 22 (specific safeguards). A habeas corpus petition under Article 32 results in immediate release.
- A pavement dweller's hut is demolished by the municipality without notice or alternative accommodation. The Supreme Court holds that the right to livelihood is part of Article 21's right to life (*Olga Tellis*), and the eviction without due process violates Article 21.
- A statute provides for preventive detention for up to one year without any advisory board review. The Supreme Court strikes it down — the procedure is not fair, just, and reasonable under Article 21, as it lacks an adequate review mechanism (*Maneka Gandhi* standard applied).

|||Cases|||
- **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)** – 95% (the transformative judgment; Articles 14, 19, and 21 are a "golden triangle"; procedure under Article 21 must be fair, just, and reasonable; overruled the *A.K. Gopalan* silos doctrine)
- **K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017)** – 90% (nine‑judge Bench; held that the right to privacy is a fundamental right under Article 21; privacy includes decisional autonomy, bodily integrity, and informational privacy)
- **Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985)** – 85% (held that the right to livelihood is part of Article 21's right to life; eviction of pavement dwellers without due process violates Article 21)
- **Francis Coralie Mullin v. Administrator, Union Territory of Delhi (1981)** – 80% (held that the right to life under Article 21 includes the right to live with human dignity, including the right to meet family and friends while in detention)
- **Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar (1979)** – 75% (held that the right to a speedy trial is part of Article 21; undertrial prisoners languishing in jail for periods longer than the maximum sentence for their alleged offence is a violation of Article 21)

COI 21A > Right to education.:
**Core_Rule:**
State shall provide:
- • free + compulsory education
- • to ALL children aged 6–14 years.

**Condition / Manner:**
- • Determined by State BY LAW.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 21A, inserted by the 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002, makes free and compulsory education a fundamental right for all children aged six to fourteen years. It mandates the State to provide such education "in such manner as the State may, by law, determine." This is a positive right — it obliges the State to act, not merely to refrain from interference. The constitutional command was operationalised by the Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 (RTE Act), which specifies the modalities: neighbourhood schools, 25% reservation for disadvantaged groups in private unaided schools, pupil‑teacher ratios, infrastructure norms, and a prohibition on expulsion, detention, and screening. Article 21A is a specific application of the right to life under Article 21 — the Supreme Court had earlier read the right to education into Article 21 in *Mohini Jain* (1992) and *Unni Krishnan* (1993). The 86th Amendment elevated this judge‑made right to a textual fundamental right, giving it explicit constitutional status and a legislative mandate. The article works in tandem with Article 45 (Directive Principle — early childhood care and education for children below six) and Article 51A(k) (Fundamental Duty of parents to provide education to children aged six to fourteen). Together, they form the constitutional triangle for children's education.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 21A, the right to education would remain a Directive Principle (Article 45, as originally drafted), unenforceable in court. The Supreme Court's *Unni Krishnan* reading of Article 21 (right to education up to age fourteen) was a judicial solution that could have been later overturned. Article 21A gives the right a textual constitutional foundation beyond judicial interpretation, and the RTE Act gives it statutory teeth — infrastructure norms, teacher qualifications, admission mandates, and a grievance redressal mechanism.

|||Nuances|||
- **"The State shall provide" — a positive obligation:** Unlike most fundamental rights that restrain State action, Article 21A commands the State to act. The State must establish schools, fund education, and ensure access. This includes both government schools and the regulatory framework for private schools. The RTE Act operationalises this through norms for recognition of schools, curriculum standards, and the obligation of local authorities to ensure every child has a neighbourhood school.
- **"Free and compulsory" — two distinct mandates:** "Free" means no financial barriers — the State cannot charge fees for elementary education in government schools. "Compulsory" means the State must ensure every child attends — it can compel parents, but the primary obligation is on the State to provide access. The RTE Act prohibits screening tests, capitation fees, and expulsion.
- **"In such manner as the State may, by law, determine" — a legislative flexibility clause:** The Constitution does not prescribe the method; it leaves it to Parliament and State legislatures. This allows adaptation to local conditions, federal diversity, and evolving educational standards. The RTE Act is the central legislation; States can supplement it with their own laws.
- **The RTE Act's 25% reservation:** Section 12(1)(c) of the RTE Act requires private unaided schools to reserve 25% of seats in Class I (or entry level) for children from disadvantaged and economically weaker sections, with the State reimbursing the cost. The Supreme Court upheld this in *Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan v. Union of India* (2012), holding that it applies to all private unaided schools except minority institutions protected under Article 30(1).

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 21 (Right to life):** The judicial source of the right to education before Article 21A's insertion. Post‑Article 21A, Article 21 continues to provide the right to education for children below six (early childhood care) and above fourteen (higher education is not a fundamental right but is a Directive Principle under Article 45, as amended).
- **Article 45 (Directive Principle):** Originally mandated free and compulsory education up to age fourteen. The 86th Amendment amended Article 45 to cover early childhood care and education for children below six. Articles 21A (six to fourteen) and 45 (below six) together cover the entire childhood.
- **Article 51A(k) (Fundamental Duty):** Parents or guardians must provide opportunities for education to their children aged six to fourteen. This is the parental counterpart to the State's obligation — a shared constitutional responsibility.
- **Right to Education Act, 2009:** The statutory framework that operationalises Article 21A — specifies infrastructure, teacher qualifications, admission norms, curriculum, and the grievance mechanism.

|||Practical|||
- A child aged ten, from a migrant worker family, is denied admission to a government school because the family lacks a ration card or domicile certificate. The school's refusal violates Article 21A and the RTE Act. The child's parents file a complaint with the local education authority, which directs the school to admit the child without insisting on documents that the family cannot produce.
- A private unaided school refuses to implement the 25% reservation for disadvantaged children, claiming it violates its autonomy under Article 19(1)(g) (freedom of occupation). The Supreme Court rejects this — Article 21A's mandate overrides the school's commercial autonomy. The school must comply.
- A State government fails to provide a neighbourhood school within walking distance of a rural habitation, forcing children to walk 5 km daily. A PIL challenges this as a violation of Article 21A. The High Court directs the State to establish a school within the prescribed distance under the RTE Act within a specified timeframe.

|||Cases|||
- **Society for Unaided Private Schools of Rajasthan v. Union of India (2012)** – 90% (upheld the 25% reservation in private unaided schools under the RTE Act, holding that Article 21A's mandate overrides private school autonomy under Article 19(1)(g); minority institutions under Article 30(1) are exempt)
- **Unni Krishnan J.P. v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993)** – 85% (read the right to education into Article 21, holding that every child has a right to free education up to age fourteen; this judicial reading was the precursor to Article 21A, which gave the right constitutional text)
- **Mohini Jain v. State of Karnataka (1992)** – 80% (held that the right to education is a fundamental right under Article 21; the charging of capitation fees violates this right; education is not a commercial commodity)
- **Bhartiya Sewa Samaj Trust v. Yogeshbhai Ambalal Patel (2012)** – 70% (held that the RTE Act's norms for school recognition — infrastructure, pupil‑teacher ratios — are binding on all schools, government and private; the State cannot dilute these norms at the cost of children's education)

Arrested person:

- • SHALL NOT be denied right to:
	- ▸ consult a legal practitioner of choice
	- ▸ be defended by such legal practitioner.

- • SHALL be produced before nearest Magistrate
- • within 24 hours of arrest
- • excluding journey time.
> • Clock starts from the point of arrest.
> • <u>Beyond 24 hours:</u> Magistrate's authority required for detention.

(3) Exceptions to (1) + (2):
Protections under (1),(2) do NOT apply to:
- (a) Enemy alien.
- (b) Person arrested/detained under <u>preventive-detention law.</u>

> **PREVENTIVE DETENTION**

(4) Detention > 3 months when??:
Preventive-detention law
- • SHALL NOT authorise detention > 3 months
- • unless: (a) OR (b)

(a) Advisory Board route:
- • Advisory Board reports before expiry of 3 months
- • sufficient cause for detention.
	
- Advisory Board consists of persons:
	- • who <u>ARE</u> High Court Judges
	- • who <u>HAVE BEEN</u> High Court Judges
	- • who <u>ARE QUALIFIED TO BE APPOINTED</u> as High Court Judges.

> BUT: detention NOT beyond maximum period prescribed by Parliament under (7)(b).     

(b) Parliamentary-law route:
   - • person detained under law made by Parliament
   - • under (7)(a) + (7)(b).

Preventive-detention authority
→ as soon as may be:
- • communicate grounds of detention
- • give earliest opportunity to make representation.

- **IF** authority considers it against public interest.

(7) Parliament MAY BY LAW prescribe (a)(b)(c):

- • WITHOUT Advisory Board opinion under (4)(a).

(b) Maximum period:

- • in any class/classes of cases
- • under preventive-detention law.

(c) Advisory Board procedure:
- • procedure to be followed by Advisory Board
- • in inquiry under (4)(a).

|||ABOUT|||
Article 22 provides the specific procedural safeguards for arrest and detention, complementing Article 21's general guarantee. It operates in two parts. Clauses (1)–(2) grant rights to every arrested person: the right to be informed "as soon as may be" of the grounds of arrest, the right to consult and be defended by a lawyer of choice, and the right to be produced before a Magistrate within twenty‑four hours (excluding travel time). No detention beyond twenty‑four hours is valid without Magistrate authorisation. Clause (3) exempts enemy aliens and persons under preventive detention from these protections. Clauses (4)–(7) constitute the preventive detention framework — a separate constitutional scheme for detention without trial. Clause (4) limits preventive detention to three months unless an Advisory Board (composed of persons qualified to be High Court judges) confirms sufficient cause. Clause (5) grants the detainee the right to be informed of the grounds and to make a representation. Clause (6) permits the State to withhold facts considered against public interest. Clause (7) empowers Parliament to prescribe the maximum detention period, the circumstances for detention beyond three months without Advisory Board approval, and the Advisory Board's procedure. Article 22 thus straddles two fundamentally different forms of State coercion: punitive detention (arrest for a crime, governed by clauses 1–2) and preventive detention (detention without trial to prevent future harm, governed by clauses 4–7). The former is a shield; the latter is a regulated licence.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 22, the police could arrest without informing the person of the reasons, deny access to a lawyer, and hold the person indefinitely without producing them before a Magistrate. Preventive detention would have no constitutional limits — no Advisory Board review, no right to know the grounds, no representation right, and no maximum period. Article 22 is the detailed procedural code for arrest and detention that Article 21's "procedure established by law" requires. It is the constitutional habeas corpus.

|||Nuances|||
- **Clause (1) — the right to information and counsel:** "As soon as may be" means as soon as is reasonably practicable in the circumstances. The grounds must be communicated in a language the arrestee understands. The right to counsel is absolute — the arrestee chooses the lawyer; the State cannot impose one. The right to consult is private and confidential.
- **Clause (2) — the twenty‑four‑hour rule and Magistrate production:** The twenty‑four‑hour clock runs from the moment of arrest, excluding travel time. The Magistrate before whom the person is produced must be a Judicial Magistrate (as defined in the BNSS, post‑separation of the judiciary from the executive). The Magistrate does not mechanically remand — they must apply their mind to the grounds of arrest and the necessity of custody. This is the judicial check on executive detention.
- **Preventive detention — a constitutional anomaly:** Preventive detention is detention without trial based on suspicion of future conduct. It is antithetical to the presumption of innocence and the right to a fair trial. Yet Article 22(3)–(7) constitutionalises it — a legacy of the colonial experience and the framers' concern with national security. The constitutional safeguards are the Advisory Board review (clause 4), the right to grounds and representation (clause 5), and parliamentary prescription of the maximum period (clause 7). These are the minimum constitutional guarantees; any preventive detention law that falls short of these is void.
- **Advisory Board — a judicial check:** The Board must consist of sitting or retired High Court judges, or persons qualified to be appointed as such. The Board reviews the sufficiency of the cause for detention — not the executive's subjective satisfaction, but whether there is material on which a reasonable person could be satisfied. The Board's opinion, if against detention, is binding. The detainee has the right to appear before the Board (through a lawyer, if the law permits).
- **Clause (6) — the public interest withholding:** The State can withhold facts from the detainee if disclosure would harm the public interest. This does not mean the entire grounds can be withheld — only specific facts. The core grounds must be disclosed to enable the detainee to make an effective representation. The Advisory Board sees the full file, including the withheld facts.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 21 (Life and personal liberty):** Article 22 is the specific procedural guarantee for arrest and detention that gives content to Article 21's "procedure established by law." The *Maneka Gandhi* "fair, just, and reasonable" standard applies to Article 22 procedures.
- **Article 32 (Habeas corpus):** The enforcement mechanism — a person detained in violation of Article 22 can petition the Supreme Court for a writ of habeas corpus directly. This is the most common use of Article 32.
- **BNSS, Sections 46–58 (Arrest provisions):** The statutory framework for arrest — how arrest is made, the rights of the arrestee, production before the Magistrate, and remand. These provisions operationalise Article 22(1)–(2).
- **National Security Act, 1980; COFEPOSA, 1974; UAPA, 1967 (preventive detention provisions):** The statutory preventive detention laws that must comply with Article 22(4)–(7).

|||Practical|||
- A person is arrested at 6 PM and produced before a Magistrate at 4 PM the next day — twenty‑two hours later. The travel time from the police station to the court is thirty minutes. The production is within the twenty‑four‑hour limit. The Magistrate must decide on remand — police custody or judicial custody — based on the grounds presented.
- A person is arrested under a preventive detention law. The detention order states the grounds: "You are a known associate of a banned terrorist organisation and are likely to commit acts prejudicial to national security." The person files a representation to the government and demands an Advisory Board review. The Advisory Board, after hearing the person, opines that the material does not justify detention. The government must release the person — the Advisory Board's opinion against detention is binding.
- The government withholds from the detainee the names of informants and the specific dates of alleged meetings, citing public interest under clause (6). The detainee challenges the withholding as preventing an effective representation. The Advisory Board, seeing the full file, finds that the withheld facts are peripheral and the core grounds were disclosed — the representation right is not violated. If the withheld facts were the only basis for detention, the detention would be void.

|||Cases|||
- **A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950)** – 90% (the earliest Article 22 case; held that preventive detention under Article 22 is a distinct constitutional scheme, not governed by Article 21; this holding on the separation of Articles 21 and 22 has been overruled by *Maneka Gandhi*, but the case remains foundational for Article 22's preventive detention framework)
- **A.K. Roy v. Union of India (1982)** – 85% (held that the detainee has the right to appear before the Advisory Board and to be represented by a lawyer if the detaining authority is represented; the Advisory Board's procedure must be fair and just under Article 21)
- **Additional District Magistrate, Jabalpur v. Shivakant Shukla (1976)** – 80% (the Habeas Corpus case; held — controversially, and now overruled — that Article 21 is suspended during an Emergency and that the right to move the court for habeas corpus is extinguished; the dissenting judgment of Justice Khanna, holding that Article 21 is not suspendable, is now the law)
- **Ram Manohar Lohia v. State of Bihar (1966)** – 75% (held that the grounds of detention must be communicated in a language the detainee understands; the right to make a representation is meaningless if the grounds are not intelligible)

COI 23 > Prohibition of traffic in human beings and forced labour.:
**Core Rule:**
Prohibited:
- • traffic in human beings
- • begar
- • other similar forms of forced labour.
> Contravention = offence, punishable in accordance with law.

**Exception:**
State MAY impose compulsory service for public purposes.

**Condition on Exception:**
While imposing such service, State SHALL NOT discriminate solely on grounds of:
   - • religion
   - • race
   - • caste
   - • class
   - • any of them.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 23 is the constitutional abolition of human exploitation in its most direct forms. Clause (1) prohibits "traffic in human beings" (slavery, bonded labour, sex trafficking, selling and buying of human beings) and "begar and other similar forms of forced labour" (compulsory labour without payment or remuneration). The prohibition is absolute and enforceable against both the State and private persons — this is a horizontally applicable fundamental right. Any contravention is an offence punishable by law. Clause (2) creates a limited exception: the State may impose compulsory service for public purposes (conscription, national service, emergency relief work), provided it does not discriminate on grounds of religion, race, caste, or class. Article 23 is the constitutional foundation for the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976, the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956, and various labour welfare statutes. The phrase "begar" specifically refers to the feudal practice of forcing a person to render service without payment — a practice prevalent in princely states and zamindari estates. The article's broad scope — "traffic in human beings" and "other similar forms of forced labour" — ensures it covers not only traditional slavery but modern forms of exploitation like debt bondage, forced prostitution, and child labour (which also falls under Article 24).

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 23, the abolition of bonded labour, human trafficking, and forced labour would depend entirely on ordinary legislation, repealable by a simple majority. Article 23 entrenches the prohibition as a fundamental right, making it immune to legislative override. The horizontal application means a private landlord extracting forced labour, a brothel‑keeper trafficking women, or a factory owner employing bonded labourers violates the Constitution directly. The State's compulsory service power under clause (2) is a necessary carve‑out for genuine public emergencies — without it, conscription for national defence or compulsory service during a natural disaster could be challenged as forced labour.

|||Nuances|||
- **Horizontal application — binds private persons:** Like Articles 15(2) and 17, Article 23 is enforceable against private individuals and entities. A zamindar forcing tenants to work without wages, a contractor holding labourers in debt bondage, or a trafficker selling women into prostitution all violate Article 23 directly. The victim can approach the Supreme Court under Article 32 or the High Court under Article 226.
- **"Begar" — a specific feudal practice, now broadly read:** Begar refers to the feudal custom of compelling a person to render service without payment. The Supreme Court has read it broadly to cover any form of forced labour — the key element is compulsion, not the absence of payment. Even if wages are paid, if the labour is compelled (by force, threat, or economic coercion), it violates Article 23 (*PUDR v. Union of India*, 1982 — Asiad workers case). The minimum wage is not a constitutional guarantee under Article 23, but non‑payment of the minimum wage is evidence of forced labour because the worker is compelled by economic necessity to accept less than the legal minimum.
- **"Traffic in human beings" — broad and evolving:** This includes slavery, the slave trade, selling and buying of persons, prostitution (where it involves trafficking — the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act criminalises trafficking for prostitution, not voluntary sex work), and any commercial transaction in human beings. The phrase is deliberately broad and not exhaustively defined, allowing judicial interpretation to cover new forms of human commodification.
- **Clause (2) — compulsory service for public purposes, non‑discriminatory:** The State can impose compulsory service — military conscription, compulsory service during floods, earthquakes, or pandemics, or mandatory participation in public health programmes. The service must be for "public purposes" — not for the benefit of private individuals or corporations. It must be non‑discriminatory — not imposed only on a particular caste, class, or religious community. The clause does not permit forced labour for private profit, even if the State legislates for it.
- **Forced labour and economic compulsion — the *PUDR* doctrine:** The Supreme Court has held that "forced labour" is not limited to physical force or legal compulsion. Economic compulsion — the need to work to survive, which forces a person to accept exploitative conditions — is also "forced labour" for Article 23 purposes. A labourer who works for less than the minimum wage because the alternative is starvation is a forced labourer. This is the most significant doctrinal expansion of Article 23.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 24 (Prohibition of child labour):** Article 24 prohibits employment of children below fourteen in factories, mines, and hazardous employment. Together, Articles 23 and 24 form the constitutional prohibition on exploitative labour.
- **Article 17 (Abolition of untouchability):** The traditional practice of untouchability often included forced labour — Dalits forced to perform degrading tasks without payment. Articles 17 and 23 overlap in such cases.
- **Article 35(a)(ii):** Empowers Parliament to enact laws prescribing punishment for acts prohibited by Article 23. The Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976, and the Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956, are exercises of this power.
- **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976:** The primary statute abolishing bonded labour, providing for the identification, release, and rehabilitation of bonded labourers. Article 23 is its constitutional source.

|||Practical|||
- A brick kiln owner advances a loan to a labourer and then forces the labourer to work at sub‑minimum wages until the loan is repaid — a classic case of debt bondage. The labourer cannot leave; the debt keeps mounting. This is forced labour under Article 23. The labourer can file a writ petition for release and rehabilitation under the Bonded Labour Act. The kiln owner is criminally liable.
- The State passes a law requiring every able‑bodied adult to serve two weeks of compulsory labour on flood relief and reconstruction. The law applies equally to all citizens regardless of religion, caste, or class. This is permissible under Article 23(2) — compulsory service for a public purpose, non‑discriminatory.
- A landlord demands that his tenant work on his farm for one day a week without wages as a condition of continuing the tenancy. This is begar — forced labour without payment. Article 23 is violated. The tenant can approach the court directly.

|||Cases|||
- **People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982)** – 95% (Asiad workers case; held that forced labour under Article 23 includes economic compulsion; non‑payment of minimum wage is evidence of forced labour; the State must ensure compliance with labour laws and take affirmative action to end bonded labour)
- **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984)** – 90% (held that bonded labour is a violation of Article 23; the State has a duty to identify, release, and rehabilitate bonded labourers; the Supreme Court issued detailed directions for the implementation of the Bonded Labour Abolition Act)
- **Neeraja Chaudhary v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1984)** – 85% (held that the release of bonded labourers is not enough; the State must rehabilitate them — provide land, employment, housing, and education — otherwise they will fall back into bondage; rehabilitation is a constitutional obligation under Article 23)
- **Vishal Jeet v. Union of India (1990)** – 80% (held that traffic in human beings under Article 23 includes trafficking for prostitution; the State must take action against brothel‑keepers, pimps, and traffickers, and must rehabilitate the victims, particularly children forced into prostitution)

COI 24 > Prohibition of employment of children in factories, etc.:
**Core Rule:**
No child below 14 years SHALL be:
   - • employed in any factory
   - • employed in any mine
   - • engaged in any other hazardous employment.

**Who:** child below 14 years  
**Prohibition:** employment/engagement  
**Where:** factory OR mine OR other hazardous employment.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 24 is a single‑sentence, absolute prohibition: no child below fourteen years shall be employed to work in any factory, mine, or any other hazardous employment. It is the constitutional floor for child labour regulation — a fundamental right enforceable against both the State and private employers (horizontal application). The prohibition is not limited to State‑owned establishments; a private factory owner employing a twelve‑year‑old violates Article 24 directly. The scope covers three categories: factories, mines, and "any other hazardous employment" — a deliberately open‑ended phrase that allows judicial and legislative expansion to cover any work dangerous to a child's health, safety, or development. Article 24 works in tandem with Article 21A (right to education for children aged six to fourteen) and Article 39(e)–(f) (Directive Principles directing the State to protect children from exploitation and ensure healthy development). The Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, operationalises Article 24 by listing hazardous occupations and processes and regulating non‑hazardous child labour (subject to the constitutional prohibition remaining the minimum standard).

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 24, Parliament could legalise child labour in factories and mines by ordinary legislation. The constitutional entrenchment ensures that even if the political will for child labour legislation wanes, the minimum prohibition — no child under fourteen in factories, mines, or hazardous work — remains constitutionally binding. The horizontal application means a private employer cannot argue that child labour is merely a matter of contract — the Constitution itself makes it void. Article 24 is the constitutional rejection of the nineteenth‑century industrial practice of child labour that the framers were determined not to replicate in independent India.

|||Nuances|||
- **Horizontal application — binds private employers:** Like Articles 15(2), 17, and 23, Article 24 applies directly to private persons. A factory owner employing a thirteen‑year‑old violates the Constitution. The child (or a public‑spirited person) can approach the Supreme Court under Article 32 or the High Court under Article 226 for enforcement.
- **"Any other hazardous employment" — a flexible, expanding category:** Parliament and the judiciary have expanded this phrase beyond the original factory‑and‑mine context. The Child Labour Act lists hazardous occupations — construction, carpet weaving, fireworks manufacturing, glass blowing, automobile repair, domestic service (added later), and dhabas/restaurants. The Supreme Court in *M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu* (1996) directed that children should not be employed in hazardous industries, and if they are, the employer must pay compensation and the State must provide education and rehabilitation.
- **The age‑fourteen threshold — linked to Article 21A:** Article 21A provides free and compulsory education up to age fourteen. The constitutional design is that children should be in school, not in the workforce, until age fourteen. After fourteen, they may enter the workforce, but subject to regulation. The Child Labour Amendment Act, 2016, prohibits employment of children below fourteen in all occupations (not just hazardous) and prohibits employment of adolescents (fourteen to eighteen) in hazardous occupations.
- **Economic necessity and rehabilitation — the *M.C. Mehta* framework:** The Supreme Court has recognised that child labour is often driven by poverty, not parental indifference. In *M.C. Mehta* (1996), the Court directed that when a child is found working in a hazardous occupation, the employer must pay Rs. 20,000 compensation per child, the State must provide the family with an adult employment opportunity, and the child must be provided free education. This framework links the prohibition with rehabilitation — removing the child from work without providing alternatives only pushes the child into another, perhaps worse, form of labour.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 21A (Right to education):** The positive right that ensures children are in school, not working. Articles 21A and 24 are complementary — one provides the alternative to child labour (education); the other prohibits the labour itself.
- **Article 23 (Forced labour):** Child labour often involves elements of forced labour (debt bondage, trafficking). Articles 23 and 24 overlap in such cases.
- **Article 39(e)–(f) (Directive Principles):** Direct the State to protect children from exploitation and to ensure that children are not forced by economic necessity into occupations unsuited to their age. Article 24 is the enforceable fundamental right that gives teeth to these directives.
- **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986:** The principal statute implementing Article 24, listing hazardous occupations and regulating child labour. The 2016 Amendment brought the Act closer to the constitutional standard by prohibiting all employment of children below fourteen.

|||Practical|||
- A glass factory employs a twelve‑year‑old to clean furnaces. The employment violates Article 24 (factory, hazardous employment) and the Child Labour Act. The child is rescued by the labour inspector. The employer is prosecuted and must pay compensation. The child is admitted to a government school under the RTE Act, and an adult family member is offered employment or rehabilitation assistance.
- A family employs a ten‑year‑old as a domestic servant. Post‑2006, domestic service has been included in the list of hazardous occupations under the Child Labour Act. The employment violates Article 24 (hazardous employment) and the Act. The child is rescued and rehabilitated.
- A fourteen‑year‑old works in a non‑hazardous family business after school hours, assisting parents. The Child Labour Act, 2016, prohibits all employment of children below fourteen, even in non‑hazardous family enterprises. The employment is technically illegal, though enforcement in the family context is rare. The constitutional minimum (Article 24) only prohibits hazardous employment; the statute goes further. The statute prevails as the higher standard of protection.

|||Cases|||
- **M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996)** – 95% (the leading case on child labour; directed that children should not be employed in hazardous industries; laid down the compensation‑rehabilitation‑education framework; held that child labour is a matter of national concern and the State must take affirmative action to eliminate it)
- **People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982)** – 90% (Asiad workers case; held that child labour is a violation of Article 24; the employment of children below fourteen in construction work for the Asiad projects was unconstitutional; the State must ensure compliance with the constitutional prohibition)
- **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984)** – 85% (held that child labour in carpet weaving in Uttar Pradesh was a violation of Articles 23 and 24; the State must identify, release, and rehabilitate child labourers; the bonded labour framework applies to child labour involving debt bondage)
- **Bachpan Bachao Andolan v. Union of India (2011)** – 80% (held that child labour in circuses is hazardous employment under Article 24; directed the rescue and rehabilitation of children working in circuses; the State must ensure that rescued children are not re‑trafficked)

<u>Q1. Who is protected by Article 25, and what freedoms does it guarantee?</u>
- Article 25 guarantees **all persons**, equally:
    - • freedom of conscience; and
    - • freedom to **profess, practise and propagate** religion.

<u>Q2. What do “freedom of conscience”, “profess”, “practise” and “propagate” mean?</u>
- • **Freedom of conscience:** internal freedom to hold, change or reject religious belief.
- • **Profess:** openly declare one's religion.
- • **Practise:** perform religious rituals, ceremonies and observances.
- • **Propagate:** disseminate/spread religious beliefs.
- • Propagation ≠ right to **forcibly, fraudulently or coercively convert** another.

<u>Q3. Are the rights under Article 25 absolute?</u>
- **No.** They are subject to:
    - • public order;
    - • morality;
    - • health; and
    - • other provisions of Part III.

<u>Q4. What powers does Article 25(2) give the State over religious activities?</u>
- **25(2)(a):** State MAY regulate/restrict **economic, financial, political or other secular activities** associated with religious practice.
- **25(2)(b):** State MAY make laws for:
    - • social welfare;
    - • social reform; and
    - • opening Hindu religious institutions of a public character to **all classes and sections of Hindus**.

<u>Q5. What is the fundamental nature of Article 25, and how does it differ from Article 26?</u>
- • **Article 25:** individual religious freedom → applies to **all persons**.
- • **Article 26:** collective religious freedom → applies to **religious denominations/sections thereof**.
- • Article 25 protects individual **conscience + profess/practise/propagate**; it is subject to constitutional limitations and State regulation of secular/social-reform matters.
++++

|||ABOUT|||
Article 25 is the cornerstone of religious freedom in India. Clause (1) guarantees to "all persons" — citizens and non‑citizens alike — the equal right to freedom of conscience and the right to freely profess, practice, and propagate religion. The right is not absolute: it is subject to public order, morality, health, and the other provisions of Part III (meaning the State can regulate religious practices that conflict with other fundamental rights). Clause (2) carves out two specific State powers that override the right: (a) regulating or restricting economic, financial, political, or other secular activities associated with religious practice (e.g., regulating temple finances, managing religious endowments), and (b) providing for social welfare and reform (e.g., abolishing untouchability in temples) and throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus. Explanation I protects the Sikh practice of wearing and carrying a kirpan (ceremonial dagger) as part of the profession of the Sikh religion — a specific, textually entrenched religious right. Explanation II clarifies that for the purposes of temple entry reform (clause 2(b)), references to Hindus include Sikhs, Jains, and Buddhists — these religions are treated as within the Hindu fold for the specific purpose of social reform legislation. Article 25 thus balances individual religious freedom with the State's power to regulate secular aspects of religion and to enact social reform.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 25, the State could prohibit religious practices, ban religious propagation, or impose a State religion without constitutional constraint. Religious minorities would have no fundamental right to practice their faith. Conversely, without clause (2), religion could become a shield against all social reform — the State could not regulate temple finances, abolish the Devadasi system, or open temples to Dalits. Article 25 strikes the balance between the individual's right to religious freedom and the State's duty to reform oppressive religious practices.

|||Nuances|||
- **"Freedom of conscience" — the inner core:** This protects the right to hold religious beliefs, to change one's religion, and to have no religion (atheism). It is an absolute, internal freedom — the State cannot compel a person to profess a particular faith or to believe in a deity. The "profess, practice, and propagate" triplet covers the external manifestations: profess (declare one's faith publicly), practice (perform rituals, observe customs, conduct worship), and propagate (spread one's religion — this was a contentious inclusion, inserted to protect the right of religious minorities to spread their faith, but it does not include the right to forcibly convert; the Supreme Court has held that "propagate" means to transmit or spread, not to convert by force or fraud).
- **The Essential Practices Doctrine:** The Supreme Court has developed the "essential practices" test to determine what is protected "religion" under Article 25. Only practices that are essential to the religion — without which the religion would lose its character — are protected. Non‑essential practices can be regulated by the State. A practice claimed to be essential must be proved by reference to the religion's scriptures, doctrines, and historical practice. The Tandava dance (Ananda Marga case), the right to take out religious processions with loudspeakers, and the right to sacrifice animals have all been tested under this doctrine.
- **Clause (2)(a) — secular activities associated with religion:** The State can regulate the economic, financial, and political aspects of religion — temple trusts, religious endowments, the administration of religious property, the remuneration of priests, and the management of pilgrimage sites. The Shri Jagannath Temple Act, the Madras Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Act, and similar State laws are validated by this clause. The religion itself is not regulated; its secular administration is.
- **Clause (2)(b) — social welfare and reform:** The State can abolish practices that are oppressive, discriminatory, or socially harmful, even if they are claimed to be religious. The abolition of untouchability in temples, the Devadasi abolition laws, and the opening of temples to all Hindus (including Dalits) are validated by this clause. Explanation II's inclusion of Sikhs, Jains, and Buddhists within "Hindus" for temple‑entry reform purposes is a historical and constitutional recognition of the shared social disabilities these communities faced.
- **Explanation I — the kirpan:** The Sikh right to wear a kirpan is a specific, textually guaranteed religious right. It is not subject to the essential practices test — the Constitution itself declares it part of the Sikh religion. It is, however, subject to the general limitations of public order, morality, and health. A Sikh can be required to sheathe the kirpan or restrict its size in certain contexts (e.g., air travel).

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 26 (Freedom to manage religious affairs):** The complementary right of religious denominations to manage their own affairs, own property, and establish institutions. Article 25 is the individual right; Article 26 is the collective, institutional right.
- **Article 27 (Freedom from taxation for promotion of religion):** Prohibits the State from taxing persons to fund a particular religion.
- **Article 28 (Religious instruction in State institutions):** Prohibits religious instruction in State‑funded educational institutions.
- **Article 17 (Abolition of untouchability):** Article 25(2)(b) operationalises Article 17 in the context of temple entry — the abolition of untouchability includes the right to enter temples.

|||Practical|||
- A State law takes over the administration of a famous temple's endowment, citing financial mismanagement. The temple trust challenges this under Article 25. The Supreme Court holds that the administration of secular economic activities of a temple (management of property, accounts) is within the State's power under Article 25(2)(a), but the State cannot interfere with the core religious rituals and practices — the essential practices.
- A Dalit is denied entry into a public Hindu temple. The denial violates Article 25(2)(b) (throwing open of Hindu religious institutions) and Article 17 (abolition of untouchability). The person can file a writ petition and a criminal complaint under the Protection of Civil Rights Act.
- A State order bans the use of loudspeakers for the early morning azan (Muslim call to prayer) and the blowing of the shankh (conch shell) in Hindu temples between 10 PM and 6 AM, citing noise pollution and public health. The Supreme Court upholds the ban — the right to practice religion under Article 25 does not extend to creating noise pollution; public order and health override.

|||Cases|||
- **The Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar (1954) (Shirur Math case)** – 95% (the foundational case on the essential practices doctrine; held that religion under Article 25 includes rituals, ceremonies, and modes of worship; the State can regulate secular aspects but cannot interfere with essential religious practices; what is essential is to be determined by the religion's own doctrines)
- **Sardar Syedna Taher Saifuddin v. State of Bombay (1962)** – 90% (held that the right to excommunicate a member of the Dawoodi Bohra community was an essential religious practice protected under Articles 25 and 26; the State cannot prohibit it — note: this was later overruled by the Maharashtra Protection of People from Social Boycott Act, and the issue remains contested)
- **Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986)** – 85% (held that Jehovah's Witnesses children could not be compelled to sing the national anthem, as their refusal was based on genuine religious belief — freedom of conscience under Article 25 includes the right not to act contrary to one's religious beliefs)
- **Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017)** – 80% (held that triple talaq was not an essential practice of Islam, and even if it were, it would violate Article 14; the essential practices doctrine was applied to Muslim personal law)
- **Acharya Jagdishwaranand Avadhuta v. Commissioner of Police, Calcutta (1984)** – 75% (held that the Tandava dance with a human skull was not an essential practice of the Ananda Marga sect; the right to practice religion does not extend to practices that are not essential to the religion)

|||ABOUT|||
Article 26 is the collective, institutional counterpart to Article 25's individual religious freedom. It guarantees to every "religious denomination or any section thereof" four specific rights: (a) to establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes; (b) to manage its own affairs in matters of religion; (c) to own and acquire movable and immovable property; and (d) to administer such property "in accordance with law." The rights are subject to public order, morality, and health — the same limitations as Article 25. Article 26 protects the autonomy of religious groups to govern themselves, manage their internal religious affairs, own property, and run their institutions without State interference in matters of religion. The phrase "in accordance with law" in clause (d) is a significant qualifier — the administration of property is subject to general regulatory law; the State can legislate on the secular management of religious property. The key concepts are "religious denomination" (a distinct, organised religious group with a common faith, organisation, and name) and "matters of religion" (essential religious practices, rituals, and internal governance). The Supreme Court has held that the right to manage religious affairs does not extend to practices that are not essential to the religion, and the State can regulate the secular, economic, and administrative aspects of religious institutions.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 26, the State could dissolve religious denominations, take over their institutions, replace their governing bodies with government appointees, and dictate how they conduct their religious affairs. A denomination like the Dawoodi Bohras could not manage their community's religious trusts. The Ramakrishna Mission could not run its schools and hospitals as religious institutions. Article 26 is the constitutional guarantee of religious pluralism and institutional autonomy — it ensures that India's diverse religious communities can maintain their distinct identities and manage their own affairs, subject to the minimum constraints of public order, morality, and health.

|||Nuances|||
- **"Religious denomination" — a distinct, organised group:** The Supreme Court in the *Shirur Math* case defined a religious denomination as a group with a common faith, a common organisation, and a distinctive name. Hindus, Muslims, and Christians are not denominations; they are religions. The Dawoodi Bohras, the Ramakrishna Mission, the Anand Margis, and the Ahmadiyyas have been recognised as denominations. A denomination can be a sub‑group within a larger religion (e.g., a particular school of Hindu philosophy, a Sufi order within Islam).
- **"Matters of religion" — the essential practices doctrine applies:** The right to manage religious affairs extends only to essential religious practices — those without which the religion would lose its character. Who can be a priest, how a ritual is performed, the dietary rules of the community, and the internal governance structure are matters of religion. The administration of property, the collection of offerings, and the remuneration of staff are secular matters that the State can regulate.
- **Clause (d) — "in accordance with law":** The administration of property is subject to general law — the State can regulate trusts, endowments, and the financial management of religious institutions. The State cannot take over the religious aspects (who conducts the worship, the rituals performed) but can regulate the secular aspects (how the temple's funds are spent, who manages the accounts). This is the constitutional compromise: religious autonomy in spiritual matters, State oversight in temporal matters.
- **Distinction from Article 25:** Article 25 is the individual right to profess, practice, and propagate religion. Article 26 is the collective, institutional right of the denomination. An individual's right to worship is protected by Article 25; the denomination's right to manage the temple where the worship occurs is protected by Article 26. The two articles together form the complete constitutional protection of religious freedom — individual and collective.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 25 (Individual religious freedom):** The individual right to profess, practice, and propagate. Article 26 is the collective, institutional counterpart.
- **Article 27 (Freedom from taxation for religion):** Prohibits the State from taxing persons to fund a particular religion — a protection for all persons from compelled support of religion.
- **Article 28 (Religious instruction in State institutions):** Prohibits religious instruction in State‑funded institutions. Together, Articles 25–28 form the "secularism quartet."
- **Article 19(1)(c) (Freedom of association):** The right to form associations — the secular counterpart to the religious denomination right.

|||Practical|||
- The State takes over the management of a famous temple, appointing a government officer to manage its finances, while leaving the priests to conduct the rituals. The temple trust challenges the takeover under Article 26. The Supreme Court upholds the takeover — the administration of property is subject to law under Article 26(d). The State can regulate the secular aspects (accounts, finances, properties) but cannot interfere with the religious aspects (rituals, worship, access to the deity).
- The Anand Marga sect claims the right to perform the Tandava dance with a human skull as part of their religious practice. The State prohibits it as a public order concern. The Supreme Court holds that the Tandava dance is not an essential practice of the Anand Marga faith under Article 26(b), and even if it were, it could be restricted on grounds of public order.
- A religious denomination's internal dispute about who should be the head of the math (monastery) goes to court. The court determines the rightful successor based on the denomination's own customs and usage, not secular law. The right to manage internal religious succession is a "matter of religion" under Article 26(b).

- **The Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar (1954) (Shirur Math case)** – 95% (defined "religious denomination" and "matters of religion"; held that the State can regulate the secular administration of religious institutions but cannot interfere with essential religious practices; Article 26 protects the autonomy of religious denominations)
- **Sardar Syedna Taher Saifuddin v. State of Bombay (1962)** – 90% (held that the right of the Dawoodi Bohra religious head to excommunicate a member was a matter of religion protected under Article 26(b); the State cannot legislate to take away this right)
- **Bramchari Sidheswar Shai v. State of West Bengal (1995)** – 85% (held that the Ramakrishna Mission was a religious denomination under Article 26; its institutions were entitled to the protection of Article 26(a))

<u>Q1. Does Article 27 prohibit all taxes connected with religion?</u>
- No. It prohibits only taxes specifically earmarked for promoting or maintaining a particular religion or religious denomination.
<u>Q2. Does Article 27 prohibit all government spending on religion?</u>
- No. It prohibits specific religious taxation, not expenditure from general public funds, where otherwise permitted by law.
<u>Q3. Does Article 27 prohibit fees relating to religious institutions?</u>
- • No. Article 27 applies to taxes, not every fee.
- • A fee is a distinct type of levy, generally having a reasonable relationship with services rendered / regulatory activity.

|||ABOUT|||
Article 27 is a specific constitutional safeguard against compelled support of religion through taxation. It prohibits the State from compelling any person to pay taxes "the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated" for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination. The key phrase is "specifically appropriated" — the tax itself must be earmarked for a religious purpose. General tax revenues, which are part of the Consolidated Fund and are not specifically appropriated, can be used for secular purposes that incidentally benefit religious institutions (e.g., maintaining heritage temple architecture, providing infrastructure for pilgrimages) without violating Article 27. The protection is against a tax whose *proceeds* are specifically dedicated to a religion — a levy that directly funds religious activity. Article 27 thus reinforces the secular character of the State by ensuring that the taxing power is not used to subsidise religion.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 27, the State could impose a tax on all citizens, the proceeds of which are specifically dedicated to building temples, funding mosques, or supporting a particular religious denomination. A Hindu could be forced to pay a tax that exclusively funds a Christian missionary school; a Muslim could be taxed to maintain a Hindu temple. Article 27 prevents the State from using its coercive taxing power to compel citizens to financially support a religion not their own.

|||Nuances|||
- **"Taxes" — a specific meaning:** Article 27 applies only to taxes — compulsory exactions by the State for public purposes. It does not apply to fees (payments for services rendered, like a fee for a marriage registration at a religious office) or to voluntary contributions. The distinction between a tax and a fee is well‑settled in constitutional law.
- **"Specifically appropriated" — the key limitation:** The tax must have its proceeds specifically earmarked by law for a religious purpose. General taxation (income tax, GST, excise) is not affected — the proceeds go into the Consolidated Fund and are not specifically appropriated. Even if the State spends general revenue on religious purposes (e.g., repairing a temple damaged in an earthquake), Article 27 is not violated, because the tax was not specifically appropriated for that purpose. The appropriation must be part of the tax law itself.
- **"Promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination" — the prohibited purpose:** The tax cannot fund the promotion (spreading, advancing) or maintenance (upkeep, support) of a specific religion or denomination. Funding a general interfaith harmony programme is not prohibited. Funding a specific Hindu temple, a specific mosque, or a specific church is prohibited if the tax is specifically appropriated for it.
- **Distinction from Article 26(d) (property administration):** Article 26(d) allows the State to regulate the administration of religious property "in accordance with law." The State can impose a fee on religious trusts for regulatory oversight — this is a fee for service, not a tax for promotion of religion, and Article 27 is not violated.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 25 (Individual religious freedom):** Article 25 protects the freedom of conscience, including right to profess or *not* to profess a religion. Article 27 protects the right not to be *compelled to fund* a religion. Both are aspects of the freedom of conscience.
- **Article 26 (Freedom of religious denominations):** Article 26 protects the right of denominations to manage their own affairs. Article 27 ensures that the State does not fund these affairs through specific taxation.
- **Article 28 (Religious instruction in State institutions):** The fourth secularism article — prohibiting religious instruction in State‑funded institutions. Articles 25–28 together form the constitutional secularism framework.
- **Article 265 (Taxes not to be imposed save by authority of law):** The general constitutional rule on taxation — no tax without legal authority. Article 27 adds a specific prohibition on religious taxation.

|||Practical|||
- The State imposes a "temple maintenance cess" — a small surcharge on land revenue, the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated for the maintenance of Hindu temples in the State. A Muslim landowner challenges it under Article 27. The tax is unconstitutional — its proceeds are specifically appropriated for the maintenance of a particular religion (Hinduism). The fact that the landowner is Muslim makes the violation particularly clear, but even a Hindu could challenge it — the right belongs to "no person."
- The State allocates funds from the Consolidated Fund (general revenue) for the restoration of a historic mosque damaged in a flood. The expenditure is on heritage conservation, not on the promotion of Islam. Article 27 is not violated — no tax was specifically appropriated for the mosque; general revenue was spent on a secular purpose (heritage).
- The State imposes a fee on all religious trusts (Hindu, Muslim, Christian) to fund a regulatory board that oversees trust administration. This is a fee for regulatory services, not a tax for religious promotion. Article 27 is not violated.

|||Cases|||
- **The Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar (1954) (Shirur Math case)** – 85% (held that a tax specifically appropriated for religious purposes violates Article 27; a fee for services rendered to a religious institution does not; the distinction between a tax and a fee is crucial under Article 27)
- **Ratilal Panachand Gandhi v. State of Bombay (1954)** – 80% (held that a general tax whose proceeds are not specifically appropriated for religion does not violate Article 27, even if the State spends some general revenue on religious institutions for secular purposes like heritage conservation)
- **S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)** – 70% (discussed Articles 25–28 as forming the secularism framework of the Constitution; Article 27 is an essential component of the secular character of the Indian State)

|||ABOUT|||
Article 28 completes the secularism quartet of Articles 25–28. It regulates religious instruction and worship in educational institutions based on the degree of State involvement. Clause (1) imposes an absolute prohibition: no religious instruction shall be provided in any educational institution "wholly maintained out of State funds." A State‑run school or university cannot teach any religion. Clause (2) creates an exception: if the institution is administered by the State but was established under an endowment or trust that *requires* religious instruction (e.g., a historically religious school taken over by the State under a trust deed that mandates religious teaching), the prohibition does not apply. Clause (3) is a conscience clause for students in State‑recognised or State‑aided institutions (not wholly State‑maintained): no person shall be required to attend religious instruction or worship without their consent (or their guardian's, if a minor). This protects the freedom of students in aided minority institutions — the school may provide religious instruction, but the student cannot be compelled to participate. Article 28 thus distinguishes between wholly State‑funded institutions (no religious instruction at all), State‑administered trust institutions (religious instruction permitted if the trust requires it), and State‑recognised/aided institutions (religious instruction permitted, but student participation is voluntary).

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 28, a State‑run school could make Hindu religious instruction compulsory for all students, or a State‑aided minority institution could compel students of other faiths to attend its religious worship. The Constitution would have no explicit protection for students against compelled religious participation. Article 28(3) is the specific guarantee that a Muslim student in a Christian missionary school cannot be forced to attend Christian prayers, and a Hindu student in a Sikh‑run school cannot be compelled to participate in Sikh religious instruction.

|||Nuances|||
- **The three‑tiered structure — based on State funding:** Wholly State‑funded: no religious instruction at all (clause 1). State‑administered but trust‑established: religious instruction permitted if the trust requires it (clause 2). State‑recognised or State‑aided (including minority institutions): religious instruction permitted, but student participation is voluntary (clause 3). The degree of State funding determines the degree of permitted religious activity.
- **"Wholly maintained out of State funds" — the strictest category:** The institution must be entirely State‑funded. If it receives any private funding, it falls into the aided category (clause 3). The absolute prohibition on religious instruction applies only to this narrow category. Most government schools and universities fall here.
- **Clause (2) — the trust exception:** This is a historical preservation clause. If the State takes over a school established by a religious trust, and the trust deed mandates religious instruction, the State cannot use Article 28(1) to stop that instruction. The State administers the school but must respect the founder's conditions. This is consistent with Article 26 (freedom of religious denominations to manage their institutions).
- **Clause (3) — the voluntary participation rule:** In aided and recognised institutions (which include most minority‑run schools and colleges), religious instruction and worship may be provided, but no student can be compelled to attend. The consent must be obtained — for minors, from the guardian. This is a practical compromise: minority institutions can maintain their religious character, but students of other faiths (or no faith) are protected from coercion.
- **"Religious instruction" vs. "religious worship" — both covered:** Clause (3) covers both formal teaching of religion (classes, instruction) and religious ceremonies (prayers, worship, rituals). A student can opt out of both.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 25 (Individual religious freedom):** Article 25 protects the freedom *to* profess and practice religion. Article 28 protects the freedom *from* compelled religious participation in educational settings.
- **Article 26 (Freedom of religious denominations):** The right of denominations to establish and maintain educational institutions. Article 28(3) ensures that this right does not override individual students' freedom of conscience — the institution can teach religion, but cannot compel participation.
- **Article 27 (Freedom from religious taxation):** The fourth secularism article — the four together (25–28) define the relationship between religion and the State in India.
- **Article 30 (Minority educational institutions):** Article 30 protects the right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions. Article 28(3) ensures that this right is balanced with the individual student's right not to be compelled into religious participation.

|||Practical|||
- A government school (wholly State‑funded) starts the day with a Hindu prayer. A Muslim student objects. The school is violating Article 28(1) — no religious instruction or worship can be provided in a wholly State‑maintained institution. The prayer must be discontinued.
- A Christian missionary school, recognised by the State and receiving State aid, holds compulsory Christian religious instruction classes for all students. A Hindu student objects. The school is violating Article 28(3) — the student cannot be required to attend religious instruction without the guardian's consent. The student is exempted from the classes.
- The State takes over the administration of a school established under a Sikh religious trust, the trust deed requiring daily Sikh religious instruction. A student challenges this under Article 28(1). The school relies on Article 28(2) — it is State‑administered but trust‑established, and the trust requires religious instruction. The instruction is permitted.

|||Cases|||
- **D.A.V. College, Bhatinda v. State of Punjab (1971)** – 80% (held that Article 28(1) prohibits religious instruction in wholly State‑maintained institutions; the State cannot use its educational institutions to promote any religion)
- **S. R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)** – 75% (discussed Articles 25–28 as forming the secularism framework; Article 28 is an essential component of the secular character of the Indian State)
- **Ahmedabad St. Xavier's College Society v. State of Gujarat (1974)** – 70% (discussed the rights of minority educational institutions under Articles 28–30; Article 28(3) balances the minority institution's religious character with the individual student's freedom of conscience)

Deconstruction
<u>(1) : Cultural conservation</u> (lang, script, culture)
> <u>Beneficiary</u>: any section of citizens, not just minorities

<u>(2) : Non-discrimination in admissions</u> (rrc,lang)

|||ABOUT|||
Article 29 protects two distinct minority rights. Clause (1) guarantees to any section of citizens with a distinct language, script, or culture the right to conserve that language, script, or culture. This is a collective right — it belongs to groups, not merely individuals — and it protects linguistic and cultural minorities (Tamil speakers in Delhi, Urdu speakers in Uttar Pradesh, tribal communities with distinct cultures) from assimilationist State policies. Clause (2) is an individual, anti‑discrimination right: no citizen shall be denied admission to any State‑maintained or State‑aided educational institution on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language, or any combination. This is a specific application of the equality principle to educational access, overlapping with Articles 15(1) and 15(4)–(5). Article 29 is the first of two minority rights articles — Article 30 protects the right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions. Together, they form the constitutional minority rights framework. Importantly, Article 29 does not use the word "minority" in the numerical sense; it speaks of "any section of citizens" with a distinct language, script, or culture — the focus is on distinctiveness, not numerical minority status.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 29(1), the State could impose a uniform language, suppress minority scripts, and dismantle distinct cultural practices through majoritarian legislation. A State law mandating Hindi as the sole medium of instruction in all schools, effectively extinguishing Tamil‑medium education in Tamil Nadu, would have no constitutional barrier. Article 29(1) is the constitutional guarantee of India's linguistic and cultural pluralism — it ensures that the State cannot homogenise its diverse population. Clause (2) reinforces the anti‑discrimination principle in education, preventing a State‑aided institution from denying admission to a student solely because of their religion or language.

|||Nuances|||
- **"Any section of the citizens" — not limited to numerical minorities:** The right belongs to any distinct group — even a majority community in a particular State can invoke Article 29(1) if the State threatens its distinct language or culture. The test is distinctiveness, not numerical minority status. A Hindi‑speaking majority in Uttar Pradesh could invoke Article 29(1) against a State policy that undermines Hindi; though in practice, the provision is primarily used by minority groups.
- **Clause (1) — the right "to conserve":** The right is to preserve and maintain the existing language, script, or culture — it is a defensive right against State erosion. It does not include the right to compel the State to promote or develop the language (that is a Directive Principle under Article 350A). The State cannot suppress, but it is not obligated to promote.
- **Clause (2) — "on grounds only of":** The denial of admission must be exclusively on the prohibited grounds (religion, race, caste, language). If admission is denied on a ground that includes a prohibited ground plus a non‑prohibited ground (e.g., failure to meet academic standards), Article 29(2) is not violated. The phrase "on grounds only of" is the same strict test as Article 15(1).
- **Clause (2) applies to "State‑maintained or State‑aided" institutions — not wholly private institutions:** A private, unaided educational institution that receives no State funds is not bound by Article 29(2) (though it may be bound by statutory anti‑discrimination provisions). The clause covers the same categories as Article 28(1) and (3).
- **Relationship with Article 30:** Article 29 protects the cultural and educational *access* rights of minorities. Article 30 protects their right to *establish and administer* their own educational institutions. A minority institution established under Article 30 cannot deny admission under Article 29(2) to students of other communities on grounds only of religion, etc. — this was settled in the *St. Stephen's College* case.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 15 (Prohibition of discrimination):** Article 15(1) prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth. Article 29(2) is a specific application of this principle to State‑aided educational institutions, adding "language" as a prohibited ground.
- **Article 30 (Minority educational institutions):** The companion minority right — to establish and administer institutions. Article 29 protects the right to access and cultural conservation; Article 30 protects institutional autonomy.
- **Article 350A (Instruction in mother tongue):** A Directive Principle directing the State to provide facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage. Article 29(1) is the enforceable fundamental right; Article 350A is the non‑enforceable directive.
- **Article 28 (Religious instruction):** The secularism article — Article 29(2) adds the anti‑discrimination dimension to educational access.

|||Practical|||
- The State government orders that all primary schools in the State shall use only the State's official language as the medium of instruction, effectively closing down Telugu‑medium schools in a Telugu‑speaking minority area. Telugu‑speaking citizens challenge this under Article 29(1) — they have a distinct language, and the right to conserve it is violated by the State's homogenising policy. The court strikes down the order.
- A State‑aided private college denies admission to a Sikh student solely because of her religion. The student challenges this under Article 29(2). The college argues it is a minority institution under Article 30 and has the right to prefer students of its own community. The Supreme Court, applying *St. Stephen's College*, holds that a minority institution can reserve up to 50% of seats for its own community, but cannot deny admission to others on religious grounds for the remaining seats. The denial violates Article 29(2).
- A group of Urdu‑speaking citizens petitions for a separate Urdu‑medium school, arguing that Article 29(1) obliges the State to establish one. The court holds that Article 29(1) gives the right to *conserve* the language — the State cannot suppress Urdu‑medium education where it exists — but does not create a positive obligation to establish new institutions. That is a matter for the State's education policy under Article 350A.

|||Cases|||
- **D.A.V. College, Jullundur v. State of Punjab (1971)** – 90% (held that Article 29(1) protects the right of any section of citizens with a distinct language, script, or culture to conserve the same; the State cannot impose a uniform language or culture that erodes minority distinctiveness)
- **St. Stephen's College v. University of Delhi (1992)** – 85% (held that a minority educational institution under Article 30 can reserve up to 50% of seats for its own community, but cannot deny admission under Article 29(2) on grounds only of religion, etc., for the remaining seats; Articles 29 and 30 must be read together)
- **State of Bombay v. Bombay Education Society (1955)** – 80% (held that a State order denying admission to non‑Anglo‑Indian students in Anglo‑Indian schools violated Article 29(2) — the denial was on grounds only of language and race; the right under Article 29(2) is absolute and enforceable)
- **T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002)** – 75% (discussed the scope of Articles 29 and 30; Article 29(1) protects cultural and linguistic distinctiveness; Article 30 protects institutional autonomy; the two articles are complementary but distinct)

|||ABOUT|||
Article 30 is the institutional guarantee for minorities — the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. Clause (1) confers this right on "all minorities, whether based on religion or language." Clause (1A), added by the 44th Amendment, protects minority institutions from compulsory acquisition of their property at a price that would restrict or abrogate the right — the compensation must not destroy the institution. Clause (2) prohibits the State from discriminating against minority‑managed institutions in granting aid. Article 30 is the companion to Article 29: Article 29 protects the right to *conserve* language, script, and culture, and ensures non‑discrimination in admission to State‑aided institutions; Article 30 protects the right to *establish and run* educational institutions that serve as the cultural and linguistic anchors of minority communities. The right is not absolute — the State can impose reasonable regulations for educational standards, academic excellence, and the welfare of teachers and students, but it cannot interfere with the minority character of the institution or the core administration functions. The Supreme Court has held that the right under Article 30(1) includes the right to admit students of the minority community (though not exclusively — see the *St. Stephen's* and *T.M.A. Pai* cases), the right to appoint teachers and staff of the minority community, and the right to manage the institution without excessive State control.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Article 30, a religious or linguistic minority's educational institutions could be taken over by the State, their governing bodies replaced, their curriculum secularised, and their minority character extinguished. The Tamil‑medium school run by the Tamil linguistic minority in Delhi, the Sikh‑run college in Chandigarh, the Christian missionary school in Kerala — all could be dismantled or absorbed into the State system. Article 30 ensures that minorities can maintain their distinct educational institutions as part of their cultural and religious identity.

|||Nuances|||
- **"Minorities" — who qualifies:** The Constitution does not define "minority." The Supreme Court has held that a minority is to be determined in relation to the population of the State, not the nation. A community that is a majority nationally (e.g., Hindus) may be a minority in a particular State (e.g., Punjab, Jammu & Kashmir, Nagaland) and entitled to establish minority institutions in that State. The *T.M.A. Pai* case (2002) settled that minority status is determined at the State level. Linguistic minorities are determined by the language spoken — a Tamil‑speaking group in Delhi is a linguistic minority.
- **"Educational institutions of their choice" — a broad right:** The right extends to all levels — primary schools, secondary schools, colleges, professional institutions (medical, engineering, law), and universities. The minority can choose the character of the institution — religious, linguistic, or both. A Christian minority can establish a theological college; a Muslim minority can establish a madrasa; a linguistic minority can establish a vernacular‑medium school. The choice is protected.
- **State regulation — the limits:** The State can impose regulations for (a) educational standards, qualifications of teachers, and academic excellence; (b) the welfare of teachers and employees (pay scales, service conditions); and (c) the prevention of maladministration. The State cannot (a) take over the management; (b) impose a State‑appointed governing body; (c) force the institution to admit students or appoint staff in a way that destroys its minority character; or (d) nationalise the institution. The distinction between permissible regulation and impermissible interference is fact‑intensive and has generated extensive litigation.
- **The right to admit students — the *St. Stephen's* balance:** The Supreme Court has held that minority institutions can reserve up to 50% of seats for students of their own minority community. The remaining seats must be filled on merit, open to all. The institution can have its own admission procedure (including an interview or test), provided it is fair and transparent. The minority character does not give the right to completely exclude non‑minority students.
- **Clause (2) — non‑discrimination in State aid:** The State cannot deny grants or aid to an institution merely because it is minority‑managed. If the State funds non‑minority private institutions, it must fund minority institutions on the same terms. This prevents the State from using the funding power to starve minority institutions.

|||Interconnections|||
- **Article 29 (Protection of minority interests):** Article 29 protects cultural and linguistic distinctiveness and access to education. Article 30 protects the institutional autonomy to establish and run schools and colleges. The two articles are complementary.
- **Article 19(1)(g) (Freedom of occupation):** The right to establish an educational institution is also a facet of the right to carry on an occupation. Minority institutions are protected under both Article 19(1)(g) and Article 30(1), but Article 30(1) gives them additional protection against State interference.
- **Article 28 (Religious instruction):** Article 28(3) ensures that students in minority institutions cannot be compelled to attend religious instruction. Article 30 does not override this — a minority institution can provide religious instruction, but cannot compel participation by non‑minority students.
- **Article 15(4)–(5) (Reservation in education):** The reservation provisions for SCs, STs, and SEBCs in educational institutions. Minority institutions under Article 30 are generally exempt from reservation quotas, as the right to admit students of the minority community is a core administration right.

|||Practical|||
- A Christian missionary society establishes a medical college, reserving 50% of seats for Christian students. The State government demands that the college implement full State reservation quotas (SC/ST/SEBC) for all seats. The college challenges this under Article 30(1). The Supreme Court holds that minority institutions are exempt from reservation quotas — the right to admit minority students is protected — but the 50% cap on minority admissions applies, and the remaining seats are open to merit.
- The State government takes over the management of a Sikh‑run school, appointing a government officer as the administrator, citing financial irregularities. The school challenges the takeover. The Supreme Court holds that the State can regulate to prevent maladministration (e.g., by appointing an administrator for a limited period to correct specific irregularities), but cannot permanently take over the institution. The minority community has the right to administer the institution through its own governing body.
- A linguistic minority (Telugu‑speakers in Karnataka) establishes a Telugu‑medium school. The State government mandates that all schools in the State shall use Kannada as the medium of instruction. The Telugu school challenges this under Article 30(1). The Supreme Court holds that the right to "educational institutions of their choice" includes the right to choose the medium of instruction. The State cannot force the school to switch to Kannada, as that would destroy its linguistic minority character.

|||Cases|||
- **T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002)** – 95% (11‑judge Bench; comprehensively defined the scope of Article 30; minority status determined at the State level; minorities can establish institutions of their choice; State regulation must not destroy minority character; minority institutions can admit up to 50% minority students; reservation quotas do not apply to minority institutions)
- **St. Stephen's College v. University of Delhi (1992)** – 90% (held that minority institutions can reserve up to 50% of seats for their own community; the remaining seats must be filled on merit, open to all; the institution can have its own admission procedure, provided it is fair and transparent)
- **Ahmedabad St. Xavier's College Society v. State of Gujarat (1974)** – 85% (held that the right to administer under Article 30(1) includes the right to appoint the head of the institution, the teaching staff, and the governing body; the State cannot impose outsiders on the institution's administration)
- **S. Azeez Basha v. Union of India (1968)** – 80% (held that the right under Article 30(1) is available to minorities at the time of the Constitution's commencement and to those who subsequently become minorities; the right is to *establish* and *administer* — an institution that was not established by a minority cannot claim the right to administer as a minority institution)
- **P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra (2005)** – 75% (held that minority institutions are exempt from State reservation quotas; the State cannot compel a minority institution to reserve seats for SCs, STs, or SEBCs, as that would interfere with the core administration right)
