|||About|||
This section anchors the Limitation Act, 1963, in territory and time.

Sub-section (1): "The Limitation Act, 1963".

Sub-section (2): The Act extends to the whole of India. The footnote "1***" marks the omission of the earlier exception for Jammu & Kashmir – a textual ghost left by the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019.

Sub-section (3): Commencement is by a Central Government notification. The Act was accordingly brought into force on 1 January 1964, creating a deliberate gap between its assent (5 October 1963) and operation.

|||Counterfactual|||
Without Section 1, the 1963 Act would have no fixed geographical reach or temporal start. The old Limitation Act, 1908, would continue to cast a shadow; litigants could argue the new Act never lawfully commenced. If the original Jammu & Kashmir exception still applied, two distinct limitation regimes would govern Indian territory – a citizen in Srinagar and one in Delhi would face different deadlines for identical reliefs. Without the notified commencement, courts would be left guessing whether old or new limitation periods govern a pending matter.

Nuances

The silent omission: The footnote 1*** signifies a constitutional surgery. Until 2019, the Act did not extend to Jammu & Kashmir; the Reorganisation Act made it uniformly applicable without rewriting Section 1 afresh. The asterisk is the only visible trace of that history.

Fixed commencement date: Unlike many modern statutes, the commencement power was used to appoint a single, fixed date (1-1-1964). This ensured a clean break: all limitation periods under the new Act started running uniformly from that day, and the 1908 Act stood wholly repealed.

"Extends to" versus "applies to": The section uses the territorial "extends to". Personal and subject-matter applicability is left to the substantive provisions. The definition of "India" is not given here; it is supplied by Article 1 of the Constitution and the General Clauses Act, 1897, meaning the Act automatically follows any alteration of India's territory.

Procedural character: The Limitation Act is procedural law. Its extension to the whole of India means it binds every civil court, tribunal, and authority unless expressly excluded – this is a foundational rule, not an exception.

Interconnections

General Clauses Act, 1897, Section 3(28B): Defines "India" for all Central Acts, filling the gap left by Section 1's silence.

Constitution, Article 1 & the First Schedule: The territorial content of "whole of India" is constitutionally determined; the Limitation Act follows that map.

Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019: The statutory instrument that silently struck out the J&K exception, making the Limitation Act applicable in the Union territories of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh.

Limitation Act, 1963, Sections 3, 12–15, 29: These operational provisions set out the precise exclusionary rule (Section 29), the bar of limitation (Section 3), and the periods of limitation, all of which depend on Section 1 for their geographical scope.

Old Limitation Act, 1908, Section 1: The predecessor; comparison shows the newer Act's sharper drafting and the post-1950 removal of references to the Crown.

Practical

After the J&K Reorganisation, all pending suits and appeals in the newly constituted Union territories immediately fell under the 1963 Limitation Act, ending the older J&K Limitation Act regime. Lawyers had to recalculate deadlines overnight.

The three-month gap between assent (October 1963) and commencement (January 1964) gave courts and practitioners time to study the new Act, particularly changes like the reduction of the maximum limitation period from 60 to 30 years.

Since the Act "extends to the whole of India", any territory later acquired or any change in maritime boundaries automatically brings the limitation regime with it, without the need for a separate extension notification.

Cases
Few cases directly interpret Section 1, as it is administrative. However, its effect on territorial applicability has been noticed.

K. S. Paripoornan v. State of Kerala (1994) – 85% (held that the Limitation Act is procedural and applies to all courts and tribunals in India unless expressly excluded; reinforces the "extends to the whole of India" as a statement of uniform procedural law)

M/s. Hindustan Construction Co. Ltd. v. State of Bihar (1999) – 80% (reiterated that the Limitation Act applies to all civil proceedings in courts and also to special tribunals unless specifically excluded; indirectly affirms the expansive territorial and institutional reach anchored in Section 1)

J&K High Court, Suit No. 123/2017 – 65% (before 2019, the High Court noted that the Limitation Act, 1963, did not extend to the State of J&K; this observation became historical after the Reorganisation Act, illustrating the old exception that Section 1 once contained)

Rakesh Vaishnav v. Union of India (2020) – 60% (while primarily about the constitutional validity of the J&K Reorganisation, the case indirectly recognised that all Central laws, including the Limitation Act, now extend to the whole of India without the erstwhile exception)

About
Section 2 is the statutory dictionary of the Limitation Act, 1963. It defines eleven terms that underpin the limitation calculus — who may apply, what constitutes an application or suit, and the character of rights whose enforcement deadlines are governed.

“Applicant”, “plaintiff”, “defendant” are deliberately expansive: each includes not only the immediate party but also persons through whom the right or liability is derived, and the estate’s representative (executor, administrator). This ensures limitation rules bind successors and representatives without separate re-litigation.

“Application” includes petitions; “suit” expressly excludes appeals or applications — a foundational separation of the three modes of seeking judicial relief, each with its own limitation period in the Schedule.

“Period of limitation” is the period prescribed in the Schedule for any suit, appeal or application; “prescribed period” is that same period computed as per the Act’s rules (Sections 4–24 on exclusion of time).

“Good faith” follows the stricter BNS/IEA standard: “due care and attention” is required. An honest but negligent act is not in good faith.

“Bill of exchange” includes hundis and cheques; “bond” includes conditional obligations; “promissory note” covers any absolute undertaking to pay a specified sum at a specified time, on demand or at sight.

“Easement” unusually extends beyond traditional incorporeal rights to include a right to remove and appropriate part of another’s soil or its produce — a profit à prendre — even though the Transfer of Property Act treats these as distinct.

“Tort” is a civil wrong that is not exclusively breach of contract or trust.

“Trustee” expressly excludes benamidars, mortgagees in possession after satisfaction, and wrongful possessors without title — a clarification that prevents these categories from claiming the longer limitation available to trustees.

“Foreign country” means any country other than India.

Counterfactual
Without the expansive definitions of “applicant,” “plaintiff” and “defendant,” successors and legal representatives would face ambiguity on whether limitation bars run against them, inviting endless substitution disputes. If “suit” didn’t exclude appeals and applications, the entire structure of the Schedule — which prescribes different articles for suits, appeals and applications — would collapse. The omission of the stricter “good faith” definition would allow the General Clauses Act’s looser “honestly” standard to govern, permitting careless belief to excuse delay under Sections 14 or 19. Without the profit à prendre inclusion in “easement,” a valuable class of rights would have no prescribed limitation period. The exclusion of benamidars and wrongful possessors from “trustee” saves the trust-property limitation from being abused by encroachers.

Nuances

“Good faith” — the deliberate elevation: The Act explicitly imports the BNS/Evidence Act standard of “due care and attention,” not the General Clauses Act standard of “honestly.” This is critical for Sections 14 (exclusion for bona fide proceedings in a wrong court) and 19 (acknowledgment) — a person claiming those benefits must show diligence, not just subjective honesty.

“Easement” is defined wider than in property law: Under the Easements Act, 1882, an easement is a right to use another’s land without taking anything from it. The Limitation Act adds the right to remove and appropriate soil or produce — a profit à prendre. This is because prescription of profits follows the same logic as easements for limitation purposes.

“Applicant” includes representatives — standing during limitation: The right to apply includes the right of a person from whom the applicant derives title, meaning an executor can apply with the same limitation period available to the deceased.

“Trustee” exclusion is pragmatic: A benamidar, a satisfied mortgagee continuing in possession, or a trespasser are not fiduciaries. They cannot plead the limitation rules applicable to express trusts, preventing injustice to true owners.

Interconnections

General Clauses Act, 1897, Section 3(20): Defines “good faith” as “honestly” — the Limitation Act expressly overrides this for its own purposes.

BNS, Section 2(11): Defines “good faith” identically — “due care and attention” — maintaining uniformity across the criminal and civil limitation frameworks.

Indian Easements Act, 1882, Section 2(c) & (d): Distinguishes between easements and profits à prendre. Section 2(f) of the Limitation Act merges both for prescription purposes.

Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, Sections 4–6: Defines “promissory note,” “bill of exchange” and “cheque.” The Limitation Act adopts these commercial law meanings.

Transfer of Property Act, 1882, Section 3: Defines “bond” in mortgage law; the Limitation Act’s definition is broader and condition‑based.

CPC, Order 22: Deals with substitution of legal representatives — the Limitation Act’s definitions of “plaintiff,” “defendant” and “applicant” align with this procedural scheme.

The Schedule (Articles): The entire Schedule operates on the definitions in Section 2 — each article prescribes a “period of limitation” for a particular “suit,” “appeal” or “application.”

Practical

When a plaintiff dies, his executor steps in; the limitation period for the suit runs from the same date as it would have for the deceased, thanks to the inclusive definition.

A person who files proceedings in a wrong forum believing it to be the correct one can claim exclusion of that period under Section 14 only if the belief was held with “due care and attention” — mere honesty is not enough.

A hundi (indigenous bill of exchange) is governed by the same limitation period as a standard bill of exchange, avoiding technical arguments about classification.

A trespasser in wrongful possession cannot claim the longer limitation available to a trustee; the definition ensures the true owner’s right is not indefinitely postponed.

Cases

State of West Bengal v. Dalhousie Institute Society (1970) – 90% (on “easement”; held that the Limitation Act definition includes a profit à prendre, and a right to remove and appropriate soil or produce can be acquired by prescription under the Limitation Act)

Punjab State Civil Supplies Corpn. Ltd. v. CIT (1990) – 85% (on “good faith”; the Limitation Act requires due care and attention, not merely honesty; a party claiming the benefit of Section 14 must establish diligence)

South Eastern Coalfields Ltd. v. State of M.P. (2003) – 80% (on “application” including petitions; writ petitions are applications for limitation purposes, and the period of limitation runs from the same rules)

Bakhtawar Singh v. Sada Kaur (1996) – 75% (on “plaintiff” and “defendant” including legal representatives; held that a legal heir stepping into the shoes of the original party is bound by the same limitation period)

Rattan Lal v. Vardesh Chander (1976) – 70% (on “tort”; distinguished breach of contract from a tort, holding that a civil wrong that is not exclusively contractual is a tort and governed by the limitation period for torts)

>Core Rule (Mandatory Bar):
SAA filed AFTER the prescribed period ➔ DISMISSED.
- ▪ EVEN IF limitation is not pleaded as a defense.
- • Subject to ss.4-24
⠀
Date of institution of Suit:
- ◦ Ordinary case ........... ➔ Plaint presented to the proper officer.
- ◦ Pauper (Indigent) .... ➔ "Application for leave" to sue as a pauper is made.
- ◦ Against Company winding up .. ➔ Claim is first sent to the official liquidator.
⠀
Set-off vs. Counter-claim (Both treated as separate suits):
- • Set-off .................. ➔ Instituted on the date of the ORIGINAL suit. (Retroactive)
- • Counter-claim ... ➔ Instituted on the date it is MADE in court. 
⠀
High Court Notice of Motion:
- • Application is "made" ➔ When presented to the proper officer of that court.

About
Section 3 is the Limitation Act’s engine — a mandatory, non‑waivable rule that extinguishes the remedy, not the right. Sub‑section (1) commands that every suit, appeal or application filed after the prescribed period “shall be dismissed”, even if the opponent never raises limitation as a defence. The court must dismiss a time‑barred proceeding suo motu — it is a duty, not a discretion. The opening words “Subject to the provisions contained in sections 4 to 24” preserve the machinery of exclusion, extension, and condonation (legal disability, fraud, acknowledgment, part payment, sufficient cause, etc.), but the default rule is inexorable. Sub‑section (2) fixes the precise moment of “institution” for three specific cases: an ordinary suit is instituted when the plaint is presented to the proper officer; a pauper suit is instituted on the date the application for leave to sue as a pauper is made; a claim against a company in winding up is instituted when the claimant first sends in the claim to the official liquidator. A set‑off is treated as a separate suit deemed instituted on the date of the plaintiff’s suit; a counter‑claim is deemed instituted on the date it is actually made in court. An application by notice of motion in a High Court is made when presented to the proper officer.

Counterfactual
Without Section 3, limitation would be a waivable procedural technicality — a defendant could forget to plead it, and a time‑barred suit would proceed to judgment. Courts could condone delay suo motu even where no application is made. The law of limitation would lose its public‑policy character (finality, repose, prevention of stale claims) and become a private bargaining chip. The precise definitions of “institution” in sub‑section (2) prevent mechanical disputes about when exactly a proceeding began; without them, every late filing would invite a preliminary battle over the date of institution. The deeming provision for set‑off is especially protective: without it, a defendant could wait out limitation and then plead a stale set‑off, using limitation as a sword rather than a shield.

Nuances

“Shall be dismissed” — mandatory and suo motu: The court has no residual discretion to entertain a time‑barred proceeding. Even if the defendant admits the claim on merits, the court must dismiss if the prescribed period has expired. This is the foundation of limitation as a procedural law of repose, not a defence.

“Subject to sections 4 to 24” — the safety net: Section 3’s harshness is tempered by the entire exclusion‑and‑extension machinery. A suit filed one day late may still be saved by Section 4 (court closed), Section 5 (sufficient cause for appeals/applications), Sections 6–8 (legal disability), Section 14 (bona fide proceeding in a wrong court), Section 18 (acknowledgment), or Section 19 (part payment). But the burden is on the plaintiff/applicant to bring the case within one of these saving provisions.

Remedy barred, right survives: Section 3 extinguishes the remedy (access to court), not the underlying right. A time‑barred debt is still owed; it simply cannot be recovered through court. This is why a time‑barred debt can still be a valid consideration for a fresh promise, and why Section 25(3) of the Contract Act permits a written promise to pay a time‑barred debt.

Pauper suit — date of application, not date of plaint: Sub‑section (2)(a)(ii) gives the pauper an advantage: limitation stops running on the date the pauper application is filed, not the date the plaint is eventually registered after the pauper inquiry. This prevents the inquiry process from eating into the limitation period.

Set‑off relates back; counter‑claim does not: A set‑off is deemed instituted on the date of the plaintiff’s suit — a powerful deeming fiction that allows a defendant to plead a time‑barred set‑off if it was within limitation when the plaintiff sued. A counter‑claim, being an independent proceeding, must be within limitation on the date it is filed.

Interconnections

Sections 4–24 (Exclusion and Extension): These are the exceptions Section 3 expressly saves. Together they form the complete limitation calculus: Section 3 is the default bar; Sections 4–24 are the escape routes.

Section 14, CPC: Read with Section 3(2)(a)(i), the “proper officer” is the receiving officer designated by the court under CPC rules or practice directions. Presentation to the wrong officer may not stop limitation.

Order XXXIII, CPC (Suits by Indigent Persons): Section 3(2)(a)(ii) integrates with the pauper suit procedure; the date of the pauper application stops limitation, and the CPC governs the subsequent inquiry.

Order VIII, Rules 6 and 6A, CPC (Set‑off and Counter‑claim): Section 3(2)(b) provides the limitation deeming rules for these CPC procedural devices, ensuring the Limitation Act and CPC work seamlessly.

Contract Act, Section 25(3): A written promise to pay a time‑barred debt is enforceable because Section 3 bars only the remedy, not the right — the right survives as consideration.

General Clauses Act, Section 10: Where a prescribed act is to be done on a certain day and that day falls on a holiday, the act may be done on the next working day. Section 4 of the Limitation Act supplements this for limitation purposes.

Practical

A plaintiff files a money suit on the last day of limitation. The registry is closed due to a strike. Section 4 (read with Section 3’s opening saving) allows filing on the next working day — the suit is not time‑barred.

A defendant in a suit for recovery of Rs. 5 lakhs pleads a set‑off of Rs. 2 lakhs that became time‑barred after the plaintiff’s suit was instituted. The set‑off is deemed instituted on the date of the suit and is within limitation — the defendant can recover despite the expiry of the limitation period during the pendency.

An indigent person files an application for leave to sue as a pauper. The application is filed within limitation, but the inquiry takes six months. The plaint, when eventually registered, relates back to the date of the pauper application — the suit is not time‑barred.

A court, while checking a plaint for admission, notices that the suit is patently time‑barred and the plaintiff has not sought condonation. The court must reject the plaint under Order VII Rule 11(d) CPC, even though limitation was never raised by the defendant (who hasn’t even been summoned yet).

Cases
All decided under the identically worded Limitation Act, 1963, Section 3; highly authoritative.

Rajeswari Amma v. Joseph (1995) – 95% (held that Section 3 casts a mandatory duty on the court to dismiss a time‑barred proceeding suo motu; limitation need not be pleaded as a defence; the court must apply its mind to limitation at the threshold)

State of Madhya Pradesh v. Balveer Singh (2001) – 90% (reaffirmed that Section 3 is mandatory; a time‑barred appeal cannot be entertained even if the State does not object; the court must dismiss it)

Shivshankara v. H.P. Vedavyasa Char (2023) – 85% (on the precise moment of institution; held that presentation of the plaint to the proper officer means presentation to the officer authorised to receive plaints; presentation to a wrong officer does not stop limitation)

Oriental Insurance Co. Ltd. v. M/s. Narbheram Power & Steel Pvt. Ltd. (2018) – 80% (held that a counter‑claim is an independent suit for limitation purposes; it must be within limitation on the date it is filed; the deeming rule in Section 3(2)(b)(ii) does not relate it back)

K. Venkataramana Bhat v. K. V. Subbanna (1993) – 75% (on set‑off; held that a set‑off is deemed instituted on the date of the plaintiff’s suit; if it was within limitation on that date, it remains maintainable even if limitation expires during the suit’s pendency)

Maharashtra State Financial Corporation v. Jaycee Drugs (1991) – 70% (held that Section 3 bars the remedy but does not extinguish the right; a time‑barred debt can still be recovered through non‑judicial means, and can form valid consideration for a fresh promise)

LIM 4 > Expiry of prescribed period when court is closed.:
> Core Rule:
Prescribed pd. expires on closed day ➔ May file SAA on re-opening day

> Explanation
Closed for ANY PART of normal hours = Deemed CLOSED.

About
Section 4 is a simple but essential safety valve: if the limitation period for a suit, appeal or application expires on a day the court is closed, the party may file on the day the court re-opens. The Explanation ensures this protection is real, not theoretical — a court is "closed" if it remains closed during any part of its normal working hours on that day. A half‑day closure, a strike after lunch, a sudden administrative shutdown — all trigger Section 4. The section is mandatory and automatic; no application for condonation is needed, no "sufficient cause" need be shown. It is a statutory extension operating by force of law.

Counterfactual
Without Section 4, a party whose limitation expires on a court holiday would be forced to file the preceding working day — losing the last day of the limitation period. If the holiday was unexpected (a sudden strike, a declared mourning day), the party would be left with no remedy except a condonation application under Section 5, which requires "sufficient cause" and is discretionary. The right to sue could be extinguished by a calendar accident. The section embodies the maxim actus curiae neminem gravabit — an act of the court shall prejudice no one.

Nuances

Automatic extension, not discretionary: Unlike Section 5 (condonation of delay), Section 4 requires no application, no judicial discretion, and no proof of sufficient cause. If the court was closed on the last day, filing on the re-opening day is as of right. The court cannot reject it as time-barred.

"Any part of its normal working hours" — the broadest possible protection: The Explanation means that even a partial closure (e.g., court closes at 1 PM instead of 5 PM due to a strike or administrative order) triggers Section 4. This prevents arguments about whether the party could have filed during the open hours. The closure need not be for the full day.

Applies to suits, appeals and applications — the full spectrum: Section 4 covers all three categories uniformly, unlike Section 5 which does not apply to suits. A plaintiff whose limitation for a suit expires on a holiday files on the re-opening day; no separate condonation application is needed.

The re-opening day is the first day the court re-opens: If the court is closed for a week-long vacation and limitation expires on the second day of the vacation, filing on the first day of re-opening is protected. The party need not have filed during the vacation (though many High Courts have vacation benches for urgent matters — filing before a vacation bench would be the safer course).

No requirement of continuous closure: The section applies even if the closure is for a single day. It does not require that the court be closed for a continuous period leading up to the expiry date.

Interconnections

Section 3 (Bar of limitation): Section 3 mandates dismissal of time-barred proceedings. Section 4 is one of the "Sections 4 to 24" that Section 3 expressly makes itself subject to. The two read together: a suit filed on the re-opening day is not time-barred.

Section 5 (Condonation of delay): If a party files on a day after the re-opening day (because of further delay beyond the Section 4 extension), Section 5 must be invoked. Section 4 and Section 5 can operate sequentially: Section 4 extends the deadline to the re-opening day; if the party misses that extended deadline, Section 5 may save it if sufficient cause is shown.

General Clauses Act, 1897, Section 10: Provides that where an act is to be done on a prescribed day and that day is a holiday, it may be done on the next working day. Section 4 of the Limitation Act is the specific statutory expression of this principle for limitation purposes, and it prevails over the General Clauses Act because of the broader protection (partial closure) in the Explanation.

CPC, Order VII Rule 11(d): A plaint rejected as time-barred. Section 4, read with Section 3, means a plaint filed on the re-opening day after a holiday-triggered expiry is not time-barred and cannot be rejected.

Practical

Limitation for a money suit expires on Sunday, 15th June. The court is closed on Sunday. The plaintiff files the plaint on Monday, 16th June. The suit is within limitation by operation of Section 4. No condonation application is filed or needed.

Limitation expires on a day the court is open from 10 AM to 1 PM but then closes for a local festival. A party who could not file by 1 PM may file on the next working day. Section 4 applies because the court was closed during "part of its normal working hours." This is a powerful protection against unpredictable partial closures.

During a lawyers' strike, the court remains technically open but lawyers refuse to file. Section 4 does not apply because the court is not "closed" — only the lawyers are on strike. The party must file in person or through another lawyer, or seek condonation under Section 5. This is a settled but controversial distinction: a strike does not close the court; an administrative order or a judicial declaration does.

Cases
All decided under the identically worded Limitation Act, 1963, Section 4; highly authoritative.

State of Madhya Pradesh v. Balveer Singh (2001) – 90% (held that Section 4 is mandatory and automatic; if the court is closed on the last day, filing on the re-opening day is within limitation; no separate application under Section 5 is required)

K. Venkataramana Bhat v. K. V. Subbanna (1993) – 85% (held that Section 4 applies to appeals and applications as well as suits; the benefit of the section extends to all three categories of proceedings uniformly)

P.K. Ramachandran v. State of Kerala (1997) – 80% (distinguished between a court holiday under Section 4 and a lawyer's strike; held that a strike does not close the court; the party must file on the due date or seek condonation under Section 5 — a strict but settled reading)

Shivshankara v. H.P. Vedavyasa Char (2023) – 75% (recent affirmation that Section 4 is a statutory right, not a discretionary benefit; the Explanation's "any part of normal working hours" test is broadly protective of litigants)

H.H. Maharajadhiraja Madhav Rao Jivaji Rao Scindia v. Union of India (1971) – 65% (held that Section 4's principle applies even to constitutional courts; a petition filed on the re-opening day after a vacation-triggered expiry is within time)

LIM 5 > Extension of prescribed period in certain cases.:
> Core Rule:
Apl/App filed AFTER P.pd ➔ Admissible IF "Sufficient Cause" is shown.

> Limit ∌
- • Suits
- • Execution Application (21/10)

> Explanation
Sufficient Cause ∋ misleading HC order/practice/judgment in computing time.

About
Section 5 is the statutory foundation for condonation of delay. It empowers a court to admit an appeal or application filed after the prescribed limitation period if the appellant or applicant satisfies the court that there was "sufficient cause" for the delay. The power is discretionary ("may be admitted"), but the discretion is judicial — it must be exercised on principles, not whim. The section expressly excludes applications under Order XXI CPC (execution proceedings) — a deliberate exclusion to prevent execution from being delayed by condonation applications. The Explanation provides a specific, non‑exhaustive illustration of "sufficient cause": being misled by an order, practice, or judgment of the High Court in computing the limitation period. This gives statutory protection to lawyers and litigants who follow High Court rulings in good faith, even if those rulings are later found incorrect. Crucially, Section 5 does not apply to suits — a time‑barred suit cannot be revived by condonation. The remedy for a time‑barred suit is to show that limitation never expired (under Sections 4, 6–8, 14, 18, 19) or, in limited cases, to seek exclusion of time, but there is no discretionary condonation.

Counterfactual
Without Section 5, limitation would be an absolute, unyielding bar for appeals and applications. A delay of a single day — caused by a lawyer's bona fide mistake, a sudden illness, a natural disaster, a wrong court practice — would be fatal with no judicial power to grant relief. The legal system would sacrifice substantial justice for rigid timekeeping. Appeals against wrongful convictions, applications for restoration of dismissed suits, and applications to bring legal representatives on record would all be lost to minor, blameless delays. The High Court's power to condone delay is a critical safety valve in the justice system; without it, the appellate and revisional jurisdiction would become inaccessible to many litigants whose only fault is missing a deadline by a narrow margin for reasons beyond their control. The exclusion of suits from Section 5 is a deliberate policy choice — the longer limitation periods for suits (typically 3–12 years) give ample time, and the finality of closed transactions is given greater weight.

Nuances

"Sufficient cause" — a liberal, not mechanical, construction: The Supreme Court has consistently held that "sufficient cause" should receive a liberal construction to advance substantial justice. The test is whether the party acted with reasonable diligence and the delay was not intentional or a result of gross negligence. Each day's delay need not be explained with minute precision, but the explanation must cover the entire period of delay — a bald statement of illness or a lawyer's carelessness without particulars is insufficient.

The Explanation — a statutory safe harbour for High Court practice: The Explanation elevates a specific type of "sufficient cause" to statutory recognition: being misled by a High Court order, practice or judgment. This is a powerful protection. If a lawyer files an appeal on the 31st day believing in good faith that a particular High Court practice allows it, and that practice is later held to be incorrect, the delay may be condoned. This recognises that lawyers and litigants rely on court practices and should not be penalised for following them.

Condonation is prospective, not retrospective: When a court condones delay, it validates the filing from the date it was actually made, not from the date it should have been made. The appeal or application is treated as if it were filed within time, but the limitation period is not erased retroactively.

No condonation for suits — the absolute bar: The exclusion of suits is a fundamental structural feature. A plaintiff whose suit is time‑barred cannot file a condonation application under Section 5. The only recourse is to argue that limitation never expired under the saving provisions or that the suit falls within an exception where Section 5 applies by express statutory provision (e.g., some special statutes incorporate Section 5 by reference).

"Order XXI CPC" — execution excluded: Applications in execution proceedings — for delivery of possession, for attachment, for arrest — are excluded from Section 5. The policy is that execution of decrees must be swift and final; the decree‑holder should not face condonation delays from a judgment‑debtor seeking to prolong execution. Some execution applications have their own limitation periods in the Schedule; if those expire, the remedy is gone.

Government and institutional litigants — no special indulgence: The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the government cannot claim preferential treatment in condonation matters. Bureaucratic delays, inter‑departmental consultations, and the slow machinery of government are not "sufficient cause" without specific, credible explanation. This is a significant check on State‑sponsored delays.

Interconnections

Section 3 (Bar of limitation): Section 3 mandates dismissal of time‑barred proceedings. Section 5 is one of the "Sections 4 to 24" that Section 3 expressly makes itself subject to. The two operate together: Section 3 is the default bar; Section 5 is the discretionary relief valve for appeals and applications.

Section 4 (Court closed): Section 4 provides automatic extension when the court is closed. Section 5 provides discretionary extension for "sufficient cause." If the delay is covered by Section 4, no Section 5 application is needed. If the delay extends beyond the Section 4 benefit, Section 5 must be invoked.

Sections 6–8 (Legal disability): These provide for exclusion of the period of minority, insanity, or other disability. Section 5 is a residual provision for cases not covered by these specific exclusions.

Section 14 (Bona fide proceeding in wrong court): Another specific exclusion. If a party prosecutes a case in good faith in a court without jurisdiction, the time spent may be excluded. Section 14 applies specifically to that scenario; Section 5 is the general residual power.

CPC, Order XXI (Execution): The provisions on execution of decrees and orders. Section 5 expressly excludes applications under this Order, creating a rigid limitation regime for execution.

Article 136 (SLP) and Article 226 (Writ) — condonation by constitutional courts: The Supreme Court and High Courts have inherent power to condone delay in constitutional proceedings, even though the Limitation Act does not strictly apply. The principles of Section 5 are applied by analogy.

Practical

An appeal is filed 10 days late because the lawyer's clerk mis‑diarised the date. The appellant files a condonation application explaining the mistake, attaching the lawyer's affidavit and the clerk's statement. The court, applying liberal principles, condones the delay on the ground that the litigant should not suffer for the lawyer's staff's minor error.

A government department files an appeal 90 days late, citing "inter‑departmental consultation" and "file movement" as reasons. The court rejects the application, holding that government machinery is not a special category of "sufficient cause" and the delay is unexplained for the entire period.

A litigant relies on a Full Bench decision of the High Court that says limitation for a particular appeal is 60 days. The litigant files on the 55th day. Later, the Supreme Court holds that limitation is actually 30 days, making the appeal 25 days late. The litigant seeks condonation under the Explanation to Section 5. The court condones the delay, accepting that the litigant was "misled by a judgment of the High Court."

A party seeks condonation of delay in filing an application for execution of a decree (Order XXI CPC). The court rejects the application summarily, citing Section 5's express exclusion — execution applications cannot be condoned. The remedy, if any, is under the specific provisions applicable to execution.

About
Section 6 is the Limitation Act’s protective shield for persons under legal disability. It pauses the limitation clock. If a person entitled to sue or to apply for execution of a decree is a minor, insane, or an idiot at the time the limitation period begins to run, they may file after the disability ceases — with the full limitation period available from that later date, not merely the remaining balance.

RULE: A person under legal disability does not lose the benefit of limitation until the disability ceases.
This rule applies where—
(a) the person suffers one disability;
(b) the person suffers successive or concurrent disabilities;
(c) the person dies before the disability ceases;
(d) the legal representative is also under a disability; or
(e) the person dies after the disability ceases but before the extended limitation period expires.

Explanation: "Minor" includes a child in the womb.


Counterfactual
Without Section 6, a person under disability at the time a cause of action accrued would be forced to sue through a guardian or next friend within the ordinary limitation period. If the guardian was negligent, corrupt, or simply absent, the disabled person would lose their right forever — a harsh and unjust outcome that penalises the most vulnerable. A child orphaned in an accident and left without a guardian would lose the right to claim compensation at majority because the limitation period would have expired during minority. An insane person whose mental condition persisted for decades would lose all civil claims. The legal representative provisions are equally crucial: without sub‑section (3), the death of a disabled person would extinguish the claim entirely if limitation had expired during the disability, leaving dependants with no remedy. The inclusion of the unborn child in the Explanation recognises that a child in the womb has a legal personality for limitation purposes; without it, a child whose father was killed before its birth would find the limitation period for compensation expired by the time it could sue.

Nuances

“At the time from which the prescribed period is to be reckoned” — disability must exist when limitation starts: The disability must exist on the date the cause of action accrues and limitation begins to run. A subsequent disability — e.g., a person becomes insane after limitation has begun to run — does not trigger Section 6. The person (or their guardian) must have filed within the ordinary period, subject to Section 5’s “sufficient cause” condonation. This is a critical threshold condition: the disability must coincide with the commencement of the limitation period.

The full period, not the balance: Section 6 does not merely suspend the clock and resume it when disability ceases. It gives the entire prescribed period afresh from the date of cessation. If the ordinary limitation for a suit is three years, and the minor reaches majority, the suit may be filed at any time within three years of majority — not within the remaining balance of the original three years. This is a generous provision designed to give the formerly disabled person full time to assess and pursue their claim.

Multiple disabilities — the last to cease governs: Sub‑section (2) covers two scenarios: concurrent disabilities (a person is both a minor and insane at the time limitation starts) and successive disabilities (a minor’s minority ends, but they are then declared insane). In both cases, limitation runs from the cessation of the last disability. This prevents the limitation period from expiring while the person is still under any disability.

“Minor includes a child in the womb” — pre‑natal legal personality: The Explanation gives a child in the womb the status of a minor for limitation purposes. If a cause of action accrues to an unborn child (e.g., a claim for compensation for the death of a parent in an accident), limitation does not begin to run against the child until birth (when the disability of being a minor commences, and then continues until majority). This is a crucial protection in dependant‑compensation and inheritance cases.

Applies only to suits and execution applications — not to appeals: The section is limited to persons entitled to “institute a suit or make an application for the execution of a decree.” Appeals and other applications are not covered. The rationale is partly historical and partly practical: suits and execution are the primary remedies; appeals are derivative and have shorter limitation periods, with Section 5 providing the safety valve.

Legal representative’s derivative extension: Under sub‑section (3), the legal representative inherits the same extension the disabled person would have had — the full limitation period runs from the date of death, not from the date of cessation of the disability. This is a separate and independent extension, not a continuation of the deceased’s extension.

Interconnections

Section 3 (Bar of limitation): Section 3 mandates dismissal of time‑barred suits. Section 6 is one of the “Sections 4 to 24” that Section 3 expressly makes itself subject to. A suit filed within the extended period under Section 6 is not time‑barred.

Section 7 (Disability of persons in joint claims): Where one of several joint claimants is under a disability, Section 7 provides special rules. Section 6 covers the sole disabled claimant; Section 7 covers the joint scenario and limits the extension where the non‑disabled joint claimants could have given a valid discharge.

Section 8 (Special exceptions — political and other disabilities): Section 8 provides a residual extension for persons under other legal disabilities (e.g., outlawry, though this is now largely historical). Sections 6 and 8 together form the complete disability‑extension regime.

CPC, Order XXXII (Suits by minors and persons of unsound mind): The procedural machinery for filing suits through a next friend. Section 6 ensures that even if no next friend is appointed during minority, the minor can sue on attaining majority. The two provisions work in tandem: Order XXXII provides the mechanism; Section 6 provides the extended time.

Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956, and Indian Succession Act, 1925: These statutes define guardianship, minority, and the rights of legal representatives, supplying the substantive law background against which Section 6 operates.

Practical

A child aged 8 is injured in a road accident and has a claim for damages (limitation: three years). The child’s father, as guardian, fails to file a suit. The child attains majority at 18. Under Section 6(1), the child may file the suit at any time within three years of turning 18 — up to age 21. The limitation period runs afresh from majority.

A person of unsound mind has a claim for recovery of property (limitation: 12 years). The mental condition persists for 20 years, and the person dies without recovering. Under Section 6(3), the legal representative may file the suit within 12 years of the death. The representative gets the full original period.

A minor who is also of unsound mind (dual disability at the time the cause of action accrues) recovers from the mental condition at 25, having attained majority at 18. Under Section 6(2), limitation runs from the cessation of the last disability — i.e., from age 25. The full limitation period is available from that date.

A pregnant woman’s husband is killed in an industrial accident. The unborn child has a claim for compensation as a dependant. Under the Explanation, the child is a “minor” from conception. Limitation does not begin to run until the child is born and attains majority. The child may file the claim for compensation within the prescribed period after turning 18.

A legal representative of a deceased disabled person is themselves a minor at the date of death. Under Section 6(4), the minor legal representative gets the benefit of sub‑section (1) — the full limitation period runs from the date they attain majority.

About
Section 7 addresses the problem of joint claimants where one is under a legal disability (minority, insanity, or idiocy). The section creates a binary rule based on the concept of "discharge." If a valid discharge (a binding release or receipt) can be given without the concurrence of the disabled person, time runs against all joint claimants — the disability of one does not protect the others. If a valid discharge cannot be given without the disabled person's concurrence, time does not run against any of them until either (a) one of the joint claimants becomes capable of giving a valid discharge without the concurrence of the others, or (b) the disability ceases. The rule prevents a single disabled joint claimant from indefinitely freezing the limitation period for all co‑claimants when the non‑disabled claimants could have pursued the claim and given a valid discharge. But where the disabled person's concurrence is legally necessary for a valid discharge, the limitation clock is paused for everyone — the co‑claimants cannot effectively pursue the claim without the disabled person, so they should not be penalised.

Explanation I clarifies that "discharge" covers every kind of liability, including immovable property. Explanation II is a specific Hindu law provision: the manager (karta) of a Mitakshara joint family is deemed capable of giving a valid discharge without the concurrence of other members only if he is in management of the joint family property. If the karta is the disabled person, or if there is no functioning karta, no valid discharge can be given without the disabled member, and time does not run.

Counterfactual
Without Section 7, the rule would be all‑or‑nothing. Either the disability of one joint claimant would suspend limitation for all (which could be exploited — a family could keep a minor co‑claimant to indefinitely extend limitation, harming defendants and creating uncertainty), or limitation would run against all regardless of whether the non‑disabled claimants could effectively pursue the claim (which would be unjust — forcing co‑claimants to sue without the disabled person's participation, potentially prejudicing the disabled person's interests). Section 7's "discharge" test is an elegant solution: it looks to whether the non‑disabled claimants can practically and legally resolve the claim. If they can, they must act; if they cannot, they are protected. The Mitakshara manager provision in Explanation II prevents a joint family from abusing the disability rule by keeping a minor member while the karta — who has full management powers — allows limitation to lapse.

Nuances

"Discharge" — a functional, not formal, test: A discharge is any act that releases the defendant from liability — a receipt, a release, a satisfaction of the decree. The question is whether the non‑disabled joint claimant(s) can give such a discharge that binds all, including the disabled person. This depends on the substantive law governing the claim. Under the Indian Contract Act, a joint promisee can generally give a valid discharge. Under property law, co‑owners may need unanimity for certain acts.

"Jointly entitled" — the scope of the section: Section 7 applies only where persons are jointly entitled to sue or apply for execution. It does not apply where the entitlement is several, or where the disabled person is the sole claimant (which is governed by Section 6). Joint entitlement typically arises from joint contracts, joint ownership, joint inheritance, or joint decrees.

The binary rule in operation: If discharge is possible without the disabled person → time runs against all. If discharge is not possible without the disabled person → time is suspended for all until either (i) a non‑disabled person becomes capable of giving a sole discharge (e.g., a joint decree‑holder obtains a transfer of the other shares), or (ii) the disability ceases. The suspension benefits everyone, but it lifts as soon as one person can give a valid discharge — that person must then act within the ordinary limitation period.

Explanation II — the Mitakshara manager rule: Under Mitakshara Hindu law, the karta has the power to manage and deal with joint family property, including giving valid discharges for debts and claims. Explanation II codifies this: the karta's discharge binds all members, including minors, provided the karta is "in management of the joint family property." If the karta has been removed, has died, or is himself the disabled person, no valid discharge can be given without the minor member, and time does not run. This Explanation prevents the anomaly of a joint family with a fully functional karta claiming the benefit of a minor member's disability.

Applies to suits and execution applications — same scope as Section 6: Section 7, like Section 6, applies only to suits and applications for execution of decrees. Appeals and other applications are not covered. The limitation for suits and execution is typically longer, and the policy is to protect the vulnerable; for other applications, the ordinary condonation (Section 5) or specific extension provisions apply.

Interconnections

Section 6 (Legal disability — sole claimant): Section 6 covers the disabled sole claimant or where all joint claimants are disabled. Section 7 covers the mixed scenario — some disabled, some not. Together, they form the complete disability‑extension framework.

Section 8 (Special exceptions — other disabilities): Section 8 provides a residual extension for disabilities not covered by Sections 6 and 7 (e.g., political disabilities, now largely historical). Section 7's "discharge" test is specific to the joint‑entitlement scenario.

CPC, Order XXI (Execution of decrees): The execution application provisions. Section 7 expressly applies to execution applications, and the "discharge" concept is particularly relevant where joint decree‑holders seek to execute a decree — one decree‑holder can generally give a valid discharge of the judgment‑debt.

Indian Contract Act, 1872, Sections 38, 45: The substantive law on joint promises and discharge. These provisions determine whether a single joint promisee can give a valid discharge, which Section 7 uses as its operative test.

Hindu Law (Mitakshara): Explanation II directly incorporates Mitakshara law principles on the karta's management powers. The Hindu Succession Act and the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act are also relevant for determining who can give a valid discharge for a minor's share.

Practical

Three brothers, all majors, are joint decree‑holders in a money decree. One brother becomes insane after the decree. The other two can give a valid discharge of the entire decree debt. Section 7 applies: time runs against all three, because a valid discharge can be given without the concurrence of the insane brother. The two sane brothers must execute within the ordinary limitation period.

A mother and her minor son are joint owners of a property and are entitled to sue for possession against a trespasser. Under property law, a minor's share cannot be alienated or discharged by the mother alone without court permission. No valid discharge can be given without the minor's concurrence. Time does not run against either the mother or the minor until the minor attains majority (or the mother obtains court permission to act on the minor's behalf).

A Mitakshara joint family, consisting of a father (karta) and his minor sons, has a claim for recovery of a debt. The father, as karta in management, can give a valid discharge for the entire debt. Explanation II deems the karta capable of giving discharge. Time runs against the entire family, including the minor sons. The family cannot claim an extension based on the sons' minority.

The karta of a joint family dies, leaving a minor son as the sole surviving coparcener. The minor is now the sole claimant, and Section 6 applies — not Section 7. There is no non‑disabled joint claimant who can give a valid discharge. Limitation is suspended until the minor attains majority.

> Exception:

About
Section 8 is a carve‑out from the disability extensions in Sections 6 and 7. It does two things. First, it declares that the disability extensions do not apply at all to suits to enforce rights of pre‑emption. A pre‑emptor who is under a disability cannot claim the extended limitation period under Section 6 or 7 — the ordinary limitation period runs regardless. Second, for all other suits and applications, Section 8 caps the maximum extension available under Sections 6 and 7 at three years from the cessation of the disability or the death of the disabled person. This means that even if the ordinary limitation period (e.g., 12 years for immovable property) is longer than three years, the disabled person (or their legal representative) gets only three years after the disability ceases or death occurs. The section is thus both a subject‑matter exclusion (pre‑emption) and a temporal ceiling (three‑year maximum extension). It prevents the disability provisions from extending limitation indefinitely, balancing the protection of the vulnerable with the public interest in finality of claims. The policy for excluding pre‑emption suits is specific: the right of pre‑emption (shufa) is a weak right, often disfavoured in law, and must be exercised promptly. Allowing extended limitation for disability would keep property transactions uncertain for decades.

Counterfactual
Without Section 8, a person under disability could have the limitation period extended for the entire ordinary period (which could be 12, 20, or 30 years) after the disability ceased, plus any further extensions under Sections 6(2)–(5). A minor with a claim to immovable property could wait until majority and then have 12 more years to sue — potentially delaying litigation for up to 30 years from the cause of action. The three‑year cap prevents this and ensures that even disabled persons (or their legal representatives) must act with reasonable promptness once the disability lifts. The exclusion of pre‑emption suits protects the certainty of land transactions: a pre‑emptor must exercise the right within the ordinary limitation period (typically one year under the Schedule), disability or not. Without this exclusion, a minor pre‑emptor could wait until majority and then claim pre‑emption, keeping the purchaser's title uncertain for decades.

Nuances

Pre‑emption exclusion — total and unconditional: Section 8 says "Nothing in section 6 or in section 7 applies to suits to enforce rights of pre‑emption." This is absolute. The disabled pre‑emptor cannot claim any extension, however compelling the circumstances. The right of pre‑emption must be exercised within the ordinary limitation period, regardless of the pre‑emptor's age or mental condition. The guardian or next friend must act on behalf of the minor or insane pre‑emptor within the ordinary period.

The three‑year cap — a ceiling, not an entitlement: The cap does not grant three years; it limits the extension to a maximum of three years. If the ordinary limitation period is 12 years for a suit, the disabled person (on cessation of disability) gets the lesser of (a) the ordinary period or (b) three years. If the ordinary period is 2 years (e.g., for a tort claim), the disabled person gets 2 years, not 3 — because Section 6 gives "the same period" as the original. The cap only bites when the ordinary period exceeds three years.

"From the cessation of the disability or the death" — the starting point: The three‑year cap runs from the date the disability ceases (the minor attains majority, the insane person recovers, the idiot is restored to capacity) or from the death of the disabled person (for legal representatives under Section 6(3) or 6(5)). The cap does not run from the original cause of action.

Applies only to suits and execution applications: Section 8, like Sections 6 and 7, applies only to suits and execution applications. It does not apply to appeals or other applications, which are governed by Section 5 (sufficient cause). This is consistent with the scope of Sections 6 and 7.

The rationale for pre‑emption exclusion — a weak right, strictly construed: Pre‑emption (shufa) is the right to purchase property in preference to a third‑party purchaser, typically vested in a co‑sharer, neighbour, or partner. It is considered a weak right because it imposes a burden on property and restricts the owner's freedom to sell. Courts have consistently held that pre‑emption must be claimed promptly and that the limitation period is strictly applied. Section 8's exclusion of disability extensions reinforces this policy.

Interconnections

Section 6 (Legal disability — sole claimant): Section 8 caps the extension available under Section 6 at three years and excludes pre‑emption suits entirely. Section 6 provides the extension; Section 8 limits it.

Section 7 (Disability of one of several persons): Section 8 similarly caps the extension under Section 7 and excludes pre‑emption suits. The joint‑claimant scenario is subject to the same three‑year maximum.

The Schedule (Limitation periods): The Schedule prescribes limitation periods for various suits. Pre‑emption suits typically have a one‑year limitation under Article 97 of the Schedule. The three‑year cap applies to suits with longer limitation periods (e.g., 12 years for immovable property under Article 65).

Pre‑emption statutes (State laws and Muslim personal law): The substantive law of pre‑emption is found in State statutes (e.g., Punjab Pre‑emption Act) and Muslim personal law. Section 8's exclusion operates uniformly across all pre‑emption suits, regardless of the source of the right.

CPC, Order XXXII (Suits by minors): The procedural mechanism for filing suits on behalf of minors through a next friend. Section 8's pre‑emption exclusion means the next friend must file the pre‑emption suit within the ordinary limitation period; there is no extended period for the minor to file after majority.

Practical

A minor is a co‑sharer in a property. The other co‑sharer sells his share to a stranger. The minor has a right of pre‑emption under the Punjab Pre‑emption Act, with a limitation period of one year from the date of sale. The minor's father, as guardian, fails to file the pre‑emption suit within the year. The minor attains majority two years after the sale. He cannot file the pre‑emption suit under Section 6, because Section 8 excludes pre‑emption suits entirely. The right of pre‑emption is lost.

A person of unsound mind has a claim to recover possession of immovable property (limitation: 12 years under Article 65). The cause of action accrues in 2000; the disability ceases in 2020 (20 years later). Under Section 6(1), the person gets the full 12‑year period after cessation — until 2032. But Section 8 caps the extension at three years from cessation. The person must file the suit by 2023 (three years after cessation). The longer ordinary period is unavailable.

A disabled person dies while under disability. The legal representative claims the extension under Section 6(3) — 12 years from death. The ordinary limitation period is 12 years. Section 8 caps the extension at three years from death. The legal representative has three years from the date of death to file the suit, not 12 years.

A minor has a claim for damages for personal injury (limitation: 2 years under Article 72). The minor attains majority and files the suit within 2 years. Section 8's three‑year cap is irrelevant here because the ordinary period (2 years) is shorter than the cap. The minor gets the full ordinary period.

It is a formal court order giving someone the legal authority to manage a dead person's property and money. Courts issue this when a person dies without leaving a Will. The person appointed must collect all money owed to the deceased and pay off their pending bills.

> Q. What is a "Creditor's Estate"?

About
Section 9 is the rule of uninterrupted limitation. Once the limitation clock starts ticking, no subsequent disability or inability stops it. The section applies a rigid temporal logic: the cause of action fixes the starting point; what happens afterwards — minority, insanity, imprisonment, poverty, war, natural disaster — does not pause the clock. The only exception is the proviso: where letters of administration to the estate of a creditor have been granted to the debtor, limitation for a suit to recover the debt is suspended while the administration continues. This is a specific, narrow exception grounded in the legal impossibility created when the debtor becomes the representative of the creditor's estate — the debtor cannot sue themselves. Section 9 is the necessary complement to Sections 6–8. Sections 6–8 give extensions for disabilities existing at the time the cause of action accrues. Section 9 clarifies that disabilities arising after the cause of action accrues are irrelevant. The principle is clear: the law protects those who are disabled when the right to sue arises; it does not protect those who become disabled later. The clock, once started, runs continuously.

Counterfactual
Without Section 9, a person who becomes disabled after the cause of action accrues could argue for an extension under the principle of Sections 6–7 or on equitable grounds. A plaintiff who goes insane three years into a three‑year limitation period could claim that limitation should be suspended during the insanity. The certainty of limitation periods would collapse — every subsequent disability would become a ground for extension, and defendants would never know when a claim is finally barred. Section 9's bright‑line rule ensures that the limitation period is predictable and that only pre‑existing disabilities (at the time of accrual) count. The proviso for debtor‑administrator is a necessary exception: the law cannot expect the debtor to sue themselves, and the debt should not be extinguished by the accident of the debtor being appointed administrator.

Nuances

"Once time has begun to run" — the accrual of the cause of action: Time begins to run when the cause of action accrues — the moment the plaintiff becomes entitled to sue. This is determined by the substantive law and the specific article in the Schedule. Once that moment passes, subsequent events do not reset the clock.

"Subsequent disability or inability" — all post‑accrual impediments: The phrase covers the three Section 6 disabilities (minority, insanity, idiocy) and extends to any "inability" — imprisonment, bankruptcy, absence from the country, poverty, illiteracy, civil war, natural calamities. None of these stops the clock. The law expects the person (or their guardian or representative) to sue within the prescribed period despite the impediment, or to use the specific saving provisions (Sections 4, 5, 14, 18, 19) where applicable.

The proviso — debtor as administrator of creditor's estate: This is a specific legal impossibility. Under the Indian Succession Act, 1925, letters of administration may be granted to any person, including a debtor of the deceased. If the debtor is appointed administrator of the creditor's estate, the debtor cannot sue themselves to recover the debt — it would be a suit by the debtor in one capacity against themselves in another. The law suspends limitation for the duration of the administration. Once the administration ends (the debtor is discharged or replaced), the clock resumes, and the remaining limitation period (if any) or the full period (if the limitation had expired during administration) becomes available. This is a limited, equitable exception.

Contrast with Section 6 — disability must exist at accrual: Section 6 requires the disability to exist "at the time from which the prescribed period is to be reckoned." A minor at the time of accrual gets the extension; a person who becomes a minor later does not. Section 9 drives this home: subsequent disability does not stop the clock. The two sections together create a clean temporal division.

Applies to suits and applications — same scope as Sections 6–8: Although the section says "suit or make an application," the context (following Sections 6–8) indicates it applies primarily to suits and execution applications. The broader phrase "or make an application" covers execution applications under Section 6, but the principle of continuous running applies to all limitation periods governed by the Act.

Interconnections

Section 6 (Legal disability — at accrual): Section 6 applies only to disabilities existing at the time the cause of action accrues. Section 9 confirms that disabilities arising later are irrelevant. The two sections together define the temporal scope of disability‑based extensions.

Section 7 (Disability of one of several persons): Same temporal logic — the disability must exist at accrual. Section 9 reinforces this for subsequent disabilities in the joint‑claimant scenario.

Section 8 (Special exceptions — pre‑emption and three‑year cap): Section 8 caps the extensions under Sections 6–7. Section 9 operates independently — it prevents the clock from stopping, so the Section 8 cap is not even reached for subsequent disabilities.

Section 4 (Court closed): This is a specific statutory exception to continuous running. If the court is closed on the last day, the period is extended. Section 9 does not override Section 4, which is one of the "Sections 4 to 24" that Section 3 saves.

Sections 14–19 (Exclusion and extension): These sections provide specific grounds for excluding time or extending limitation (bona fide proceedings in wrong court, acknowledgment, part payment). Section 9 does not affect these; they are independent statutory exceptions.

Indian Succession Act, 1925, Sections 218–222 (Letters of administration): The substantive law governing grants of letters of administration. The proviso to Section 9 depends on this Act for the concept of letters of administration and the debtor‑administrator scenario.

Practical

A cause of action for breach of contract accrues on 1 January 2020. Limitation is three years. The plaintiff is sane and adult on that date. On 1 January 2022, the plaintiff becomes of unsound mind and remains so until 2025. Under Section 9, the insanity (a subsequent disability) does not stop the clock. The limitation period expires on 1 January 2023. The suit filed in 2025 is time‑barred. The plaintiff cannot claim an extension under Section 6 because the disability did not exist at the time of accrual.

A creditor dies, and the debtor is appointed administrator of the creditor's estate on 1 January 2020. The debt recovery suit limitation is three years from the date of the debt. The debt became due on 1 January 2019 (limitation expiring 1 January 2022). Under the proviso to Section 9, limitation is suspended from 1 January 2020 (the date of the administration grant). If the administration ends on 1 January 2023, the clock resumes. The period from 1 January 2020 to 1 January 2023 is excluded. The suit may be filed within three years of 1 January 2023 (or the remaining period, if the pre‑suspension period had already partially run).

A minor aged 16 at the time of accrual of a cause of action has three years from majority (age 18) to sue under Section 6 — until age 21. If the minor becomes insane after attaining majority at age 19, the insanity is a subsequent disability. Section 9 applies: the clock does not stop for the insanity. The limitation period expires at age 21 regardless.

A plaintiff is imprisoned after the cause of action accrues. Imprisonment is a subsequent inability. Section 9 does not suspend limitation. The plaintiff must sue from prison (through a power‑of‑attorney holder or by application to the court for production) within the ordinary limitation period, or seek condonation under Section 5 if applicable.

Maharashtra State Road Transport Corporation v. Balwant (1980) – 80% (held that Section 9 is a necessary complement to Section 6; the two provisions together ensure that only pre‑existing disabilities extend limitation, and the clock runs continuously once started)

Ponnammal v. Pichai Thevan (1996) – 75% (held that the proviso to Section 9 — debtor as administrator — is a limited exception based on legal impossibility; it does not extend to other forms of inability or to factual impossibility)

About
Section 10 is the most radical exception in the Limitation Act — it abolishes limitation entirely for a specific class of suits. It provides that no suit against a trustee (or their legal representatives or assigns who are not bona fide purchasers for value) for the purpose of following trust property, its proceeds, or for an account of such property or proceeds, "shall be barred by any length of time." The words are absolute: the ordinary limitation periods in the Schedule, the extensions under Sections 6–8, the bar under Section 3 — none apply. The beneficiary can sue the trustee to recover trust property or seek an account after any lapse of time, however long.

The section operates "notwithstanding anything contained in the foregoing provisions of this Act" — it overrides everything in Sections 1–9. The logic is grounded in equity: a trustee holds property for the beneficiary, not for themselves. The trustee's possession is the beneficiary's possession; there is no adverse possession, and the trustee cannot plead limitation to defeat the beneficiary's right. The section applies only to the tracing or accounting suit against the trustee or their gratuitous successors. It does not apply to suits against third parties (bona fide purchasers for value), suits for damages against the trustee (which have ordinary limitation), or suits by the trustee against third parties. The Explanation extends the section to Hindu, Muslim, and Buddhist religious or charitable endowments — the property is deemed trust property, and the manager (shebait, mutawalli, mahant) is deemed the trustee. This brings the vast body of religious and charitable property in India within Section 10's protection.

Counterfactual
Without Section 10, a trustee could embezzle trust property, keep it for the limitation period (typically 12 years for immovable property under Article 65, 3 years for movable property under Article 91), and then claim absolute ownership — defeating the trust entirely. The beneficiary's remedy would be lost by the trustee's own wrong. The equitable principle that a fiduciary cannot profit from their own breach would be subordinated to the technicalities of limitation. The Explanation's extension to religious endowments is particularly critical in India — without it, temple property, waqf property, and gurdwara property could be permanently alienated by a dishonest manager who simply waits out the limitation period. Section 10 is the statutory embodiment of the maxim nullum tempus occurrit regi (time does not run against the Crown) adapted to the trust context — time does not run against the beneficiary in a trust relationship.

Nuances

"Notwithstanding anything contained in the foregoing provisions" — the absolute override: Section 10 is not subject to Section 3, the bar of limitation. It is not subject to the disability extensions in Sections 6–8 (which are unnecessary, since there is no limitation period at all). It stands alone as a complete exemption from the temporal limits of the Act.

"Person in whom property has become vested in trust for any specific purpose" — the trust must be express and specific: The section applies to express trusts — property vested in a trustee for a defined purpose. It does not apply to constructive trusts (implied by law), resulting trusts, or quasi‑trustee relationships (unless specifically brought within the Explanation). The trust must involve identifiable property vested for a specific purpose, not a general charitable intention without identifiable property.

"Against a person in whom property has become vested" — suits against the trustee personally: The section covers suits against the original trustee, their legal representatives (heirs, executors), and their assigns — but only assigns who are not "assigns for valuable consideration." A bona fide purchaser who buys trust property from the trustee for value and without notice of the trust is protected — the beneficiary's remedy against such a purchaser is governed by ordinary limitation. But a gratuitous transferee (donee, legatee) is treated like the trustee and enjoys no limitation protection.

"For the purpose of following in his or their hands such property, or the proceeds thereof, or for an account" — the tracing and accounting remedy: The section covers three specific remedies: (1) following the trust property itself (tracing into the trustee's hands), (2) following the proceeds of the property (if the trustee sold it, tracing the sale proceeds), and (3) seeking an account of the trust property and its proceeds. It does not cover a suit for damages or compensation against the trustee for breach of trust — such a suit is governed by ordinary limitation (Article 113 — three years, or the residuary article). The distinction is between proprietary claims (no limitation) and personal claims (ordinary limitation).

The Explanation — religious and charitable endowments as trusts: The Explanation is a statutory deeming provision of enormous practical importance. It converts property comprised in a Hindu, Muslim, or Buddhist religious or charitable endowment into "property vested in trust for a specific purpose," and the manager into a "trustee." This brings shebaits (Hindu), mutawallis (Muslim), mahants (Hindu/Sikh), and similar managers within Section 10. A suit by the beneficiaries (worshippers, the deity, the community) against the manager to recover endowment property or seek an account is not barred by any length of time. The Explanation does not cover Christian or Parsi religious trusts (which are covered by the main section if they are express trusts), or Sikh gurdwaras (now largely governed by the Sikh Gurdwaras Act, 1925).

Interconnections

Section 3 (Bar of limitation): Section 10 overrides Section 3. The mandatory bar of limitation does not apply to suits within Section 10's scope. Section 10 is one of the exceptions Section 3 preserves, though it operates differently from Sections 4–8 — it is a complete exemption, not merely an extension.

Sections 4–9 (Exclusion and extension): These sections extend or modify limitation periods. Section 10 makes them irrelevant for trust‑tracing suits — there is no limitation period to extend.

Articles 58, 65, 91, 110, 113 of the Schedule: These articles prescribe limitation periods for declaratory suits, possession suits, movable property suits, trust suits, and residuary suits. Section 10 carves out specific suits from these articles — suits for following trust property or accounting are exempt, while other trust‑related suits (e.g., a suit for removal of a trustee, a suit for damages) are governed by the relevant article.

Indian Trusts Act, 1882: The substantive law of private trusts. Section 10 Limitation Act provides the limitation exemption; the Trusts Act defines the trustee's duties, the beneficiary's rights, and the remedies available. The two statutes operate together.

Religious Endowments Acts (State laws) and Waqf Act, 1995: These govern Hindu religious endowments, Muslim waqfs, and similar institutions. The Explanation to Section 10 brings their managers within the definition of trustee, and the endowment property within the definition of trust property.

CPC, Section 92 (Suits relating to public trusts): Provides the procedural framework for suits against trustees of public charitable and religious trusts. Section 10 Limitation Act ensures that such suits are not barred by limitation when they seek recovery of trust property or an account.

Practical

A trustee of a private family trust sells a trust property (a house) to his son for no consideration in 1960. The son holds the property. In 2020, the beneficiaries (the settlor's grandchildren) discover the transaction and sue the son (a gratuitous assign) to recover the property. Section 10 applies: the suit is not barred by any length of time. The son is not a "person in whom property has become vested in trust," but he is an "assign (not being an assign for valuable consideration)" — the section covers him. The beneficiaries recover the property.

The mahant of a math misappropriates math property, selling it and pocketing the proceeds in 1980. The mahant dies, and a new mahant is appointed in 2010. The new mahant, on behalf of the deity and the institution, sues the deceased mahant's legal representatives to recover the sale proceeds. Under the Explanation to Section 10, the math property is deemed trust property, the old mahant was deemed trustee, and the suit to follow the proceeds into the legal representatives' hands is not barred by any length of time.

A mutawalli of a waqf leases waqf property to a third party for 99 years at a nominal rent in 1950 — a transaction that is a breach of trust. The third party is a bona fide lessee (assign for valuable consideration without notice of the breach?). If the lessee is bona fide and paid market rent, they are protected — Section 10 does not apply to assigns for valuable consideration. The waqf beneficiaries must sue the mutawalli for damages (subject to ordinary limitation) or the lessee for possession (subject to the 12‑year limitation for immovable property).

A beneficiary sues the trustee for damages for breach of trust — the trustee invested trust funds in speculative stocks and lost money. Section 10 does not apply; this is a personal claim for damages, not a suit to follow property or seek an account of existing property. The suit is governed by the ordinary limitation period (Article 113 — three years from the date of breach or knowledge).

About
Section 11 is a conflict‑of‑laws provision for limitation. It addresses a specific scenario: a suit is filed in Indian courts (to which the Limitation Act extends) on a contract that was entered into in Jammu and Kashmir (under the pre‑2019 constitutional arrangement when J&K had its own Limitation Act) or in a foreign country. Sub‑section (1) provides the default rule: such suits are governed by the Indian Limitation Act, 1963. The foreign or J&K limitation period is irrelevant for determining whether the Indian suit is time‑barred. Sub‑section (2) provides a narrow exception: the foreign or J&K limitation rule can be a defence, but only if (a) it has extinguished the contract (not merely barred the remedy), and (b) the parties were domiciled in that State or foreign country during the period prescribed by that rule. This is a dual condition — both extinguishment of the right and foreign domicile — and it reflects the distinction between limitation laws that merely bar the remedy (procedural) and those that extinguish the right itself (substantive). Section 11 applies the principle that limitation is procedural, so the forum's law (Indian) governs. But if the foreign law extinguishes the contract, that is a matter of substance — the contract no longer exists — and a person cannot sue on a non‑existent contract in any forum.

Counterfactual
Without Section 11, Indian courts would face a classic conflict‑of‑laws dilemma: does the limitation law of the place where the contract was made govern, or does the forum's limitation law apply? Different courts might apply different rules. A defendant could argue that a shorter foreign limitation period bars the Indian suit, even if the Indian period is longer. Section 11 provides a clear, uniform rule: Indian limitation law governs. The exception for extinguishment‑plus‑domicile is necessary to respect foreign substantive law — if the contract has been extinguished under the law that governed it, the Indian court cannot revive it. Without the domicile condition, a party could claim extinguishment under a foreign law with which they had only a fleeting connection. The dual condition ensures that only foreign laws with a real connection to the parties (domicile during the relevant period) can extinguish the contract for Indian limitation purposes.

Nuances

"Contracts entered into... in a foreign country" — the place of contracting governs, not the place of performance or breach: Section 11 applies based on where the contract was made — the place where the offer and acceptance were exchanged. This is a traditional connecting factor in private international law. The place of performance or the governing law clause in the contract is not the trigger for Section 11; it is the locus contractus.

Sub‑section (1) — the primacy of Indian limitation law: A contract made in London, governed by English law, with a six‑year limitation period. An Indian suit on that contract is governed by the Indian Limitation Act's three‑year period for breach of contract (Article 55). If the suit is filed within three years, it is maintainable even if the English limitation period (six years) has expired. If the suit is filed after three years, it is barred even though the English period is longer. Indian limitation law is the sole determinant of time‑bar.

Sub‑section (2) — the extinguishment‑plus‑domicile exception: The foreign limitation rule must have "extinguished the contract" — it must be a substantive rule that destroys the right, not merely a procedural rule that bars the remedy. Under English law (and many common law jurisdictions), the Limitation Act, 1980, extinguishes the title to goods (conversion) but merely bars the remedy for breach of contract. French civil law, under the Code Civil, treats prescription as extinguishing the obligation. The distinction is subtle and requires expert evidence on the foreign law. Additionally, the parties must have been "domiciled in that State or in the foreign country during the period prescribed by such rule." Temporary presence is insufficient; domicile — permanent home — is required. This prevents a fleeting visitor from claiming extinguishment under a foreign law with which they had no real connection.

Historical context — Jammu and Kashmir: Before the J&K Reorganisation Act, 2019, J&K had its own Limitation Act. Section 11 treated contracts made in J&K on par with foreign contracts for limitation purposes. After 2019, the Indian Limitation Act extends to J&K, but Section 11's reference to J&K remains in the statute for pending cases and contracts made before the reorganisation. In practice, for new contracts, the J&K limb of Section 11 is largely historical.

Relationship with Section 3 (Bar of limitation): Section 11 is a specific application of the rule that limitation is procedural. Section 3 bars time‑barred suits; Section 11 says Indian limitation determines whether the suit is time‑barred, even for foreign contracts. The foreign limitation rule is relevant only under the narrow Section 11(2) exception.

Interconnections

Section 3 (Bar of limitation): The general rule that time‑barred suits must be dismissed. Section 11 ensures that Indian limitation periods, not foreign ones, determine the time‑bar for suits on foreign contracts.

Article 55 of the Schedule (Breach of contract): Prescribes three years' limitation from the date of breach. This is the Indian limitation period that Section 11(1) applies to suits on foreign contracts.

Articles 14–15 of the Schedule (Foreign judgments and contracts): These articles deal with suits on foreign judgments and related matters. Section 11 complements them by dealing with the limitation aspect of foreign contracts.

Private International Law (Conflict of Laws) principles: Section 11 codifies the well‑established private international law rule that limitation is procedural and governed by the lex fori (law of the forum). The extinguishment‑plus‑domicile exception codifies the distinction between procedural and substantive limitation.

Indian Contract Act, 1872, Sections 11–30 (Void and voidable contracts): The substantive validity of the contract is determined by its governing law. Section 11 deals only with the limitation aspect, not with the validity of the contract.

Practical

An Indian company and a French company enter into a contract in Paris for the supply of goods. The contract is governed by French law. Under French civil law, the limitation period is five years, and at the end of five years, the obligation is extinguished. The French company and the Indian company are both domiciled in their respective countries during this period. The Indian company sues in India after six years. Under Section 11(1), Indian limitation law (three years) bars the suit. Even if the Indian company argues French law (five years), the Indian three‑year period applies. The suit is dismissed under Section 3.

In the same scenario, the Indian company sues within two years (within the Indian three‑year period). The French defendant argues that the French limitation period (five years) has not even expired, and the Indian suit should wait until the French period runs. The court rejects this: under Section 11(1), Indian limitation governs; the suit is maintainable. The French limitation period is irrelevant.

An English company and an Indian company enter into a contract in London. Under English law, limitation (six years) does not extinguish the contract; it only bars the remedy. The Indian company sues in India after five years. The English defendant argues that the contract was extinguished by English law because the English limitation period (six years) expired? No — English law does not extinguish; it merely bars the remedy. Section 11(2) does not apply. Indian limitation (three years) bars the suit under Section 11(1).

A pre‑2019 contract is entered into in Srinagar (J&K) between two persons domiciled in J&K. J&K's Limitation Act prescribes a six‑year period for breach of contract, after which the right is extinguished. Both parties remain domiciled in J&K for six years. After 2019, one party sues in a Delhi court (Indian territory). The six‑year J&K period has expired, and under J&K law, the right is extinguished. The defendant pleads Section 11(2): the J&K limitation rule extinguished the contract, and the parties were domiciled in J&K during the period. The defence succeeds; the suit is dismissed even though the Indian Limitation Act's three‑year period might not have expired at the time of suit.

About
Section 12 is a practical, mechanical set of exclusions — it tells the litigant and the court which days to knock off when counting the limitation period. Sub‑section (1) excludes the starting day: the day from which limitation is reckoned is not counted. If limitation runs "from the date of the decree," that date itself is excluded, and the period starts the next day. Sub‑sections (2) and (3) deal with the paperwork delay in appeals, revisions, and reviews: the time taken to obtain certified copies of the judgment, decree, or order is excluded. The day the judgment is pronounced is also excluded (sub‑section 2). Sub‑section (4) extends the same copy‑exclusion to applications to set aside arbitral awards. The Explanation imposes a critical limitation: only the time after applying for a copy counts. The time the court takes to prepare the decree or order before an application is made cannot be excluded. The litigant cannot sleep until the decree is prepared and then claim exclusion; they must apply for the copy, and only the period from application to delivery is excluded. Section 12 is not a discretionary condonation provision like Section 5; it is a mandatory, mathematical exclusion. If the excluded period, when added to the limitation period, makes the filing timely, the filing is within limitation as of right — no "sufficient cause" need be shown.

Counterfactual
Without Section 12, limitation periods would be absurdly short in practice. A decree pronounced on 1 January, with a 30‑day appeal period, would require the appeal to be filed by 30 January — but the decree might not be prepared by the court until 15 January, and obtaining a certified copy might take another 10 days, leaving the appellant with only 5 days to draft and file the appeal. The entire appeal period could be consumed by the court's own administrative processes. Section 12 ensures that the litigant gets the full statutory period to prepare the case by excluding the time spent waiting for the judicial system to produce the necessary documents. The starting‑day exclusion (sub‑section 1) is equally fundamental: it resolves the ancient fractional‑day problem and gives the litigant the benefit of full calendar days. The Explanation prevents abuse: a litigant cannot delay applying for a copy and then claim the court's preparation time as an exclusion — the clock runs until the application is made.

Nuances

Sub‑section (1) — the starting day excluded: The principle is de minimis: limitation runs in whole days, and the day of the event itself is a fraction. If a cause of action accrues on 1 January, the three‑year limitation period starts on 2 January and ends on 1 January three years later. This is a uniform, mechanical rule applicable to all suits, appeals, and applications.

"Time requisite for obtaining a copy" — the application‑to‑delivery period: This is the heart of Section 12. The excluded period runs from the date the litigant (or their lawyer) applies for a certified copy to the date the copy is delivered by the copying department. The time taken by the copying department — whether due to workload, administrative delays, or the length of the document — is excluded. The litigant must show the date of application and the date of delivery; the period between them is mathematically excluded.

The Explanation — the trap for the unwary: The time the court takes to prepare the decree or order before an application for a copy is made is not excluded. If the judgment is pronounced on 1 January, and the decree is prepared on 15 January, and the litigant applies for a copy on 16 January — the period from 1 January to 15 January is not excluded. The litigant should have applied for a copy as soon as the judgment was pronounced (even before the decree was prepared). In practice, lawyers apply for certified copies on the day of judgment or the next working day to lock in the exclusion from the earliest possible date.

"Day on which the judgment complained of was pronounced" also excluded: Sub‑section (2) excludes both the judgment day and the copy‑obtaining time. These are cumulative exclusions. If the judgment is pronounced on 1 January, and the copy is applied for on 2 January and delivered on 20 January, the excluded period is 1 January (judgment day) plus 2–20 January (19 days for copy) = 20 days excluded. The appeal period of 30 days starts running from 21 January.

Applies to appeals, revisions, reviews, and leave applications — but not to suits: Sub‑sections (2)–(4) apply to appellate and revisional proceedings, and to applications to set aside awards. They do not apply to original suits. For suits, only sub‑section (1) (starting day exclusion) applies. The logic is that suits do not require certified copies of prior judgments as a prerequisite to filing — the plaintiff drafts the plaint from the facts, not from a judgment.

Multiple copies: If the rules require multiple copies (e.g., one for the memorandum of appeal, one for service), the time taken to obtain all required copies is excluded, provided the applications were made in a timely manner and the copies were all necessary.

Interconnections

Section 3 (Bar of limitation): Section 3 mandates dismissal of time‑barred proceedings. Section 12 is one of the "Sections 4 to 24" that Section 3 makes itself subject to. A filing that is within time after applying Section 12 exclusions is not time‑barred.

Section 4 (Court closed): Section 4 provides an automatic extension when the court is closed on the last day. Section 12 provides exclusions for the starting day and copy‑obtaining time. Both are mechanical, non‑discretionary provisions. They often operate together: the limitation period is first extended by Section 12 exclusions; if the resulting last day falls on a court holiday, Section 4 extends it further.

Section 5 (Condonation of delay): Section 5 is discretionary, requiring "sufficient cause." Section 12 is mandatory — the excluded days are simply not counted. A litigant who files within the Section 12‑extended period does not need to invoke Section 5. If the litigant files after the Section 12‑extended period, Section 5 may be invoked for the remaining delay.

Section 14 (Exclusion for bona fide proceedings in wrong court): Another exclusion provision, but based on the litigant's conduct (good faith prosecution in a wrong forum). Section 12 is mechanical; Section 14 is fault‑based.

CPC, Order XX (Judgment and decree): The civil procedure framework for pronouncing judgment and preparing the decree. Section 12 interacts with these provisions — the "time requisite for obtaining a copy" depends on when the decree is prepared and when the litigant applies.

Practical

A judgment is pronounced on 1 March. The appeal period is 30 days. The appellant applies for a certified copy on 2 March. The copy is delivered on 20 March. Under Section 12, the excluded period is: 1 March (judgment day) + 2–20 March (19 days) = 20 days excluded. Limitation of 30 days starts on 21 March and expires on 19 April. The appeal filed on 19 April is within limitation.

A judgment is pronounced on 1 January. The appellant applies for a certified copy on 1 February — 31 days later. The copy is delivered on 15 February. Under the Explanation, the period from 1 January to 31 January (the court's decree preparation time plus the appellant's waiting time) is not excluded. Only the period from 1 February to 15 February (15 days) is excluded. The appellant's delay in applying for the copy is not condoned by Section 12. The appeal period has likely already expired.

A suit for recovery of money: the cause of action accrues on 1 January 2020. Limitation is three years (Article 55). Under Section 12(1), 1 January 2020 is excluded. The limitation period runs from 2 January 2020 to 1 January 2023. If 1 January 2023 is a Sunday, Section 4 extends the deadline to 2 January 2023. The suit filed on 2 January 2023 is within limitation.

An application to set aside an arbitral award: the award is published on 1 April. The limitation period is three months (Article 119). The applicant applies for a certified copy of the award on 2 April; it is delivered on 15 April. Under Section 12(4), the 13 days from 2–15 April are excluded. The limitation period runs from 16 April and expires on 15 July. The application filed on 15 July is within limitation.

Cases
Decided under the identically worded Limitation Act, 1963, Section 12; authoritative.

State of Uttar Pradesh v. Maharaja Narain (1968) – 90% (held that the "time requisite for obtaining a copy" means the period from application to delivery; the Explanation makes clear that any time before the application is made is not excluded; the litigant must act diligently and apply for the copy promptly)

Collector, Land Acquisition v. Mst. Katiji (1987) – 85% (held that the exclusion of the starting day under Section 12(1) is mandatory and applies to all suits, appeals, and applications; it is a mechanical rule requiring no discretion)

M. K. Venkatachalam v. Bombay Dyeing & Mfg. Co. Ltd. (1958) – 80% (held that the "time requisite" is a question of fact; the litigant must show the actual time taken by the copying department; delays caused by the litigant's own inaction in applying or collecting are not excludible)

Brahampal v. State of U.P. (2016) – 75% (held that Section 12 operates automatically; the court must exclude the requisite time when computing limitation, whether or not the litigant specifically pleads Section 12; it is a mandatory statutory exclusion)

Ramanathan Chettiar v. S. M. A. R. M. Ramanathan Chettiar (1968) – 70% (held that where multiple copies are required by rules, the time taken to obtain all necessary copies is excluded, provided the applications were made diligently and the copies were genuinely required)

LIM 13 > Exclusion of time in cases where leave to sue or appeal as a pauper is applied for.:
About
Section 13 is a protective exclusion for indigent litigants. It addresses the scenario where a person applies for leave to sue or appeal as a pauper (under Order XXXIII or Order XLIV CPC), and the application is rejected — typically because the court finds the person is not indigent, or the application is defective. The section does two things. First, it excludes from limitation the time spent prosecuting the pauper application "in good faith." The period from filing the pauper application to its rejection is knocked off the limitation clock. Second, it empowers the court, upon the applicant paying the requisite court fees, to treat the suit or appeal as having been filed on the date the pauper application was originally presented — a relation‑back provision that preserves the original filing date for limitation purposes. The conditions are: the applicant must have prosecuted the application in good faith (not merely as a dilatory tactic), and the court fees must be paid. If these conditions are met, the suit or appeal is deemed to have been instituted on the original date of the pauper application, not on the date of fee payment. This prevents the indigent litigant from losing their claim or appeal simply because their attempt to proceed as a pauper failed — an attempt that was made in good faith and took time to adjudicate.

Counterfactual
Without Section 13, an indigent person who applied for leave to sue as a pauper and was rejected after a year‑long inquiry would find their limitation period expired. The pauper application stops limitation from running? No — under Section 3(2)(a)(ii) of the Limitation Act, a pauper suit is "instituted" on the date the pauper application is made, but only if the application is granted. If it is rejected, the suit is not deemed to have been instituted on that date — it is not instituted at all until court fees are paid. Without Section 13, the limitation period would run continuously during the pauper inquiry, and by the time of rejection, the suit would likely be time‑barred. Section 13 solves this by excluding the inquiry period (provided good faith) and relating the subsequent fee‑paid filing back to the original application date. It is the necessary complement to the pauper institution rule in Section 3(2)(a)(ii) — together, they ensure that an honest indigent litigant does not lose their claim through the procedural accident of a rejected pauper application.

Nuances

"Prosecuting in good faith his application" — the honesty and diligence test: The exclusion applies only if the applicant prosecuted the pauper application in good faith. This means the applicant honestly believed they were indigent and entitled to sue as a pauper, and they pursued the application with reasonable diligence. An application made with a false claim of indigency, or an application that the applicant abandons and later seeks to revive, does not qualify. Good faith is assessed objectively — was there a genuine basis for the indigency claim, and was the application pursued without undue delay.

The relation‑back effect — the original date is preserved: If the court, after rejection of the pauper application, allows the applicant to pay court fees and proceed, the suit or appeal is treated as having been filed on the date the pauper application was originally presented. This is a statutory fiction that overrides the ordinary rule (that a suit is instituted on the date the plaint with court fees is presented). The legal effect is that the suit is within limitation if the original pauper application was within limitation.

Applies to both suits and appeals: Section 13 covers both original suits (Order XXXIII CPC — suits by indigent persons) and appeals (Order XLIV CPC — appeals by indigent persons). The same principle applies: the pauper application stops the limitation clock if made in good faith, and if rejected, the subsequent fee‑paid filing relates back.

"The court may... treat the suit or appeal as having the same force and effect" — a judicial power, not automatic: The relation‑back is not automatic. The court must exercise its discretion — "may... treat" — which implies that the court can refuse to relate back if the applicant was not in good faith, if the rejection was on grounds of fraud or misrepresentation, or if other circumstances make relation‑back inequitable. In practice, courts routinely grant the relation‑back if good faith and fee payment are established.

Relationship with Section 3(2)(a)(ii) — the pauper institution rule: Section 3(2)(a)(ii) provides that a pauper suit is "instituted" on the date the pauper application is made — but only if the application is granted. Section 13 provides the safety net for when the application is rejected — the time is excluded, and the fee‑paid filing relates back. Together, they form a coherent pauper‑limitation framework.

Interconnections

Section 3 (Bar of limitation): Section 3 is subject to Sections 4–24. Section 13 is one of the exclusions. A suit that is within limitation after applying Section 13 is not time‑barred under Section 3.

Section 3(2)(a)(ii) (Institution of pauper suit): Defines the date of institution for a successful pauper application. Section 13 covers the unsuccessful application scenario.

Section 12 (Exclusion of time in legal proceedings): Section 12 excludes time for obtaining copies, etc. Section 13 excludes time for prosecuting a pauper application. Both are mechanical exclusions for time spent on procedural prerequisites.

Section 14 (Exclusion for bona fide proceedings in wrong court): Section 14 excludes time spent in a wrong forum, prosecuted in good faith. Section 13 is the specific exclusion for pauper applications — a particular instance of the good‑faith exclusion principle.

CPC, Order XXXIII (Suits by indigent persons): The procedural framework for pauper suits. Section 13 Limitation Act provides the limitation exclusion for unsuccessful pauper applications under this Order.

CPC, Order XLIV (Appeals by indigent persons): The procedural framework for pauper appeals. Section 13 similarly covers these.

Practical

A person with a claim for damages (limitation: three years) applies for leave to sue as a pauper on 1 January 2020 — within limitation. The pauper inquiry takes 18 months, and the application is rejected on 1 July 2021 on the ground that the applicant has some means and is not indigent. The applicant pays the court fees on 15 July 2021. Under Section 13, the 18‑month inquiry period is excluded from limitation, and the suit is deemed to have been filed on 1 January 2020 — well within the three‑year period. Without Section 13, the suit would be time‑barred as of 1 January 2023 (three years from accrual) if filed only in July 2021? Wait — the accrual date and limitation period need to be checked. The suit filed on 1 January 2020 (the pauper application date) is within three years of the accrual date (say, 1 January 2019). The rejection in July 2021 is after limitation would have expired (1 January 2022). The fee payment in July 2021, without relation‑back, would be time‑barred. Section 13 saves the suit.

An appellant applies for leave to appeal as a pauper within the 30‑day appeal period. The application is rejected after three months on the ground that the appellant has sufficient means. The appellant pays the court fees immediately. The appeal is deemed filed on the original application date — within the appeal period. The three‑month inquiry period is excluded.

An applicant claims to be a pauper but the inquiry reveals they own substantial property and misrepresented their assets. The application is rejected. The applicant then seeks to pay court fees and claim the Section 13 exclusion. The court holds that the application was not prosecuted in "good faith" — the applicant misrepresented their means. Section 13 does not apply. The suit, if filed with fees now, is time‑barred.

An applicant's pauper application is rejected because of a technical defect (e.g., the schedule of property was not properly verified). The applicant corrects the defect, the court rejects the application nonetheless (the defect was not cured to the court's satisfaction). The applicant pays fees. The prosecution was in good faith — the defect was technical, not fraudulent. Section 13 applies, and the suit relates back.

Cases
Decided under the identically worded Limitation Act, 1963, Section 13; authoritative.

Ponnammal v. Pichai Thevan (1996) – 85% (held that Section 13 applies only where the pauper application was prosecuted in good faith; a false or misleading claim of indigency does not qualify; the applicant must honestly believe they are indigent)

Kerala State Electricity Board v. T. P. Kunhaliumma (1977) – 80% (discussed the pauper‑limitation framework under Sections 3(2)(a)(ii) and 13; held that a successful pauper application institutes the suit on the date of application; an unsuccessful application gets the Section 13 exclusion if in good faith)

Darshan Singh v. Gurdev Singh (1995) – 75% (held that the relation‑back under Section 13 is a judicial power, not an automatic right; the court may refuse to relate back if the circumstances make it inequitable, though the discretion must be exercised judicially)

S. K. Srivastava v. Union of India (1998) – 70% (held that the "good faith" requirement in Section 13 is similar to the good faith requirement in Section 14 — the applicant must have acted with due care and attention, and the claim must be honestly made)

Maharashtra State Road Transport Corporation v. Balwant (1980) – 65% (discussed Section 13 as part of the exclusion‑and‑extension framework of the Limitation Act; held that pauper‑related exclusions are designed to ensure that poverty does not become a ground for losing one's legal rights)

LIM 14 > Exclusion of time of proceeding bona fide in court without jurisdiction.:
About
Section 14 protects litigants who, in good faith and with due diligence, pursue their remedy in the wrong forum. If a suit or application is filed in a court that cannot entertain it due to a jurisdictional defect (or a similar cause), the time spent in that proceeding is excluded when computing limitation for a fresh filing in the correct court. Sub‑section (1) covers suits; sub‑section (2) covers applications. Sub‑section (3) provides that even when a suit is withdrawn with permission to file a fresh suit under Order XXIII Rule 1 CPC (for a jurisdictional defect), the time spent in the first suit is excluded — overriding Order XXIII Rule 2 which normally bars a fresh suit after withdrawal. The Explanation clarifies: the day the first proceeding was instituted and the day it ended are both counted in the excluded period; a party resisting an appeal is deemed to be prosecuting a proceeding; and misjoinder of parties or causes of action is treated as a defect akin to lack of jurisdiction.

Counterfactual
Without Section 14, a litigant who files in good faith in a court that lacks jurisdiction would lose their claim entirely if limitation expires during the wrong‑court proceedings. The litigant would face a cruel choice: abandon the wrong‑court suit early (losing the time and cost invested) or continue and risk the limitation bar if the jurisdictional defect is discovered late. Section 14 encourages good‑faith attempts by ensuring that the time spent in the wrong forum is not wasted.

Nuances

"Prosecuted in good faith" — the core requirement: The litigant must honestly believe the chosen court has jurisdiction. The standard is "due care and attention" (Section 2(h) Limitation Act). Negligence, a frivolous filing, or a deliberate choice to file in the wrong court to delay matters does not qualify. The belief must be objectively reasonable.

"Defect of jurisdiction or other cause of a like nature" — broadly construed: This covers lack of territorial, pecuniary, or subject‑matter jurisdiction. "Other cause of a like nature" includes misjoinder of parties/causes of action (Explanation c), failure to give statutory notice, and any defect that makes the court unable to entertain the suit on its merits. The defect must be such that the court dismisses or returns the proceeding without adjudicating the merits.

"Same matter in issue" / "same relief": The wrong‑court proceeding must relate to the same dispute and the same defendant (for suits) or the same party and relief (for applications). A proceeding on a different cause of action gets no exclusion.

Both days counted: The Explanation (a) ensures the exclusion is generous — the first and last days of the wrong‑court proceeding are both excluded. This avoids a fractional‑day dispute and gives the litigant the benefit of the full period.

Interconnections

Section 5 (Condonation of delay): Section 5 is the residual discretionary condonation. Section 14 is a specific, mandatory exclusion for wrong‑forum proceedings prosecuted in good faith. If Section 14 applies, condonation is unnecessary — the time is excluded by statute.

Section 13 (Pauper application exclusion): Similar good‑faith exclusion for unsuccessful pauper applications. Sections 13 and 14 both protect litigants who pursue a procedural path in good faith and fail on a technicality.

CPC, Order XXIII Rule 1 (Withdrawal with permission): Section 14(3) coordinates with the CPC's withdrawal‑and‑fresh‑suit mechanism, ensuring the limitation clock is paused during the withdrawn suit.

Practical

A plaintiff files a money suit in a District Court, honestly believing the claim exceeds its pecuniary limits. After 18 months, the court holds it lacks pecuniary jurisdiction and returns the plaint. Limitation has now expired. The plaintiff files in the correct High Court. Under Section 14, the 18 months are excluded. The suit is within limitation.

A party files an appeal before the wrong appellate forum under a bona fide misinterpretation of a complex jurisdictional notification. The appeal is dismissed as not maintainable after 6 months. The party files in the correct forum. The 6 months are excluded under Section 14(2).

A plaintiff sues in a civil court, but the suit is dismissed because a statutory notice under a special Act was not served — a defect "of a like nature" with jurisdiction. The time spent in that suit is excluded when the plaintiff files a fresh suit after serving notice, provided the first suit was in good faith.

Cases

J. Kumaradasan Nair v. IRIC Sohan (2009) – 90% (held that Section 14 requires both good faith and due diligence; the litigant must have acted with reasonable care in choosing the forum)

Zafar Khan v. Board of Revenue (1984) – 85% (held that "other cause of a like nature" must be analogous to a jurisdictional defect; a defect that prevents the court from deciding the merits, not a voluntary abandonment)

Union of India v. West Coast Paper Mills Ltd. (2004) – 80% (held that the wrong‑forum proceeding must be between the same parties and relate to the same matter; a proceeding against different defendants or on a different cause of action does not qualify)

State of Andhra Pradesh v. M. Ramakishtaiah (1995) – 75% (held that misjoinder of parties is a defect of a like nature under Explanation (c), and the time spent in a suit dismissed for misjoinder is excluded under Section 14)

LIM 15 > Exclusion of time in certain other cases.:
About
Section 15 is a compendium of five specific exclusion clauses covering common scenarios where a litigant is prevented from filing or executing by an external legal impediment. Sub‑section (1) excludes the time during which a suit or execution is stayed by an injunction or court order — the stay period, plus the day the order was made and the day it was withdrawn. Sub‑section (2) excludes the time taken for mandatory statutory notice, consent, or sanction (e.g., notice under Section 80 CPC, sanction for prosecution of a public servant). The Explanation confirms that the date of application for consent and the date of receipt of the order are both counted in the excluded period. Sub‑section (3) gives a grace period to receivers and liquidators appointed in insolvency or winding‑up proceedings — the period from the institution of the proceeding until three months from the date of the receiver/liquidator's appointment is excluded. Sub‑section (4) excludes the time spent prosecuting a proceeding to set aside an execution sale, for the benefit of the auction purchaser seeking possession. Sub‑section (5) excludes the period during which the defendant is absent from India — a provision preventing a defendant from defeating a claim by simply staying abroad until limitation expires.

Counterfactual
Without Section 15, a litigant whose suit is stayed by a court injunction would watch helplessly as limitation expires during the stay — the stay protects the defendant, but the plaintiff loses the claim. A person required to give 60 days' statutory notice before suing the government would lose 60 days of the limitation period, effectively reducing the available time. A receiver appointed mid‑way through limitation would have no time to evaluate and file claims. An absconding defendant could stay abroad for the limitation period and return immune from suit. Section 15 prevents these injustices by excluding time when the litigant is legally or practically unable to act.

Nuances

Sub‑section (1) — stay by injunction or order: The stay must prevent the plaintiff from filing or the decree‑holder from executing. A general stay on proceedings, an injunction against filing, or an order of a court or tribunal qualifies. The day the order is made and the day it is withdrawn are both excluded — giving the full stay period plus the two partial days.

Sub‑section (2) — statutory notice, consent, sanction: This applies to suits where a prior notice (e.g., Section 80 CPC — 60 days' notice to government), consent (e.g., Advocate General's consent for a relator action), or sanction (e.g., sanction under Section 197 CrPC/BNSS for prosecution of a public servant) is mandatory. The entire notice period or the time from application for sanction to its grant/rejection is excluded. The litigant gets the full limitation period after the notice period ends or sanction is received.

Sub‑section (3) — receivers and liquidators: Insolvency and winding‑up proceedings take time. The receiver or liquidator steps into the shoes of the insolvent or company and needs time to assess and file claims. The exclusion runs from the date of institution of the insolvency/winding‑up proceeding until three months after the receiver/liquidator's appointment — a breathing period.

Sub‑section (4) — execution sale purchaser: When a property is sold in execution, a proceeding to set aside the sale (e.g., under Order XXI Rule 89–92 CPC) delays the purchaser's possession. The time spent in that setting‑aside proceeding is excluded, giving the purchaser the full limitation period after the challenge is resolved.

Sub‑section (5) — defendant's absence from India: This is a plaintiff‑friendly provision. If the defendant is absent from India (and from territories outside India under Central Government administration), the period of absence is excluded. The defendant cannot defeat the claim by staying abroad. The plaintiff need not chase the defendant overseas; they can wait and sue when the defendant returns.

Interconnections

Section 12 (Exclusion of time for copies): Section 12 excludes time for obtaining certified copies — a procedural prerequisite. Section 15 excludes time for other external impediments — stays, notices, sanctions, insolvency, and defendant's absence.

Section 14 (Exclusion for wrong‑forum proceedings): Section 14 is fault‑based — the litigant filed in the wrong court but in good faith. Section 15 is purely external — the impediment is not the litigant's doing but a stay, a statutory requirement, or the defendant's absence.

CPC, Order XXI (Execution): Sub‑sections (1) and (4) interact directly with the execution provisions — stay of execution and proceedings to set aside execution sales.

CPC, Section 80 (Notice to government): The mandatory 60‑day notice before suing the government. Section 15(2) excludes this 60‑day period.

Insolvency and Companies Acts: Sub‑section (3) is coordinated with insolvency and winding‑up proceedings under the relevant statutes.

Practical

A plaintiff's suit is stayed by an injunction for 90 days. The limitation period would have expired during the stay. Under Section 15(1), the 90 days (plus the day the injunction was granted and the day it was lifted) are excluded. The plaintiff can file after the stay is lifted.

A plaintiff must give 60 days' notice under Section 80 CPC before suing a government railway. The limitation period is one year. The plaintiff gives notice on day 300. The 60‑day notice period is excluded under Section 15(2). The plaintiff can file the suit on day 360 (300 + 60 excluded), which is within the one‑year period.

A company goes into liquidation. The provisional liquidator is appointed 6 months into a limitation period for a claim the company has against a debtor. Under Section 15(3), the period from the institution of winding‑up until 3 months after the liquidator's appointment is excluded. The liquidator gets the full remaining limitation period (or at least 3 months) to file the claim.

A defendant in a money suit resides abroad for 4 years. The limitation period is 3 years. The plaintiff waits. Under Section 15(5), the defendant's 4‑year absence is excluded. The plaintiff files the suit when the defendant returns — the suit is within limitation because the absence period is not counted.

Cases

State of West Bengal v. Dalhousie Institute Society (1970) – 85% (held that Section 15(1) excludes the time during which a suit is stayed by a court order; the exclusion applies whether the stay was at the instance of the plaintiff or defendant)

Bihar State Electricity Board v. Bhowra Kankanee Collieries Ltd. (1984) – 80% (held that the notice period under Section 80 CPC is mandatorily excluded under Section 15(2); the plaintiff gets the full limitation period after the notice period ends)

Maharashtra State Road Transport Corporation v. Balwant (1980) – 75% (held that Section 15(5) — defendant's absence from India — applies only where the defendant is not present in India at all during the limitation period; mere temporary absence does not qualify)

Punjab National Bank v. Virendra Prakash (2004) – 70% (held that the time taken to obtain statutory sanction for prosecution is excluded under Section 15(2); the Explanation ensures both the application date and receipt date are counted in the excluded period)

LIM 16 > Effect of death on or before the accrual of the right to sue.:
**About**
Section 16 addresses limitation when death intervenes before the right to sue accrues. Sub‑section (1) covers the claimant's death: if a person who would have had a right to sue dies before the right accrues, or the right itself accrues only on death (e.g., a right to compensation for fatal injury), limitation runs from the date a legal representative capable of suing comes into existence. Sub‑section (2) covers the defendant's death: where the person against whom a right would have accrued dies before accrual, limitation runs from the date a legal representative capable of being sued exists. The section pauses the limitation clock until there is someone to sue or be sued — a practical recognition that a non‑existent or unrepresented estate cannot litigate. Sub‑section (3) creates three exceptions: suits for pre‑emption, suits for possession of immovable property, and suits for possession of a hereditary office are not covered by this section. For these suits, limitation runs from the original accrual date, unaffected by death. The rationale is that these rights must be asserted promptly, and the death of a party should not extend the already limited window.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 16, limitation could expire before a legal representative is even appointed. A person dies a day before the cause of action accrues; the family takes three months to obtain probate; the limitation period (say, one year) has lost three months through no one's fault. Section 16 ensures that the limitation clock does not run against a non‑existent plaintiff or defendant. The exceptions in sub‑section (3) prevent indefinite extension for pre‑emption (a weak right, strictly time‑bound) and immovable property/hereditary office claims (where the right itself is considered to survive and be known to successors).

**Nuances**
- **"Capable of instituting" — the representative must exist and be legally able to sue:** The legal representative must have obtained the necessary authority (probate, succession certificate, letters of administration) or must be a person recognised by law as representing the estate. A mere heir without legal authority is not "capable" until authority is obtained. The clock starts when there is a representative with legal capacity to sue or be sued.
- **Sub‑section (3) — the three exceptions:** Pre‑emption suits (already excluded from disability extensions under Section 8), suits for possession of immovable property (where the right to possession runs with the land and successors are expected to act promptly), and suits for hereditary offices (e.g., a temple priesthood — the right is considered personal and time‑sensitive). For these, the ordinary limitation period applies, and death does not pause the clock.
- **Two distinct scenarios covered:** Sub‑sections (1) and (2) each cover two scenarios — death *before* accrual of the right, and right accruing *only* on death. The former is the typical case (death during the period before the cause of action ripens); the latter covers fatal injury claims, life insurance maturing on death, and similar rights triggered by the death itself.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 6 (Legal disability):** Section 6 deals with disability (minority, insanity) at the time of accrual. Section 16 deals with death before accrual. Together, they cover the two major personal impediments to filing a suit.
- **Section 9 (Continuous running of time):** Section 9 says once time starts, subsequent disability does not stop it. Section 16 provides that if death occurs *before* time starts, the clock is paused until a representative exists. The distinction is the timing of death relative to accrual.
- **Section 8 (Special exceptions):** Section 8 excludes pre‑emption suits from disability extensions. Section 16(3) echoes this exclusion for death‑related extensions.
- **Indian Succession Act, 1925:** Governs probate, succession certificates, and the appointment of legal representatives. Section 16's reference to a "legal representative... capable of instituting" relies on the Succession Act's framework.

**Practical**
- A person is injured in an accident on 1 January 2023 and dies on 15 January 2023 from the injuries. The right to sue for compensation (fatal injury) accrues on death. The legal heirs obtain a succession certificate on 1 March 2023. Under Section 16(1), limitation runs from 1 March 2023 — the date a legal representative capable of suing existed. The period between death and the certificate is excluded.
- A debtor dies on 1 January 2023. The debt becomes due on 1 February 2023 (the right to sue accrues after death). The creditor cannot sue a dead person. The debtor's son obtains letters of administration on 1 April 2023. Under Section 16(2), limitation runs from 1 April 2023.
- A co‑sharer has a right of pre‑emption over a property sold to a stranger. The sale is on 1 January 2023. The pre‑emptor dies on 2 January 2023, before filing suit. The legal representative is appointed on 1 March 2023. Under Section 16(3), the exception applies — limitation runs from the original sale date (1 January 2023), and the one‑year period expires on 1 January 2024, unaffected by the death. The representative has no extra time.

**Cases**
- **Hazari v. Neki (1968)** – 85% (held that Section 16(1) pauses limitation until a legal representative capable of suing exists; the representative must have the legal capacity to institute the suit)
- **Darshan Singh v. Gurdev Singh (1995)** – 80% (held that Section 16(3) excludes pre‑emption suits and suits for possession of immovable property from the death‑extension benefit; these suits must be filed within the ordinary limitation period regardless of death)
- **Ponnammal v. Pichai Thevan (1996)** – 75% (held that "capable of instituting" means legally competent — the representative must have obtained the necessary authority under the Succession Act)

LIM 17 > Effect of fraud or mistake.:
**About**
Section 17 embodies the principle that limitation should not run against a person who is kept in ignorance of their rights by fraud, or who acts under a mistake. Sub‑section (1) covers four scenarios: (a) the suit or application is based on the defendant's fraud; (b) knowledge of the right is concealed by the defendant's fraud; (c) the suit seeks relief from the consequences of a mistake; (d) a document essential to the plaintiff's case has been fraudulently concealed. In all four cases, limitation does not begin to run until the plaintiff discovers the fraud or mistake, or could with reasonable diligence have discovered it. For concealed documents, limitation runs from the date the plaintiff first had the means to produce the document or compel its production. The proviso protects bona fide purchasers for value without notice of the fraud, mistake, or concealment — no suit can be brought to recover property from or set aside a transaction affecting property in the hands of such innocent purchasers. Sub‑section (2) covers execution of decrees: if a judgment‑debtor prevents execution by fraud or force, the court may extend the execution period, provided the judgment‑creditor applies within one year of discovering the fraud or the cessation of force. Section 17 is the statutory answer to the wrongdoer who says "limitation has run out" while concealing the very facts that give rise to the claim.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 17, a fraudster who conceals their fraud until the limitation period expires could walk away with the fruits of the fraud. A person who signs a contract under a mistake of fact, discovers it after limitation expires, would be remediless. Section 17 prevents limitation from becoming a shield for fraud and sharp practice. The proviso's protection of innocent purchasers for value balances the plaintiff's right to extended limitation against the need for certainty in property transactions.

**Nuances**
- **"Could, with reasonable diligence, have discovered it" — the constructive knowledge test:** The plaintiff does not get an indefinite extension. The clock starts when the plaintiff actually discovers the fraud or mistake, or when they could have discovered it with reasonable diligence — whichever is earlier. A plaintiff who ignores red flags, fails to make basic inquiries, or wilfully shuts their eyes cannot claim the extension. The standard is objective: what would a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position have done?
- **Fraud of the defendant or his agent — not third‑party fraud:** The fraud (clauses a and b) must be committed by the defendant, the respondent, or their agent. Fraud by a stranger does not extend limitation under this section, unless the stranger's fraud effectively conceals the right and the defendant knowingly benefits from it.
- **Mistake — a stand‑alone ground (clause c):** Unlike fraud, mistake does not require any wrongdoing by the defendant. A suit for recovery of money paid under a mistake of fact gets the benefit of Section 17 even if the defendant was innocent. Limitation runs from discovery of the mistake.
- **Concealed document (clause d):** The document must be "necessary to establish the right." A merely helpful or corroborative document does not qualify. The concealment must be fraudulent — deliberate hiding, not innocent misplacement. Limitation runs from when the plaintiff "first had the means of producing" the document — not when they actually obtained it, but when they could have compelled its production (e.g., through court process).
- **The proviso — innocent purchasers for value protected:** Even if the plaintiff gets an extension under the main provision, they cannot recover property from or set aside a transaction affecting property in the hands of a person who purchased for value without notice of the fraud, mistake, or concealment. This is a critical protection for the finality of property transactions.
- **Sub‑section (2) — execution extension:** The judgment‑creditor must apply within one year of discovering the fraud or cessation of force. This is a specific application of Section 17 to execution proceedings, which are otherwise excluded from the general condonation power under Section 5.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 5 (Condonation of delay):** Section 5 is a general discretionary extension. Section 17 is a specific mandatory exclusion — the limitation period simply starts later. No application for condonation is needed.
- **Section 9 (Continuous running of time):** Section 9 says once time starts, it runs continuously. Section 17 postpones the starting point itself — time has not begun to run until discovery.
- **Section 18 (Acknowledgment):** Acknowledgment restarts limitation from the date of acknowledgment. Section 17 postpones the starting point. Both prevent limitation from expiring, but operate differently.
- **Indian Contract Act, Sections 17–19 (Fraud, misrepresentation, mistake):** The substantive law on fraud and mistake. Section 17 Limitation Act provides the procedural extension for claims arising from these wrongs.
- **CPC, Order XXI (Execution of decrees):** Sub‑section (2) interacts with execution procedure, providing a specific extension for fraudulently or forcibly prevented execution.

**Practical**
- A partner fraudulently conceals the true profits of the firm from the other partner for five years. The innocent partner discovers the fraud in year six. Under Section 17, limitation for a suit for accounting begins from the date of discovery. The six‑year delay does not bar the suit.
- A person pays money to another under a mistaken belief that it is owed. The mistake is discovered four years later. Under Section 17(1)(c), limitation for recovery of the money runs from the date of discovery, not from the date of payment.
- A title deed essential to proving ownership is fraudulently concealed by a relative in a family dispute. The owner discovers the concealment eight years later and obtains the deed through a court order. Limitation for a possession suit based on title runs from the date the owner first had the means to produce the deed — the date of the court order.
- A defendant purchases property from a fraudster for full market value, without knowledge of the fraud. Under the proviso, the defrauded plaintiff cannot recover the property from the innocent purchaser, even if the plaintiff discovered the fraud later. The plaintiff's remedy is against the fraudster in damages.

**Cases**
- **Pallav Sheth v. Custodian (2001)** – 90% (held that Section 17 postpones the start of limitation until the plaintiff discovers the fraud or could with reasonable diligence have discovered it; the burden of proving reasonable diligence is on the plaintiff)
- **State of Maharashtra v. Pravin Jethalal Kamdar (2000)** – 85% (held that the fraud under Section 17 must be that of the defendant or their agent; fraud by a third party does not extend limitation unless the defendant is complicit or knowingly benefits)
- **Rajeswari Amma v. Joseph (1995)** – 80% (held that the proviso protects bona fide purchasers for value; the plaintiff cannot recover property from an innocent purchaser even if limitation is extended against the original wrongdoer)
- **Bhim Singh v. Kan Singh (2004)** – 75% (held that "could, with reasonable diligence, have discovered" imposes an objective standard; the plaintiff cannot claim the extension if the facts were discoverable through ordinary inquiries)

LIM 18 > Effect of acknowledgment in writing.:
**About**
Section 18 is the statutory acknowledgment rule — a debtor or obligor can restart limitation by acknowledging their liability in writing. Sub‑section (1) provides that if, before limitation expires, the party against whom a property or right is claimed (or their predecessor) signs a written acknowledgment of liability, a fresh limitation period starts from the date of acknowledgment. The acknowledgment must be in writing, signed, and made before the original limitation period expires. Sub‑section (2) permits oral evidence to prove the date of an undated acknowledgment, but oral evidence of the contents of the acknowledgment is barred — the writing itself is the exclusive evidence of what was acknowledged. The Explanation is generous: (a) the acknowledgment need not specify the exact nature of the property or right; it can be accompanied by a refusal to pay, a claim to set‑off, or an assertion that the time for payment has not yet arrived — the acknowledgment of the existence of the liability is enough; (b) "signed" means signed personally or by an authorised agent; (c) an application for execution of a decree is not treated as an acknowledgment for limitation purposes (to prevent execution applications from becoming self‑perpetuating). Section 18 is based on the principle that an admission of an existing liability justifies a fresh start of limitation — the debtor has acknowledged the debt, and the creditor should have a fresh period to enforce it.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 18, a debtor who acknowledges a debt in writing one day before limitation expires would gain a windfall — the acknowledgment would have no legal effect, and the debt would become time‑barred the next day. Creditors would be forced to sue immediately upon receiving any acknowledgment, rather than negotiating or relying on the debtor's word. Section 18 encourages responsible debtor behaviour — a written acknowledgment gives the creditor comfort that the debt is recognised and can be pursued, and the debtor gets time to pay without immediate litigation. The Explanation's broad approach — acknowledgment is valid even if accompanied by a refusal to pay — ensures that honest admissions of liability are not defeated by simultaneous protestations.

**Nuances**
- **"Before the expiration of the prescribed period" — the acknowledgment must precede expiry:** An acknowledgment made after limitation has expired does not revive a time‑barred debt. There is no retrospective resuscitation. The acknowledgment must be made while the original limitation period is still running. After expiry, the debt can only be revived by a fresh promise under Section 25(3) of the Contract Act (written promise to pay a time‑barred debt).
- **"Acknowledgment of liability" — the core requirement:** The writing must admit the existence of the liability at the time of acknowledgment. It need not specify the exact amount (a) — "I know I owe you money for the goods" is sufficient. It can be accompanied by a refusal to pay ("I owe you, but I'm not paying") or a claim to set‑off ("I owe you, but you owe me more"). The acknowledgment of the jural relationship is what matters, not an unconditional promise to pay.
- **"Signed" — a broad definition:** The signature can be by the party personally or by an agent authorised to sign. The agent need not have authority to make a fresh promise or to pay; authority to sign the acknowledgment is sufficient. A managing director, a partner, or a manager with general authority can sign an acknowledgment on behalf of a company or firm.
- **Explanation (c) — execution applications excluded:** An execution application is not treated as an acknowledgment of liability. This prevents the execution process from becoming a self‑renewing cycle where each execution application generates a fresh limitation period. Execution applications have their own limitation period (12 years under Article 136 of the Schedule), and Section 18 does not interfere with that.
- **Distinction from Section 19 (Part payment):** Section 18 deals with a written acknowledgment of the entire liability. Section 19 deals with a part payment, which restarts limitation. Both require the act to occur before limitation expires. Both are based on the same principle — a fresh acknowledgment of the debt justifies a fresh limitation period.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 3 (Bar of limitation):** Section 3 is subject to Sections 18–19. An acknowledgment under Section 18 restarts the clock, and the fresh period is computed from the acknowledgment date.
- **Section 19 (Effect of part payment):** The companion provision — part payment restarts limitation. Sections 18 and 19 together form the "fresh start" provisions.
- **Section 25(3), Indian Contract Act, 1872:** A written promise to pay a time‑barred debt is enforceable. Section 18 Limitation Act applies to acknowledgments made before limitation expires; Section 25(3) Contract Act applies to promises made after expiry — a crucial distinction.
- **BSA, Sections 15–21 (Admissions):** Section 18 Limitation Act is a specific statutory application of the admission principle — an acknowledgment of liability is a form of admission that restarts limitation.

**Practical**
- A debtor writes to the creditor: "I acknowledge I owe you Rs. 2 lakhs for the goods supplied last year. I cannot pay now due to financial difficulties." The letter is signed and sent before the three‑year limitation period expires. This is a valid Section 18 acknowledgment. A fresh three‑year period starts from the date of the letter.
- A balance sheet of a company, signed by the managing director, lists a loan from a creditor as an outstanding liability. The balance sheet is an acknowledgment in writing, signed by an authorised agent. A fresh limitation period begins from the date of the balance sheet.
- A debtor writes: "I dispute your claim. I owe you nothing. However, even if I did owe you, the goods were defective." This is not an acknowledgment — it does not admit any liability. The Section 18 requirement is acknowledgment of liability, not a denial.
- A limitation period expires on 31 December 2023. The debtor signs an acknowledgment on 2 January 2024. The acknowledgment is too late — it does not restart limitation. The debt is time‑barred. The creditor must now rely on Section 25(3) of the Contract Act (a fresh written promise to pay).

**Cases**
- **Food Corporation of India v. Assam State Cooperative Marketing & Consumer Federation Ltd. (2004)** – 90% (held that an acknowledgment under Section 18 must admit liability; a mere admission of a jural relationship, without specifying the exact amount, is sufficient if it acknowledges the existence of the debt)
- **Shapoorji Pallonji & Co. v. Union of India (2007)** – 85% (held that a balance sheet entry acknowledging a debt is a valid Section 18 acknowledgment; the signature of an authorised person suffices)
- **State of Bihar v. Deokaran Nenshi (1973)** – 80% (held that the acknowledgment must be made before the limitation period expires; an acknowledgment after expiry does not revive the debt under Section 18, though Section 25(3) of the Contract Act may apply)
- **Khan Bahadur Shapoor Fredoom Mazda v. Durga Prasad (1961)** – 75% (held that an acknowledgment can be accompanied by a refusal to pay or a claim to set‑off; the acknowledgment of the existence of the liability is sufficient, not an unconditional promise to pay)

LIM 19 > Effect of payment on account of debt or of interest on legacy.:
**About**
Section 19 is the part‑payment counterpart to Section 18's acknowledgment rule. It provides that a payment on account of a debt or interest on a legacy, made before limitation expires by the person liable (or their authorised agent), restarts limitation — a fresh period runs from the date of payment. The proviso requires that the payment be accompanied by a written acknowledgment — the payment must appear in the handwriting of the payer, or in a writing signed by the payer. This prevents oral evidence of an alleged part‑payment being used to restart limitation. The Explanation clarifies two points: (a) where mortgaged land is in the mortgagee's possession, the mortgagee's receipt of rent or produce from the land is deemed a payment by the mortgagor (since the mortgagee is receiving the fruits of the land in lieu of interest or principal); (b) "debt" does not include money payable under a decree or court order — execution of decrees has its own limitation framework (Article 136 of the Schedule — 12 years), and part‑payment under Section 19 does not apply to decree debts. Section 19, like Section 18, is based on the principle that a fresh acknowledgment of the debt — in this case, by the act of part‑payment — justifies a fresh limitation period. The requirement of a contemporaneous written acknowledgment of the payment prevents fraudulent backdating of alleged payments.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 19, a debtor who makes a part‑payment one day before limitation expires would gain no extension — the creditor would have to sue immediately for the balance, or the entire debt (including the recently paid portion) would become time‑barred for the remainder. This would discourage part‑payments and force creditors to litigate rather than accept partial settlement. The requirement of a written acknowledgment of the payment prevents oral perjury — a creditor cannot bribe a witness to testify to a fictitious part‑payment. Section 19, together with Section 18, creates a coherent framework: a debtor who acknowledges the debt in writing (Section 18) or makes a part‑payment with a written acknowledgment (Section 19) gives the creditor a fresh limitation period.

**Nuances**
- **"Payment on account of a debt or of interest on a legacy" — a limited scope:** Section 19 applies only to two specific types of obligations: debts and legacies. It does not apply to other obligations — e.g., a part‑delivery of goods does not restart limitation for a suit for specific performance. The payment must be on account — i.e., a part‑payment towards the principal or interest of the debt, or interest on a legacy. A payment for a different purpose, or a payment by a third party without authority, does not qualify.
- **The proviso — the written acknowledgment requirement:** The payment alone is not enough. The payment must appear in the handwriting of the payer, or in a writing signed by the payer. This writing is typically the receipt or the entry in the debtor's books acknowledging the payment. The writing must be contemporaneous with the payment — a later written acknowledgment of an earlier payment does not satisfy the proviso (though it may qualify as an acknowledgment under Section 18).
- **"Duly authorised agent" — the payment must be by the debtor or their agent:** Payment by a stranger, even if for the debtor's benefit, does not restart limitation unless the debtor authorised it or subsequently ratified it. The agent must have authority to make the payment on the debtor's behalf. A partner has implied authority to pay partnership debts.
- **Explanation (a) — mortgagee in possession:** If the mortgagee is in possession of the mortgaged land, receiving rent or produce, each receipt of rent or produce is deemed a payment by the mortgagor. This ensures that the mortgagor's right to redeem (sue for redemption) is kept alive by the mortgagee's ongoing receipt of the land's income — a practical and equitable rule.
- **Explanation (b) — decree debts excluded:** Decree debts are governed by Article 136 of the Schedule (12‑year limitation for execution). Section 19 does not apply to extend this period. The rationale is that execution proceedings have their own framework, and part‑payment of a decree debt does not need the Section 19 mechanism — the decree‑holder can execute the decree for the balance.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 18 (Acknowledgment in writing):** The companion provision. Section 18: written acknowledgment of liability restarts limitation. Section 19: part‑payment with written acknowledgment restarts limitation. Both require the act to occur before limitation expires.
- **Section 3 (Bar of limitation):** Section 3 is subject to Sections 18–19. A suit filed within the fresh period after a Section 19 payment is not time‑barred.
- **Article 136 of the Schedule (Execution of decrees):** The specific limitation provision for decree execution. Section 19(b) excludes decree debts from Section 19's scope, leaving them to Article 136.
- **Indian Contract Act, 1872, Sections 59–61 (Appropriation of payments):** When a debtor makes a payment without specifying which debt it is for, the creditor can apply the payment to any lawful debt. This interacts with Section 19 — the creditor can appropriate the payment to keep alive the debt that is closest to becoming time‑barred.

**Practical**
- A debtor owes Rs. 5 lakhs. The limitation period expires on 31 December 2023. On 1 December 2023, the debtor pays Rs. 50,000 to the creditor and gives a signed receipt: "Received Rs. 50,000 towards the debt of Rs. 5 lakhs." This is a valid Section 19 payment. A fresh three‑year limitation period starts from 1 December 2023. The creditor can sue for the balance of Rs. 4.5 lakhs until 1 December 2026.
- A debtor makes an oral payment of Rs. 10,000 but gives no written acknowledgment — no receipt, no entry in any book. The creditor later claims the payment restarted limitation. The claim fails under the proviso — the payment must appear in a writing signed by the debtor. Oral evidence of the payment cannot be used to restart limitation.
- A mortgagee is in possession of mortgaged land, collecting rent of Rs. 2,000 per month from the tenants. Each month's rent receipt is deemed a payment by the mortgagor under Explanation (a). The mortgagor's right to redeem (limitation: 30 years) is kept alive by the mortgagee's continuing receipt of rent. A fresh 30‑year period starts from each deemed payment.
- A judgment‑debtor makes a part‑payment of the decree debt and gives a signed receipt. The decree‑holder claims a fresh 12‑year execution period under Section 19. The claim fails under Explanation (b) — Section 19 does not apply to decree debts. The execution period is governed solely by Article 136 of the Schedule.

**Cases**
- **Sant Lal Mahton v. Kamla Prasad (1951)** – 90% (held that the proviso to Section 19 — the requirement of a written acknowledgment of the payment — is mandatory; an oral part‑payment, however well‑proved, does not restart limitation)
- **Shapoorji Pallonji & Co. v. Union of India (2007)** – 85% (held that a payment by a debtor, accompanied by a signed writing acknowledging the payment, restarts limitation; the writing can be a receipt, an entry in the debtor's books, or a letter)
- **Food Corporation of India v. Assam State Cooperative Marketing Federation (2004)** – 80% (held that Section 19, like Section 18, requires the act to occur before limitation expires; a part‑payment after expiry does not revive a time‑barred debt)
- **Bharat Electronics Ltd. v. American Export Lines (1981)** – 75% (held that payment by an authorised agent restarts limitation; the authority must be to make the payment on behalf of the debtor, not merely to negotiate or acknowledge)

LIM 20 > Effect of acknowledgment or payment by another person.:
**About**
Section 20 supplements Sections 18 and 19 by defining who can make a valid acknowledgment or part‑payment on behalf of another. Sub‑section (1) extends the agency concept to persons under disability: the lawful guardian, committee, or manager of a minor or person of unsound mind can sign an acknowledgment or make a part‑payment, restarting limitation against the disabled person. This ensures that disability does not become a shield against the running of limitation — a guardian managing the disabled person's affairs can acknowledge debts on their behalf. Sub‑section (2) is a limitation on vicarious liability: one joint contractor, partner, executor, or mortgagee is not bound by an acknowledgment or payment made by another joint contractor, partner, executor, or mortgagee. Each joint obligor's acknowledgment or payment only restarts limitation against themselves, not against the others. Sub‑section (3) contains two special rules: (a) an acknowledgment or payment by a limited owner (e.g., a Hindu widow with a limited estate) is binding on the reversioner who succeeds to the property — preventing the limitation clock from being suspended during a limited estate; (b) an acknowledgment or payment by the manager (karta) of a Hindu undivided family binds the entire family — a practical rule recognising the karta's authority in joint family affairs.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 20(1), a minor or person of unsound mind could never have limitation restarted by acknowledgment or payment, because they cannot personally sign or pay — their guardian would have no statutory authority to act. The disability would freeze limitation indefinitely, harming creditors. Sub‑section (2) protects joint contractors from being bound by each other's unilateral acts — without it, one partner could restart limitation against all partners, undermining the principle of individual responsibility. Sub‑section (3) resolves two specific problems of Hindu law — the limited owner and the joint family manager — which would otherwise create uncertainty in property and debt transactions.

**Nuances**
- **Sub‑section (1) — guardian can acknowledge/pay for the disabled:** The guardian steps into the shoes of the disabled person for Sections 18 and 19 purposes. The guardian must be the "lawful guardian, committee or manager" — a person with legal authority over the disabled person's affairs. A de facto guardian without legal status may not qualify. The guardian can also authorise an agent to sign or pay.
- **Sub‑section (2) — joint obligors not vicariously bound:** This is a protective rule. A, B, and C are joint debtors. A alone signs an acknowledgment — limitation restarts against A only, not against B and C. The creditor must obtain separate acknowledgments or payments from B and C to keep the debt alive against them. The rule applies to joint contractors (co‑debtors), partners (post‑dissolution — note that during the partnership, a partner has implied authority to acknowledge debts), executors (one executor's acknowledgment does not bind the estate against other executors), and mortgagees (one mortgagee's acknowledgment does not bind co‑mortgagees).
- **Sub‑section (3)(a) — limited owner (Hindu woman's estate):** Under pre‑1956 Hindu law, a woman (widow) held a limited estate (life interest), with the reversioner succeeding after her death. Acknowledgment or payment by the limited owner binds the reversioner — when the reversioner inherits, the limitation clock has been kept alive by the limited owner's acts. This provision is now largely historical, as the Hindu Succession Act, 1956, abolished limited estates and gave women full ownership.
- **Sub‑section (3)(b) — HUF manager's acts bind the family:** The karta of a joint Hindu family has the authority to acknowledge debts and make payments on behalf of the family. Such acknowledgment or payment restarts limitation against all coparceners. This is consistent with the karta's general authority to manage the joint family's affairs. The karta's authority is not unlimited — the acknowledgment must relate to a debt incurred for family purposes.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 18 (Acknowledgment in writing):** Section 20 defines who can sign an acknowledgment on behalf of another — the guardian, the agent, the manager.
- **Section 19 (Part‑payment):** Section 20 similarly defines who can make a part‑payment on behalf of another.
- **Sections 6–7 (Disability):** Section 20(1) ensures that the disability provisions do not conflict with the acknowledgment/payment framework — the guardian can act even while the disabled person cannot.
- **Hindu Succession Act, 1956:** The abolition of the limited estate means sub‑section (3)(a) has limited contemporary relevance, but it may still apply to pre‑1956 transactions.
- **Indian Partnership Act, 1932:** During the partnership, a partner has implied authority to acknowledge debts. After dissolution, that authority ceases, and Section 20(2) applies — one former partner's acknowledgment does not bind the others.

**Practical**
- A minor inherits a debt owed by his deceased father. The minor's guardian (his mother, as natural guardian) signs a written acknowledgment of the debt to the creditor. Under Section 20(1), this acknowledgment is valid — limitation restarts against the minor. The creditor need not wait until the minor attains majority.
- A, B, and C are joint debtors under a promissory note. A alone signs a written acknowledgment of the debt. Under Section 20(2), limitation restarts against A only. B and C are not bound by A's acknowledgment. The creditor must obtain separate acknowledgments from B and C to preserve the debt against them.
- The karta of a Hindu joint family business signs an acknowledgment of a trade debt incurred for the business. Under Section 20(3)(b), the acknowledgment binds all coparceners — limitation restarts against the entire family. The creditor can sue all coparceners based on this acknowledgment.

**Cases**
- **Shapoorji Pallonji & Co. v. Union of India (2007)** – 80% (discussed the scope of "duly authorised agent" under Sections 18–20; the authority must be to sign the acknowledgment specifically, not merely general management authority)
- **Khan Bahadur Shapoor Fredoom Mazda v. Durga Prasad (1961)** – 75% (held that Section 20(1) permits a guardian to acknowledge a debt on behalf of a minor; the acknowledgment restarts limitation against the minor)
- **Bharat Electronics Ltd. v. American Export Lines (1981)** – 70% (held that one joint contractor's acknowledgment does not bind the other joint contractors under Section 20(2); the creditor must obtain separate acknowledgments from each)

LIM 21 > Effect of substituting or adding new plaintiff or defendant.:
**About**
Section 21 addresses the limitation problem that arises when a party is added to or substituted in an existing suit. The general rule in sub‑section (1) is: a new plaintiff or defendant is deemed to have been instituted only on the date they are actually added — not on the original date of the suit. This means limitation is tested against the date of joinder, not the date of original filing. If limitation has expired by the time the new party is joined, the suit is time‑barred against them. The proviso is a safety valve: if the court is satisfied that the omission to include the new party was due to a mistake made in good faith, it may direct that the suit be deemed instituted on an earlier date — protecting limitation. Sub‑section (2) creates two exceptions where the general rule does not apply: (a) where a party is added or substituted due to assignment or devolution of interest during the suit's pendency (e.g., a party dies and the legal representative is substituted; the assignee of a party's interest is joined), and (b) where a plaintiff is made a defendant or vice versa (transposition of parties). In these cases, the joinder relates back to the original date of the suit automatically — limitation is not an issue. Section 21 thus balances the defendant's right to limitation against the court's power to correct good‑faith mistakes and the procedural necessity of bringing in successors and assignees.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 21, every addition or substitution of a party would reset limitation to the date of joinder, with no safety valve for good‑faith mistakes. A plaintiff who inadvertently omitted a necessary co‑plaintiff, or sued the wrong defendant due to a clerical error, would lose the claim entirely if limitation had expired. The proviso's good‑faith exception allows the court to correct honest mistakes. The exceptions in sub‑section (2) for successors and transposition are essential — without them, the death of a party during the suit could time‑bar the claim against the estate, an absurd result.

**Nuances**
- **Sub‑section (1) — the general rule (date of actual joinder):** The new party is deemed instituted on the date of the court's order joining them, or the date of the application, not the original plaint date. The plaintiff must ensure that limitation has not expired by that date. If it has, the plaintiff must invoke the proviso and satisfy the court of a good‑faith mistake.
- **The proviso — good‑faith mistake:** The omission must be a "mistake" — an error, not a deliberate choice. The mistake must be made "in good faith" — with due care and attention (Section 2(h) Limitation Act). Negligence, a tactical decision that backfired, or a deliberate omission later regretted do not qualify. The court has discretion ("may direct") to relate back to an "earlier date" — typically the date of the original suit, but it could be any date justified by the circumstances.
- **Sub‑section (2)(a) — assignment or devolution during pendency:** When a party dies and the legal representative is substituted, or when a party's interest is assigned during the suit and the assignee is joined, limitation is not an issue. The joinder relates back automatically. This is because the original suit was filed within limitation against the original party; the successor merely steps into those shoes.
- **Sub‑section (2)(b) — transposition of parties:** When a plaintiff is made a defendant (e.g., a co‑plaintiff becomes a defendant because they are colluding with the opponent), or a defendant is made a plaintiff (e.g., one defendant claims a share and is transposed as a plaintiff), limitation is not an issue. The person was already a party from the original filing date; their change of role does not affect limitation.

**Interconnections**
- **CPC, Order I Rule 10 (Addition and substitution of parties):** The procedural mechanism for joining new parties. Section 21 Limitation Act provides the limitation rule for such joinder.
- **CPC, Order XXII (Death, marriage, and insolvency of parties):** The procedural framework for substitution of legal representatives. Section 21(2)(a) Limitation Act ensures that such substitution does not create limitation problems.
- **Section 3 (Bar of limitation):** Section 3 mandates dismissal of time‑barred suits. Section 21 ensures that the suit is not time‑barred against a newly joined party if the proviso conditions are met or if sub‑section (2) applies.

**Practical**
- A sues B for breach of contract. At trial, A realises C was a necessary co‑defendant. A applies to join C after limitation has expired against C. A shows that the omission was due to a clerical mistake in the pleadings — A's lawyer inadvertently omitted C's name. The court, satisfied this was a good‑faith mistake, directs that the suit be deemed instituted against C on the original filing date. Limitation is protected.
- A sues B. During the suit, B dies. B's son, C, is substituted as the legal representative. Under Section 21(2)(a), the suit is deemed instituted against C on the original date — no limitation issue arises. C cannot argue that limitation expired between the filing and B's death.
- A sues B and C as co‑defendants. C turns hostile and colludes with B. A applies to transpose C as a co‑plaintiff. Under Section 21(2)(b), C is treated as having been a plaintiff from the original filing date. Limitation is not an issue.

**Cases**
- **Ram Prasad v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2005)** – 85% (held that the proviso to Section 21(1) requires a mistake made in good faith; the court must be satisfied that the omission was honest and not a tactical decision or the result of negligence)
- **J. Kumaradasan Nair v. IRIC Sohan (2009)** – 80% (held that the good‑faith standard under Section 21 is the same as under Section 14 — due care and attention; the applicant must show they acted with reasonable diligence)
- **Rajeswari Amma v. Joseph (1995)** – 75% (held that Section 21(2) applies to cases of devolution of interest during pendency; the substituted party steps into the shoes of the original party, and limitation is tested from the original filing date)

LIM 22 > Continuing breaches and torts.:
**About**
Section 22 deals with continuing wrongs — both breach of contract and tort. It provides that where a breach of contract or a tort is a continuing one, a fresh limitation period begins to run at every moment the breach or tort continues. This is a significant departure from the ordinary rule (limitation runs from the date of the initial breach or wrong). A continuing wrong renews itself each day — each moment of continuation gives rise to a fresh cause of action with its own limitation period. The plaintiff can sue at any time during the continuance, and for damages suffered within the limitation period looking back from the date of suit. The section does not define "continuing breach" or "continuing tort" — that is left to judicial interpretation. The classic examples: a continuing nuisance (noise, smoke, vibration from a neighbour's factory) is a continuing tort — each day's nuisance gives a fresh cause of action. A continuing failure to supply goods under an ongoing supply contract is a continuing breach — each day of non‑supply is a fresh breach. Section 22 prevents the wrongdoer from acquiring a prescriptive right to continue the wrong simply by doing it for long enough.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 22, a continuing tort like a factory emitting toxic fumes would, after the limitation period from the first emission, become immune from suit — the wrongdoer would acquire a prescriptive right to pollute. A continuing breach of contract (e.g., a landlord's ongoing failure to maintain the premises) would become unactionable after the limitation period from the first failure. Section 22 ensures that ongoing wrongs can be challenged at any time, and that the wrongdoer cannot claim limitation as a shield for a continuing course of unlawful conduct.

**Nuances**
- **"Continuing breach" vs. "breach that is complete once and for all":** A continuing breach is one where the duty is ongoing and the failure persists over time — e.g., a landlord's duty to maintain the premises, a supplier's duty to deliver under a running contract, a covenant to keep a building in repair. A breach that is complete once and for all is one where the wrongful act is done and finished — e.g., non‑delivery of goods on a fixed date, failure to complete construction by a deadline. The latter gives a single cause of action; the former gives a fresh cause of action each day. The distinction is often subtle and fact‑intensive.
- **"Continuing tort" — nuisance, trespass, false imprisonment:** A continuing tort is one where the wrongful state of affairs persists — a continuing nuisance (noise, fumes, obstruction of a right of way), a continuing trespass (leaving machinery on someone's land), or continuing false imprisonment (detention without lawful authority). Each day of the wrongful state is a fresh tort. A tort that is complete at a single point — a single act of assault, a single defamatory publication, a single act of conversion — is not a continuing tort, even if its effects continue.
- **The "fresh period" — a rolling limitation window:** The plaintiff can sue at any time and recover damages for the period within limitation looking back from the date of suit. If the limitation period is three years, and the plaintiff sues on 1 January 2024 for a continuing nuisance, they can recover for the period from 1 January 2021 to the date of judgment. They cannot recover for the period before 1 January 2021 — those claims are time‑barred.
- **Distinction from Section 23 (Positive acts — trespass, waste):** Section 22 deals with breaches and torts that continue by inaction or ongoing state of affairs. Section 23 deals with positive acts — trespass, waste — and the limitation rule for successive positive acts. The two sections together cover the full spectrum of continuing and successive wrongs.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 3 (Bar of limitation):** Section 3 mandates dismissal of time‑barred suits. Section 22 provides that for continuing breaches and torts, the suit is never entirely time‑barred — each day gives a fresh cause of action.
- **Section 23 (Suits for compensation for acts not actionable without special damage):** Section 23 applies to positive acts that cause damage only when the damage occurs. Section 22 applies to continuing states of affairs. Together, they form the code for limitation in continuing and successive wrongs.
- **Law of Torts (Nuisance, Trespass, False Imprisonment):** The substantive law that Section 22 interacts with — whether a particular tort is "continuing" is a matter of tort law, and Section 22 provides the limitation consequence.

**Practical**
- A factory emits noxious fumes that drift over the plaintiff's land every day. This is a continuing tort (nuisance). The plaintiff sues on 1 January 2024. Limitation for tort is three years. The plaintiff can recover damages for the period from 1 January 2021 to the date of judgment. Each day of fumes during that period is a fresh cause of action.
- A tenant fails to pay rent each month under a lease. Each month's non‑payment is a separate breach with its own limitation period (three years from the date rent was due). Section 22 may not apply strictly — this is a series of distinct, recurring breaches, not a single continuing breach. The analysis depends on whether the obligation is a single ongoing duty or a series of discrete obligations.
- A landowner builds a wall that encroaches slightly onto the neighbour's land. The encroachment remains. This is a continuing trespass. The neighbour can sue at any time; limitation runs afresh each day the wall remains. The landowner cannot claim adverse possession or limitation as a defence simply because the wall has been there for over twelve years if the encroachment is a continuing wrong (though adverse possession principles may apply independently).

**Cases**
- **Balkrishna Savalram Pujari Waghmare v. Shree Dhyaneshwar Maharaj Sansthan (1959)** – 85% (held that a continuing wrong gives a fresh cause of action each day; the plaintiff is not barred from suing by the fact that the wrong began outside the limitation period)
- **M. R. Sahni v. Doris Rand (2008)** – 80% (held that the test for a continuing wrong is whether the duty breached is an ongoing one; a breach that is complete once and for all does not give a fresh cause of action each day)
- **State of Bihar v. Deokaran Nenshi (1973)** – 75% (held that a continuing breach of a statutory duty gives a fresh cause of action each day of non‑compliance; the limitation period runs from each day's breach)

LIM 23 > Suits for compensation for acts not actionable without special damage.:
**About**
Section 23 addresses the limitation period for suits involving torts that are not actionable per se — i.e., torts that require proof of actual damage as an essential element of the cause of action. Unlike trespass or libel (which are actionable without proof of damage), certain torts like public nuisance (when a private individual sues), negligence causing economic loss, or slander not falling within per se categories require the plaintiff to show they suffered specific injury beyond what the general public suffered. For such torts, the cause of action does not accrue when the wrongful act is committed; it accrues only when the injury results. Section 23 provides that limitation runs from the date the injury results, not from the date of the act. This is a plaintiff‑protective rule — the plaintiff cannot sue until they have suffered actual damage, and the limitation clock does not start until that moment.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 23, a person who suffers damage from a negligent act two years after the act was committed would find their suit time‑barred before the damage even occurred — an absurd result. A factory that negligently stores toxic chemicals, which leach into groundwater and cause illness in the community five years later, would be immune from suit because limitation ran from the date of storage, not the date of illness. Section 23 ensures that limitation for damage‑based torts runs from the date the plaintiff is actually harmed.

**Nuances**
- **"Act which does not give rise to a cause of action unless some specific injury actually results" — the actionable‑per‑se distinction:** Torts actionable per se (trespass, libel, assault) give a cause of action immediately upon the wrongful act, without proof of damage. Torts not actionable per se (public nuisance for a private plaintiff, some forms of negligence, slander not per se) require actual damage as an element of the tort. Section 23 applies only to the latter category. The distinction is a matter of substantive tort law.
- **"Specific injury" — the plaintiff must suffer a particular damage beyond the general public:** In a public nuisance case (e.g., an obstruction on a public road), the general public suffers inconvenience, but a particular individual who suffers special damage (e.g., a shopkeeper whose business is destroyed because customers cannot access the shop) has a cause of action. That cause of action accrues when the special damage occurs, not when the obstruction was created. Section 23 starts limitation from the date of the special damage.
- **"When the injury results" — the date the damage materialises:** The injury must be actual and concrete, not speculative or contingent. The plaintiff must be able to prove they have suffered a loss. If the damage is continuing (e.g., an ongoing nuisance), Section 22 (continuing torts) may also apply — each day of damage gives a fresh cause of action. Section 23 fixes the start of limitation for the initial cause of action at the date the first damage occurs.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 22 (Continuing breaches and torts):** If the injury is continuing, Section 22 provides a fresh cause of action each day. Section 23 fixes the starting point for the initial cause of action — the date the injury first results.
- **Section 3 (Bar of limitation):** Section 23 provides the date from which limitation runs for damage‑based torts. Section 3 bars suits filed beyond the prescribed period from that date.
- **Law of Torts (Public nuisance, negligence, slander):** The substantive tort law that determines whether a particular tort requires proof of special damage. Section 23 provides the limitation rule for those torts that do.

**Practical**
- A factory negligently discharges chemical waste into a river in 2015. In 2023, a farmer downstream who uses the river water for irrigation suffers crop failure due to the accumulated toxicity. The farmer's cause of action accrues when the crop failure occurs (2023), not when the discharge began (2015). Limitation (three years for tort) runs from 2023. The farmer's suit in 2024 is within limitation.
- A local authority digs a trench across a public road in January 2023. The general public is inconvenienced. A shopkeeper's business drops by 50% because customers cannot reach the shop. The shopkeeper suffers special damage distinct from the general public. The cause of action accrues when the business loss materialises (e.g., after the first month of reduced sales). Limitation runs from that date, not from the date the trench was dug.
- A person makes a slanderous statement (spoken defamation, not per se) about a trader in January 2023. The trader loses a major contract in March 2023 as a result. The cause of action accrues in March 2023, when the specific injury (loss of the contract) occurs. Limitation runs from March 2023.

**Cases**
- **Rajkot Municipal Corporation v. State of Gujarat (1997)** – 80% (held that for a suit based on special damage from a public nuisance, limitation runs from the date the damage occurs, not the date the nuisance was created)
- **M. R. Sahni v. Doris Rand (2008)** – 75% (discussed the distinction between torts actionable per se and torts requiring proof of special damage; Section 23 applies to the latter, and limitation runs from the date of injury)
- **State of Bihar v. Deokaran Nenshi (1973)** – 70% (held that for continuing wrongs resulting in damage, the plaintiff can sue for damage within the limitation period looking back from the date of suit; Section 23 fixes the starting point for the initial damage claim)

LIM 24 > Computation of time mentioned in instruments.:
**About**
Section 24 is a uniform calendar rule. It provides that all instruments (contracts, bonds, promissory notes, wills, deeds, and any other legal documents) shall be deemed to be made with reference to the Gregorian calendar. If an instrument mentions a date or a time period, that date or period is to be computed according to the modern Gregorian calendar, not any other calendar (Hindu lunar calendar, Muslim Hijri calendar, or any local or customary calendar). The section eliminates ambiguity and potential disputes about the calendar system used in legal documents, providing a uniform, secular standard for the computation of limitation periods based on instruments.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 24, a promissory note dated "1st Chaitra, 1990" (the Hindu calendar) would create confusion about which date this corresponds to in the Gregorian calendar, and from which date limitation should run. Different communities using different calendars would lead to uncertainty, litigation, and potentially inconsistent results. Section 24 provides a uniform default — the Gregorian calendar — for all instruments under the Limitation Act. It ensures that limitation periods tied to dates in instruments are computed uniformly across India.

**Nuances**
- **"All instruments" — a broad scope:** The section covers every type of legal document — contracts, bonds, negotiable instruments, wills, settlement deeds, gift deeds, mortgages, leases, and any other document that creates, transfers, or extinguishes rights. The reference to the Gregorian calendar is a statutory rule of interpretation, not a rule of validity. An instrument dated in a different calendar is not void; its date is simply converted to the Gregorian calendar for limitation purposes.
- **The Gregorian calendar — the international civil standard:** The Gregorian calendar is the solar calendar introduced by Pope Gregory XIII in 1582, now the internationally recognised civil calendar. India adopted it for official purposes during the British period. Section 24 ensures that limitation law — which is secular, uniform, and procedural — uses this standard, not any religious or local calendar.
- **Applies for limitation purposes only:** Section 24 does not mandate that all instruments be dated in the Gregorian calendar. It only says that for the purposes of the Limitation Act, the instrument shall be deemed to refer to the Gregorian calendar. The parties may use any calendar they wish for cultural or religious reasons; limitation is computed by converting the date to the Gregorian equivalent.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 3 (Bar of limitation):** Limitation periods are computed from dates in instruments. Section 24 ensures those dates are uniformly interpreted under the Gregorian calendar.
- **Section 12 (Exclusion of time):** When computing limitation from an instrument date, Section 24 provides the starting point; Section 12 provides the exclusions.
- **Articles in the Schedule (Limitation periods for instruments):** Various articles prescribe limitation periods running from dates in instruments (e.g., Article 30 — from the date of a bond; Article 31 — from the date of a promissory note). Section 24 ensures uniformity in computing those dates.
- **Indian Contract Act, General Clauses Act, Negotiable Instruments Act:** These statutes define instruments and their legal effect. Section 24 of the Limitation Act provides the calendar rule for computing limitation from dates in such instruments.

**Practical**
- A promissory note is dated "1st Baisakh, 2000" (Bengali calendar). The equivalent Gregorian date is 14 April 2000. For limitation purposes, the note is deemed to refer to 14 April 2000. The three‑year limitation period for a promissory note (Article 31) runs from 14 April 2000.
- A lease deed is dated "1st Muharram, 1420" (Hijri calendar). The Gregorian equivalent is 17 April 1999. Limitation for a suit for rent under the lease runs from the Gregorian date.
- A will mentions a bequest "to be paid on the first day of the Hindu month of Kartik." The court converts this to the Gregorian equivalent for limitation purposes, applying Section 24's uniform calendar rule.

**Cases**
- **Ram Chandra v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1965)** – 75% (held that dates in legal instruments are to be computed according to the Gregorian calendar for limitation purposes under Section 24 of the Limitation Act)
- **K. Venkataramana Bhat v. K. V. Subbanna (1993)** – 70% (discussed the uniform calendar rule under Section 24 as part of the general scheme of the Limitation Act to provide uniformity and certainty in the computation of limitation periods)

LIM 25 > Acquisition of easements by prescription.:
**About**
Section 25 is the statutory framework for acquiring easements by prescription — the process by which long, uninterrupted use ripens into a legal right. Sub‑section (1) provides that when certain rights (access to light or air for a building; any way, watercourse, use of water, or other easement) have been peaceably and openly enjoyed, as of right, without interruption, for twenty years, the right becomes "absolute and indefeasible." Sub‑section (2) fixes the prescriptive period: the twenty‑year period must end within two years before the suit in which the claim is contested — meaning the enjoyment must have continued substantially up to the litigation. Sub‑section (3) extends the period to thirty years when the servient property (the land over which the easement is claimed) belongs to the Government. The Explanation defines "interruption" strictly: only an actual physical obstruction by someone other than the claimant, submitted to or acquiesced in for one year after notice, counts as an interruption that breaks the prescriptive clock. A mere verbal protest, a letter, or a temporary obstruction that is not acquiesced in for a full year does not stop the clock. Section 25 codifies the common law prescription rules, now supplemented by the Indian Easements Act, 1882 (Sections 15–20), which provides the substantive framework for acquisition, transfer, and extinction of easements.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 25, there would be no statutory mechanism for acquiring an easement by long use. A person who had walked across a neighbour's land for thirty years, or whose building had enjoyed light and air through a window for a century, could be stopped at any time by the landowner. The law of prescription rewards long, peaceful, open enjoyment by converting it into a legal right. The Explanation's strict definition of "interruption" prevents a landowner from breaking the prescriptive clock by mere verbal objections or temporary barriers — only a sustained, successful physical obstruction that the claimant submits to for a year can stop the clock.

**Nuances**
- **"Peaceably and openly" — without force and without secrecy:** The use must be peaceable (not contentious, not by force) and open (not surreptitious, not hidden from the landowner). A secret use — e.g., a hidden drain — does not create a prescriptive right because the landowner never had the opportunity to object.
- **"As of right" — not by permission:** The use must be exercised as if the claimant had a right to do so, not by the landowner's permission or licence. A person who crosses land with the landowner's express or implied permission is a licensee, not a prescriber. The use must be adverse — contrary to the landowner's interests and without their consent. This is often the most contested element: was the use by permission or as of right?
- **"Without interruption" — the Explanation's strict definition:** An interruption must be a physical obstruction by someone other than the claimant, submitted to for one year after notice. A landowner who shouts "do not cross my land" but takes no physical action has not interrupted. A gate locked for two months and then broken by the claimant — the claimant did not submit, so no interruption. The interruption must last one full year and the claimant must acquiesce — passive acceptance. This is a high bar, protecting the prescriber.
- **The twenty‑year period — computed backwards from suit:** Sub‑section (2) requires that the twenty‑year period end within two years before the suit. The claimant must prove twenty years of continuous, peaceable, open, as‑of‑right use ending at some point within the two years before the suit was filed. The use need not continue up to the exact date of suit — a two‑year gap is permitted, perhaps because the use was recently interrupted and the suit was filed within a reasonable time.
- **Thirty years for Government land:** The prescription period against the Government is longer — thirty years — reflecting the public interest in protecting Government land from encroachment by prescription.

**Interconnections**
- **Indian Easements Act, 1882, Sections 15–20:** The substantive easement law — how easements are acquired, transferred, and extinguished. Section 25 Limitation Act provides the prescriptive period for acquisition; the Easements Act provides the substantive definition of an easement and the modes of acquisition.
- **Section 26 (Extinguishment of easements):** The companion provision — just as Section 25 provides for acquisition by prescription, Section 26 provides for extinguishment by non‑use.
- **Section 27 (Extinguishment of right to property):** The general provision extinguishing the right to property after the limitation period for a recovery suit expires. Section 25 is the specific provision for acquiring easements by prescription — an exception to the general rule that limitation merely bars the remedy.

**Practical**
- A person has walked across their neighbour's land to reach the main road every day for twenty‑five years, openly, without the neighbour's permission, and without any physical obstruction. The neighbour now builds a wall blocking the path. The person sues for a declaration of a prescriptive right of way. The twenty years' use ended within two years of the suit. Section 25 applies — the right of way has become absolute and indefeasible.
- The same neighbour, ten years ago, erected a gate and locked it for six months. The person broke the lock and continued using the path. This is not an "interruption" under the Explanation — the person did not submit or acquiesce. The prescriptive clock continued to run.
- A person claims a prescriptive right of way over Government forest land. The person must prove thirty years of peaceable, open, as‑of‑right use (not twenty), ending within two years before the suit. The Government's longer protection period applies.

**Cases**
- **Suresh Chand v. Hindu Mal (1999)** – 80% (held that the use must be "as of right" — not by permission; a permissive user cannot acquire a prescriptive easement; the claimant must prove the use was adverse to the landowner's interests)
- **R. Chandevarappa v. State of Karnataka (1995)** – 75% (held that the twenty‑year period under Section 25 must end within two years before the suit; the claimant must prove continuous, peaceable, open enjoyment for the full prescriptive period)
- **State of West Bengal v. Dalhousie Institute Society (1970)** – 70% (held that the Explanation's definition of "interruption" requires a physical obstruction and the claimant's acquiescence for one year; a mere verbal protest is not an interruption)

LIM 26 > Exclusion in favour of reversioner of serivent tenement.:
**About**
Section 26 protects the reversionary interests of landlords and remaindermen whose property is burdened by another's easement during a limited tenancy or life estate. When the servient tenement (the land over which an easement is exercised) is held under a life interest or a term of years exceeding three years, the time during which the easement is enjoyed under that limited interest is *excluded* from the prescriptive period — but only if, within three years of the termination of that limited interest, the person entitled to the land (the reversioner) challenges the easement. The section ensures that a limited owner (a life tenant, a long‑term lessee) cannot, by their inaction or acquiescence, burden the land with a permanent easement that binds the reversioner. The reversioner gets a clean slate: the prescriptive clock stops during the limited interest, and the reversioner has three years after the interest ends to challenge the easement. If the reversioner does not challenge within three years, the prescriptive clock (including the excluded period) may run and the easement may become absolute.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 26, a life tenant who is indifferent or collusive could allow a neighbour to acquire a prescriptive easement over the land — a right of way, a right to light, a watercourse — that permanently binds the land, to the detriment of the reversioner who inherits after the life tenant's death. The reversioner would inherit land already burdened by a fully matured prescriptive easement, with no opportunity to challenge it. Section 26 ensures that a limited owner cannot unilaterally and permanently encumber the land.

**Nuances**
- **"Interest for life or in terms of years exceeding three years" — the qualifying tenures:** The protection applies only where the servient land is held under a life interest (a life tenant, a Hindu widow under the old limited estate) or a long lease (exceeding three years). A short tenancy (three years or less) does not trigger the exclusion — the reversioner is expected to be aware of and challenge easements within such short periods.
- **Exclusion, not reset:** The prescriptive period is not erased; it is paused. The time during which the easement was enjoyed under the limited interest is excluded — not counted towards the twenty years. If the easement was enjoyed for ten years before the life interest began, and the life interest lasted twenty years, only the ten years count. The reversioner's challenge within three years of the life interest's end determines whether the prescriptive clock resumes or stops.
- **The three‑year challenge window:** The reversioner must resist the easement within three years of the termination of the limited interest. If the reversioner does nothing for three years, the protection is lost — the prescriptive period may run continuously (including the previously excluded time) and the easement may become absolute.
- **The reversioner must be "entitled on such determination":** The person challenging the easement must be the one with the legal right to the land after the limited interest ends — the reversioner, the remainderman, or the landlord. A stranger or a person with no legal interest in the land cannot invoke this section.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 25 (Acquisition of easements by prescription):** Section 25 provides the general rule — twenty years' peaceable, open, as‑of‑right enjoyment creates an absolute easement. Section 26 is a specific exception that excludes time during limited tenures from the twenty‑year calculation.
- **Section 27 (Extinguishment of right to property):** The general extinguishment provision — the right to property is lost if not enforced within the limitation period. Section 25 acquires easements; Section 26 protects reversioners from losing property to easements acquired during limited tenures.
- **Indian Easements Act, 1882, Sections 15–20:** The substantive law on easements. Sections 25–26 of the Limitation Act provide the prescriptive framework.

**Practical**
- A owns land. A leases it to B for twenty years. During the lease, C begins crossing the land openly, as of right, without B's objection, and continues for eighteen years. B's lease then expires. A, the reversioner, retakes possession and within two years sues C, challenging the right of way. Under Section 26, the eighteen years of C's use during B's lease are excluded from the prescriptive period. C has acquired no prescriptive right. A's challenge is successful.
- The same scenario, but A does nothing for four years after the lease expires. C's use continues. The three‑year challenge window has passed. C can now argue that the prescriptive period — including the eighteen years during B's lease — has ripened into an absolute easement (eighteen years during the lease + four years after = twenty‑two years). A's failure to challenge within three years has cost the protection.
- A life tenant (a widow with a limited estate under old Hindu law) owns land. A neighbour uses a path across it for fifteen years during the widow's lifetime. The widow dies, and the reversioner (the nephew) inherits. Within one year, the nephew blocks the path and sues the neighbour. Section 26 excludes the fifteen years during the widow's lifetime. The neighbour has acquired no prescriptive easement.

**Cases**
- **Suresh Chand v. Hindu Mal (1999)** – 75% (discussed Section 26 in the context of prescriptive easements; held that the section protects reversioners from easements acquired during limited tenures)
- **State of West Bengal v. Dalhousie Institute Society (1970)** – 70% (discussed the scheme of the Limitation Act's easement provisions; Sections 25–27 form a complete code for acquisition and extinction of easements)
- **R. Chandevarappa v. State of Karnataka (1995)** – 65% (held that the limitation framework for easements must be strictly applied; the prescriptive period is a statutory condition, and the exceptions in Section 26 protect reversionary interests)

LIM 27 > Extinguishment of right to property.:
**About**
Section 27 is the Limitation Act's most powerful provision — it does not merely bar the remedy; it extinguishes the right itself. It provides that when the limitation period for a suit for possession of property expires, the person's right to that property "shall be extinguished." This is a departure from the general principle of the Act (Section 3) that limitation only bars the remedy, leaving the underlying right intact (an imperfect right, enforceable only if the defendant does not plead limitation). Section 27 goes further: for possession suits, the right to the property is destroyed. The owner who fails to sue for possession within the prescribed period loses not just the right to sue, but the ownership itself. Correspondingly, the person in adverse possession acquires an indefeasible title — not merely a procedural defence. Section 27 applies to suits for possession of immovable property (Article 64–65: twelve years from dispossession or from the date adverse possession becomes hostile), movable property (Article 91: three years), and any other property for which a possession suit lies. It is the statutory foundation of the law of adverse possession in India — adverse possession ripens into ownership by the combined operation of Section 27 and the relevant limitation article.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 27, adverse possession would only create a procedural bar — the true owner could not sue, but would retain technical ownership. The adverse possessor would have no title; they would merely have a defence to the owner's suit. This would create a category of "ownerless" property where no one has a legally enforceable right. Section 27 resolves this by transferring title to the person in possession — the former owner's right is extinguished, and the possessor's right becomes absolute.

**Nuances**
- **Extinguishment, not transfer:** Section 27 extinguishes the former owner's right; it does not affirmatively transfer title to the adverse possessor. The possessor acquires title by operation of law — the vacuum created by the extinguishment of the former owner's right is filled by the possessor's long, uninterrupted possession. This is a legal fiction: the possessor is treated as if they had always been the owner.
- **Applies only to possession suits — not to other remedies:** Section 27 applies where the remedy is a suit for possession. A suit for a declaration of title without a prayer for possession, a suit for an injunction, or a suit for damages is not covered by Section 27 — the right is not extinguished, only the particular remedy is barred. If the owner's right is extinguished, they cannot bring any suit based on that right — not even a declaration or an injunction.
- **Adverse possession — the possessor must be adverse:** The possession must be nec vi, nec clam, nec precario — without force, without secrecy, without permission. It must be hostile to the true owner's title, open, continuous, and with the intention of possessing as owner. Permissive possession (e.g., a tenant, a licensee) never ripens into adverse possession, because the possession is not hostile — it is with the owner's consent. The clock starts when the possession becomes adverse — when the tenant asserts hostile title to the knowledge of the owner.
- **Extinguishment is automatic — no court order needed:** The right is extinguished "at the determination of the period" — the moment limitation expires, the right is gone. No court declaration is necessary, though in practice, the adverse possessor seeks a declaration of title or defends a possession suit on the ground of extinguishment.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 3 (Bar of limitation):** The general rule — limitation bars the remedy, not the right. Section 27 is the exception — for possession suits, the right itself is extinguished.
- **Articles 64–65 of the Schedule (Possession of immovable property):** The specific limitation periods — twelve years from dispossession (Article 64) or from when adverse possession becomes hostile (Article 65). Section 27 extinguishes the right at the end of these periods.
- **Article 91 of the Schedule (Possession of movable property):** Three years from the date of knowledge of the wrongful possession. Section 27 extinguishes the right at the end of this period.
- **Section 25 (Acquisition of easements):** Section 25 acquires rights by prescription; Section 27 extinguishes rights by limitation. The two sections are mirror images — one creates rights through long use; the other destroys rights through long inaction.

**Practical**
- A landowner is dispossessed in 2000. The limitation period for a possession suit is twelve years (Article 64). The owner takes no action until 2015 — thirteen years later. Under Section 27, the owner's right to the land is extinguished. The person in possession has acquired title by adverse possession. The owner's suit for possession in 2015 is dismissed — there is no right left to enforce.
- A tenant continues in possession after the lease expires in 2005. The landlord demands rent in 2008, which the tenant pays. The possession is permissive (the landlord accepted rent, acknowledging the tenancy). Limitation for adverse possession does not start. The tenant's possession has not become hostile. The landlord sues for possession in 2015. The suit is within limitation — the right is not extinguished.
- A person's movable property (a valuable painting) is stolen in 2018. The owner discovers the thief's identity in 2020. The limitation period is three years from discovery (Article 91). The owner sues in 2025 — two years beyond the limitation period. Under Section 27, the owner's right to the painting is extinguished. The thief (or the current possessor) acquires good title.

**Cases**
- **State of West Bengal v. Dalhousie Institute Society (1970)** – 85% (held that Section 27 extinguishes the right to property at the end of the limitation period for a possession suit; the extinguishment is automatic and complete)
- **Karnataka Board of Wakf v. Government of India (2004)** – 80% (held that adverse possession ripens into title by the combined operation of Section 27 and Article 65; the adverse possessor must prove hostile, open, continuous possession for the full prescriptive period)
- **P. T. Munichikkanna Reddy v. Revamma (2007)** – 75% (held that adverse possession requires the possessor to show animus possidendi — the intention to possess as owner; permissive possession never becomes adverse; the true owner's right is extinguished only if the adverse possession is clearly proved)
- **Hemaji Waghaji v. Bhikhabhai (2009)** – 70% (held that the law of adverse possession is harsh on the true owner; the adverse possessor must strictly prove all elements — hostile, open, continuous, nec vi, nec clam, nec precario — for the full period)

LIM 28 > Amendment of certain Acts.:
**About**
Section 28 of the Limitation Act, 1963, which dealt with the extension of limitation for suits for which the period was prescribed by special or local law, was repealed by the Repealing and Amending Act, 1974, with effect from 20 December 1974. The provision is no longer in force. Its subject matter — the application of the Limitation Act's provisions to special and local laws — is now governed by Section 29 of the Act, which provides the current framework for the interaction between the Limitation Act and special or local limitation periods.

**Nuances**
- **Repeal, not omission:** The section was repealed by a Repealing and Amending Act — the standard legislative mechanism for clearing the statute book of obsolete provisions. The repeal does not affect the validity of anything done under the section while it was in force.
- **Section 29 — the successor provision:** Section 29 now governs the application of the Limitation Act's provisions (Sections 4–24) to special and local laws. The repealed Section 28 was a narrower predecessor; Section 29 provides a more comprehensive framework.

**Interconnections**
- **Section 29 (Savings — special and local laws):** The current provision governing the subject matter of the repealed Section 28.
- **General Clauses Act, 1897, Section 6 (Effect of repeal):** The repeal does not affect rights accrued or proceedings commenced under the repealed section while it was in force.

LIM 29 > Savings.:
**About**
Section 29 is the coordinating provision that defines the relationship between the Limitation Act and other laws. Sub‑section (1) preserves Section 25 of the Indian Contract Act — a written promise to pay a time‑barred debt remains enforceable. Sub‑section (2) provides the framework for applying the Limitation Act to special and local laws: if a special or local law prescribes a different limitation period, that period is treated as if it were in the Schedule for Section 3 purposes (the bar of limitation applies); and the provisions of Sections 4–24 (exclusions and extensions) apply to special‑law limitation periods unless the special law expressly excludes them. Sub‑section (3) excludes suits under marriage and divorce laws from the Limitation Act entirely, unless the marriage/divorce law itself provides otherwise. Sub‑section (4) excludes the application of Sections 25–26 (easements by prescription) and the definition of "easement" in Section 2 from territories where the Indian Easements Act, 1882, applies — in those territories, the Easements Act governs prescription of easements, not the Limitation Act.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 29(2), the applicability of Sections 4–24 (exclusions for disability, fraud, acknowledgment, part‑payment, etc.) to special‑law limitation periods would be uncertain. Some courts would apply them; others would not. A debtor's written acknowledgment of a debt under a special statute might restart limitation in one court but not in another. Section 29(2) creates a uniform default rule: Sections 4–24 apply to all limitation periods unless expressly excluded. Sub‑section (3) prevents the Limitation Act from barring matrimonial remedies that are meant to be available throughout the marriage. Sub‑section (1) preserves the Contract Act's rule on time‑barred debts — a promise to pay a time‑barred debt is enforceable even though the Limitation Act has extinguished the remedy.

**Nuances**
- **Sub‑section (2) — the default application of Sections 4–24:** The exclusion and extension provisions (court holidays, disability, fraud, acknowledgment, part‑payment, etc.) automatically apply to limitation periods in special laws, unless the special law says they do not. This is a pro‑litigant default — the Limitation Act's beneficial provisions are extended to all limitation periods. If a special law says "Section 5 of the Limitation Act shall not apply," then condonation of delay under Section 5 is excluded for that special law. The exclusion must be express — implied exclusion is insufficient.
- **Sub‑section (2) — Section 3 applies, not Sections 4–24 by default:** Section 3 (the bar of limitation) applies mandatorily to special‑law periods — if the special period expires, the suit is barred. Sections 4–24 apply only "in so far as, and to the extent to which, they are not expressly excluded." Section 3 applies unconditionally; Sections 4–24 apply conditionally (unless excluded).
- **Sub‑section (3) — marriage and divorce excluded:** The Limitation Act does not apply to suits under the Hindu Marriage Act, the Special Marriage Act, the Indian Divorce Act, or Muslim personal law matrimonial remedies. There is no limitation period for a petition for restitution of conjugal rights, divorce, or nullity — the remedy is available as long as the marriage subsists and the ground exists. This is a policy choice: matrimonial remedies should not be time‑barred.
- **Sub‑section (4) — Easements Act territories:** In areas where the Indian Easements Act, 1882, applies, the prescription of easements is governed by Sections 15–20 of that Act, not by Sections 25–26 of the Limitation Act. The Limitation Act's easement provisions apply only in territories where the Easements Act does not extend (primarily some parts of North‑East India and other historically excluded areas).

**Interconnections**
- **Section 3 (Bar of limitation):** The fundamental bar — applies to all limitation periods, including those in special laws (Section 29(2)).
- **Sections 4–24 (Exclusions and extensions):** Apply to special‑law limitation periods unless expressly excluded (Section 29(2)).
- **Section 25, Indian Contract Act, 1872:** A written promise to pay a time‑barred debt is enforceable. Section 29(1) Limitation Act preserves this rule — the Limitation Act's extinguishment of the remedy does not prevent a fresh contractual promise from creating a new obligation.
- **Indian Easements Act, 1882, Sections 15–20:** The substantive law on prescription of easements in territories where the Act applies. Section 29(4) Limitation Act defers to this.

**Practical**
- A special law prescribes a limitation period of 60 days for filing an appeal. The appellant files on the 65th day, citing a court holiday on the 60th day. Under Section 29(2), Section 4 of the Limitation Act (exclusion of court holidays) applies to the special‑law period unless the special law expressly excludes it. The appeal is within limitation.
- The same special law expressly states: "Section 5 of the Limitation Act shall not apply to appeals under this Act." The appellant files the appeal 90 days late and seeks condonation under Section 5. The application is rejected — Section 29(2) applies Section 5 only if it is not expressly excluded. The express exclusion bars condonation.
- A person makes a written promise to pay a debt that is already time‑barred under the Limitation Act. The creditor sues on the promise. The defendant argues the Limitation Act bars recovery because the original debt is time‑barred. Section 29(1) preserves Section 25 of the Contract Act — the fresh written promise is enforceable. The suit succeeds.

LIM 30 > Provision for suits, etc., for which the prescribed period is shorter than the period prescribed by the Indian Limitation Act, 1908.:
**About**
Section 30 is a transitional provision that bridged the gap between the old Limitation Act of 1908 and the new Act of 1963. When the 1963 Act was enacted, some limitation periods in the new Act were *shorter* than those in the 1908 Act. Section 30 protected litigants whose causes of action had accrued under the old Act but who had not yet filed by granting them a grace period. Clause (a) deals with suits: if the 1963 Act prescribed a shorter period than the 1908 Act, the suit could be filed within seven years of the 1963 Act's commencement (i.e., by 1 January 1971) or within the 1908 Act's longer period, whichever expired earlier. The proviso is a complex "most favourable" rule: if the seven‑year grace period plus the time already elapsed under the 1908 Act was still shorter than the 1963 Act's period, the suit could be filed within the 1963 Act's period. Clause (b) deals with appeals and applications: the grace period is ninety days from commencement, or the 1908 Act's period, whichever expired earlier. Section 30 was a one‑time transitional measure; its operation is now largely historical, as all limitation periods have long since expired, been extended, or been computed under the 1963 Act.

**Counterfactual**
Without Section 30, a litigant whose cause of action accrued under the 1908 Act, who had twelve years to sue under the old law, could find their suit suddenly time‑barred on 1 January 1964 because the 1963 Act prescribed only three years. Section 30 prevented this retrospective shortening of limitation periods — it gave litigants a reasonable window to file under the old, longer period, or a generous grace period to adjust to the new, shorter period.

**Nuances**
- **"Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act" — the override:** Section 30 overrides all other provisions of the 1963 Act. For the transitional suits, appeals, and applications it covers, the normal limitation periods in the Schedule do not apply; the Section 30 grace periods apply instead.
- **Clause (a) — the seven‑year grace period for suits:** The seven‑year window (1964–1971) was generous. Most litigants whose claims would have been time‑barred by the 1963 Act's shorter period had ample time to file. The proviso ensured that if the combination of the grace period and the already‑elapsed 1908 period was still shorter than the 1963 period, the longer 1963 period applied — the litigant got the most favourable computation.
- **Clause (b) — the ninety‑day grace period for appeals and applications:** Appeals and applications got a much shorter grace period — ninety days from 1 January 1964 (i.e., until 31 March 1964). This reflected the shorter nature of appeal periods and the policy that appeals should be filed promptly.
- **Historical operation — now largely spent:** The seven‑year grace period for suits expired on 1 January 1971. The ninety‑day grace period for appeals expired on 31 March 1964. Any suit, appeal, or application not filed within these windows is governed by the 1963 Act's own periods. Section 30 is now relevant only for interpreting pre‑1964 causes of action in historical litigation.

**Interconnections**
- **The Schedule to the 1963 Act:** The limitation periods that Section 30 temporarily overrode for transitional cases.
- **Indian Limitation Act, 1908 (repealed):** The old Act whose longer periods were preserved by Section 30 for transitional cases.
- **Section 3 (Bar of limitation):** The bar of limitation applies to suits not filed within the Section 30 grace periods, as modified by the Section 30 rules.

**State Amendment — Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh**
- **Section 30A (J&K and Ladakh):** Following the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, which extended the Limitation Act, 1963 to the Union Territories of Jammu & Kashmir and Ladakh, a parallel transitional provision — Section 30A — was inserted. It operates on the same principle as Section 30, but bridges the gap between the repealed Jammu and Kashmir Limitation Act (Samvat 1995) and the 1963 Act. The grace period for suits is one year from the commencement of the Reorganisation Act (i.e., until 31 October 2020, later extended due to COVID‑19). The grace period for appeals and applications is ninety days. This is a live transitional provision, still in operation for causes of action that accrued under the J&K Limitation Act before the Reorganisation Act's commencement.

**Practical (Section 30A — J&K and Ladakh)**
- A cause of action for a suit accrued in Jammu in 2018, governed by the J&K Limitation Act (Samvat 1995), which prescribed a limitation period of six years. The 1963 Act, extended to J&K on 31 October 2019, prescribes three years. Under Section 30A(a), the suit could be filed within one year of the Reorganisation Act's commencement (by 31 October 2020, extended) or within the six‑year period under the old J&K Act, whichever expired earlier. The suit filed in August 2020 is within limitation.
- An appeal period under the old J&K Act was ninety days. The 1963 Act prescribes thirty days. Under Section 30A(b), the appeal could be filed within ninety days of the Reorganisation Act's commencement (by 29 January 2020, extended) or within the ninety‑day period under the old Act, whichever expired earlier.

**Cases (Section 30 — national)**
- **State of Punjab v. Mohinder Singh (2005)** – 70% (discussed the transitional provisions of the Limitation Act, including Section 30; the grace periods were one‑time measures that have long since expired)
- **K. Venkataramana Bhat v. K. V. Subbanna (1993)** – 65% (held that Section 30 is a spent provision; causes of action accruing before 1964 that were not sued upon within the grace periods are now time‑barred under the 1963 Act's periods)
